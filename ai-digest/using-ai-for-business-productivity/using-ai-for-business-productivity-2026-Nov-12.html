1. OpenAI's quarterly revenue reportedly tops $10 billion, now split evenly between business and consumer

Bloomberg reported on 11 November that OpenAI booked about $10 billion in revenue for July–September 2026, the first time a quarter has crossed that line. That is roughly 50% more than the prior quarter's ~$6.7 billion and nearly three times the same period last year. Investors on a 10 November call were told annualized revenue reached about $50 billion by the end of September. CFO Sarah Friar reportedly said revenue is now split roughly evenly between enterprise and consumer. The cost side has not changed: the Wall Street Journal reports the operating loss widened to about $10.9 billion from $10.1 billion. For business buyers, this confirms that workplace use of ChatGPT and its API is now half of OpenAI's business. It also shows the price of that growth has not come down. The figures come from unnamed sources and documents, not official filings.

Sources: TechCrunch, Techzine, Indian Television, Rolling Out

2. Reuters: Australian blue chips accused of "AI washing" job cuts that are really offshoring

A Reuters report published 12 November says large Australian companies are increasingly citing AI when they cut jobs and move work offshore. Unions and academics argue that many roles are going to lower-paid offshore staff, not to software. Examples include Qantas cutting 850 jobs and outsourcing some corporate functions in an AI-driven overhaul with Accenture, Telstra cutting jobs from its AI joint venture with Accenture with some work moved to India, and WiseTech and Atlassian attributing their cuts to AI. University of Sydney professor Uri Gal says calling a cost cut an "AI transformation" can make it look innovative rather than unpopular. The Reserve Bank of Australia says AI has not yet caused widespread job losses, though job ads in AI-exposed occupations are weakening. For leaders, the takeaway is that AI claims in workforce decisions are now being scrutinized politically, so productivity stories need evidence behind them.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com, Reuters via SRN News, Finimize, Devdiscourse

3. PwC will certify 85,000+ US staff in critical thinking so they check AI output rather than just approve it

On 9 November PwC US announced a Critical Thinking and Judgment certificate for more than 85,000 partners and employees, saying AI is taking over foundational tasks and making human judgment the core of quality work. The program starts with a diagnostic of each person's thinking habits, then teaches PwC's own framework through realistic cases. One example has a staffer review an AI-generated assessment of a company's internal controls. Bloomberg Tax puts the firm's investment at more than $100 million. Reports say rollout begins in December, with completion targeted for June 2027, and university partners get the framework from early 2027. The announcement comes from a firm that previously trained the same workforce to adopt AI tools. It suggests large employers now see reviewing AI output as a skill that needs formal training.

Sources: PwC newsroom, Inc., Big4 News, Creative Brands Mag

4. Gartner: 71% of firms plan agentic AI within a year, but only 21% can govern it

Speaking at Gartner IT Symposium/Xpo in Barcelona on 9 November, the firm said that balancing AI value, risk and safety is now the top challenge for CIOs. A Q2 2026 survey of more than 2,000 CIOs and technology executives found that 71% of organizations expect to deploy agentic AI within 12 months, but only 21% have agent governance capabilities in place. Gartner warns that in many organizations AI risk is growing faster than AI value. It recommends an AI governance platform that acts as a single system of record for safety and oversight, rather than separate controls per tool. InformationWeek reported the same figures at the Orlando edition in October, so the numbers are being repeated across events rather than newly measured. The 50-point gap is still one of the clearest measures of how far agent rollouts are ahead of their controls.

Sources: Gartner newsroom, CDO Magazine, InformationWeek

5. Gartner: only 21% of service leaders have cut human agents because of AI, and just 4% hit expected ROI

This item is from last week. At its Customer Service & Support conference on 5 November, Gartner released a survey of 275 service and support leaders, conducted in July–August 2026. Only 21% said their organizations had reduced human agent headcount because of AI. A separate conference release said just 4% of customer service leaders have achieved the ROI they expected from AI initiatives. Customer service is the function most often cited as ripe for automation, so the gap between vendor promises and measured results matters for anyone building a business case. The data suggests most service organizations are using AI to absorb volume and assist agents, not to replace them. It also suggests the financial payoff has been slower than planned.

Sources: Gartner newsroom (headcount survey), Gartner newsroom (Day 2 highlights)
