1. UK's FCA warns tech firms that AI agents doing regulated financial work could bring them under financial regulation

On 24 November the UK Financial Conduct Authority published a page setting out its approach to agentic AI. It is aimed at regulated firms, technology providers and social media platforms. The core message is that an AI agent that carries out regulated activities, such as payment services, financial promotions, credit broking or financial advice, can bring its provider inside the regulatory perimeter. Describing yourself as a tech company does not change that, and the FCA says it will act against anyone offering regulated services without authorisation. For regulated firms, the FCA asks them to think through consents and permissions, liability for acting on an agent's instructions, how customers get redress when something goes wrong, and whether to accept payments instructed by an AI agent or an AI-controlled wallet. Commentary from the Cloud Security Alliance and Freshfields stresses that senior managers stay accountable for third-party agents their firm relies on. Coverage says the FCA is not writing new rules and that existing rules already apply. The statement came as Meta prepares a UK launch of its Muse agent, though the FCA named no firm. For any business building agents that touch money, this is the clearest signal yet from a major regulator that automating a regulated task does not move it outside regulation.

Sources: PYMNTS, AI Affairs, Freshfields, Cloud Security Alliance research note, Sulat AI & Tech News, The Epoch Times

2. OpenAI starts testing Private Inference, which aims to keep business prompts hidden from OpenAI while models run

Sam Altman said on 25 November that OpenAI has begun limited trials of Private Inference with selected business customers. It is part of OpenAI Private Intelligence, an umbrella announced at DevDay in September, alongside Zero Data Retention with Private Safety Processing. Private Inference uses confidential computing, meaning hardware-isolated enclaves with verifiable controls, so that prompts and responses are protected even while the model is processing them, not just in transit or at rest. Analysts point out that this is a different promise from Zero Data Retention. Zero Data Retention covers what gets stored afterwards, while Private Inference covers who can look during processing. OpenAI has not disclosed the test cohort, pricing or a general-availability date. It says it is lining up confidential-compute capacity for a much larger rollout. One analysis argues that OpenAI's white paper describes narrower protections than the marketing suggests, and enclave designs generally do not defend against physical attacks. For regulated or IP-sensitive companies, this goes straight at a main reason they have kept sensitive workloads off hosted models. Buyers should still read the technical terms before routing anything confidential through it.

Sources: Unite.AI, Let's Data Science, i10x, ROIC.ai, VentureBeat (DevDay background)

3. OpenAI begins merging ChatGPT's Chat and Work into one experience, with one usage allowance shared with Codex

Starting on 24 November, OpenAI began rolling out a combined Chat and Work experience in ChatGPT to a small group of Pro subscribers, according to product lead Thibault Sottiaux. Chat and Work will draw on a single usage meter. An OpenAI spokesperson said conversations with the Instant model stay unlimited and don't count toward it. A wider rollout is planned for December, and reports say Plus, Pro and Business plans will share one token allowance, with Codex drawing from the same pool. OpenAI says it wants a simpler product by January 2027. It has not said how big the combined allowance is. It expects most users won't hit resets more often, but concedes the heaviest users may reach caps sooner. Prices are not changing. Heavy users have pushed back, and some are petitioning to keep the two modes separate. For businesses whose staff use ChatGPT for both conversation and delegated agent work, this turns capacity planning into a single budget. That makes it easier to see but also easier to exhaust, so admins should watch December's plan terms closely.

Sources: The AI Insider, Decrypt, Crypto Briefing, MLQ.ai, AI Lookout

4. Anthropic used Claude agents to move its Messages API from Python to Rust, and found that checking the work took longer than writing it

On 25 November Anthropic published an engineering account of how it used Claude agents to rewrite its Messages API, the service that every Claude app and API customer goes through, from Python into Rust. Anthropic says it did this without taking the service offline or pausing feature work. It reports roughly 70% lower CPU use per request, measured in one region. That is higher than the 40–50% an Anthropic engineering lead gave on a podcast in October, and it is Anthropic's own number. The most useful detail for other companies is where the time went. Coverage says agents produced a first version of the new request path in about a week. Proving it behaved the same as the old system took months, using the original Python service as the reference for every check. That matches what this digest keeps finding in AI-assisted software work: writing code is getting cheap, and review and verification are now the slow part. For engineering leaders weighing big legacy migrations, the lesson is to budget for checking and testing, not for writing the code.

Sources: TechRepublic, The Next Web, The Neuron, The420, Digg discussion

5. Law firm Crosby insures its AI paralegal agents for $5M and stops having lawyers review about 20% of its work

Crosby, an AI-native law firm in New York, has taken out a $5 million liability policy that covers the outputs of its AI paralegal agents. The policy is provided by Testudo and underwritten through Lloyd's of London. Crosby believes it is the first law firm to insure what its agents produce. The policy took effect on 3 November and is separate from Crosby's existing professional liability cover, which still covers lawyers and the tools they supervise. With it in place, Crosby stopped having lawyers review tasks that don't legally require a licensed attorney. It puts those at about 20% of the work its lawyers used to do. Bloomberg Law commentary and other coverage note that insurance doesn't settle whether agents can lawfully give legal advice, and bar associations have not set standards for insuring agent work. This is a fallback item, first reported earlier this month, and it isn't yet in the knowledge base. Together with the FCA item above, it shows the same shift: companies are starting to treat agent output as a liability they can price and insure, and that lets them take human review out of the loop.
