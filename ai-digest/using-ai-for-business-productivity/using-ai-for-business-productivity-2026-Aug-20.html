1. Salesforce turns its whole platform into an API for AI agents with Headless 360 expansion

Salesforce significantly expanded Headless 360 on August 19, extending the "no browser required" architecture it introduced at TDX so that essentially every Salesforce capability — data, workflows, business logic — is reachable by an authorized AI agent as an API, MCP tool or CLI command instead of a UI click. The expansion adds a new Data 360 MCP Server exposing roughly 200 APIs so agents can analyze, structure and activate governed customer data without a human walking through each tool by hand, plus a wave of reusable prebuilt "Skills," new developer tooling, and a Slackbot MCP Client now wired into 20-plus partner apps including Docusign, Notion and Zoom. The pitch to enterprise customers is that they can keep the workflows, permissions and governance they've already built on Salesforce and simply let agents operate them directly, rather than rebuilding integrations from scratch. It's a concrete example of an enterprise software incumbent racing to become the substrate agents act through, not just another app agents have to click around in.

Sources: SiliconANGLE, CX Today, Salesforce Newsroom

2. Walmart says its Sparky AI agent drives 35% bigger baskets as it builds three more "super agents"

Walmart's Q2 FY2026 results, released this morning (Aug 20), showed revenue of roughly $177.4B (up ~5.6% in constant currency) and 25% e-commerce growth, with full-year guidance raised even as adjusted EPS of 68 cents came in a touch under Wall Street's estimate. On the call, executives leaned hard on AI as a growth driver: customers who arrive via Sparky, Walmart's in-app AI shopping agent, spend about 35% more per order than customers who use keyword search, and the company is now extending the same "agentic" approach beyond shopping — building a "super agent" for associates that pulls scheduling and sales data into one place, and another for suppliers, sellers and advertisers to manage onboarding, orders and ad campaigns. For a retailer of Walmart's scale, tying a specific, quantified productivity/revenue lift directly to an AI agent on an earnings call is a notable data point in the broader debate over whether enterprise AI spend is actually paying off yet.

Sources: CIO Dive, Yahoo Finance, Investing.com

3. Lowe's says associate-facing AI is paying off too, not just its customer chatbot

Lowe's Q2 2026 results (Aug 19): sales of $26B, up 8.3% year-over-year, adjusted diluted EPS of $4.40 (+1.6%), and digital comparable sales up 15.7% for a second straight quarter above 15%. The company credits two AI tools for the digital and in-store strength: Mylow, its customer-facing shopping agent, converts customers at roughly 3x the rate of non-users, while Mylow Companion — built for store associates — has now fielded more than 5 million questions since launch, helping employees answer product questions faster and spend more time with customers instead of hunting for information across disconnected systems. Lowe's is targeting roughly $1 billion in productivity savings for 2026, explicitly tying part of that to AI-tool adoption in stores — a useful counter-example to the more common story of AI showing up only in customer-facing chat, not backroom labor productivity.

Sources: Lowe's Newsroom, Retail TouchPoints, StockTitan, Benzinga

4. An insurance brokerage says 95% of its staff now use AI daily — and calls it "enterprise-wide adoption"

IMA Financial Group, a North American insurance brokerage with more than 3,000 employees, announced on Aug 18 that it has moved past pilots into what it calls enterprise-scale AI adoption: over 95% of associates now use AI daily across thousands of agentic workflows, spanning research, analytics, document comparison and workflow automation. The company built an internal "AI Studio" that lets employees turn their own use cases and small pilots into tools rolled out firm-wide, paired with role-specific training and what it describes as a platform-agnostic AI strategy rather than a single-vendor mandate. It's a rare instance of a mid-sized, non-tech company putting a hard adoption number on AI usage rather than just spend or pilot counts — a useful benchmark given how often surveys elsewhere in this digest find adoption stuck at the "some employees, some of the time" stage.

Sources: GlobeNewswire, Coverager, The Manila Times

5. Ryanair hands Google Cloud a five-year deal to run crew logistics and flight ops through Gemini

Ryanair and Google Cloud signed a five-year data and AI partnership (announced Aug 12) that rolls out Google Workspace and Gemini Enterprise, Google's agentic AI platform, to Ryanair's roughly 35,000 employees. The airline plans to use it to automate decision-making, streamline flight-crew logistics and scheduling, and support fleet and maintenance planning, alongside a broader push to improve corporate productivity as it targets growth to 300 million passengers a year by 2034. The deal also gives Ryanair a second major cloud provider alongside its existing infrastructure, a resilience move as much as an AI one.
