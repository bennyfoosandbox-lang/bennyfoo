1. Meta's AI capex crushes free cash flow — and Zuckerberg raises the spending floor anyway

Meta's Q2 2026 earnings showed how costly the enterprise AI buildout has become: capital expenditure hit $31.1B, up 83% year-over-year, and free cash flow collapsed to $784M from $8.55B a year earlier as the AI infrastructure buildout consumed nearly all of the quarter's operating cash. Rather than pull back, Meta raised the floor of its 2026 capex guidance by $5B to a $130B–$145B range and issued roughly $25B in new long-term debt to keep funding data centers. Zuckerberg framed the spending as productivity-driving infrastructure, saying AI is "accelerating our core business today... and opening the door to entirely new enterprise opportunities," betting that AI-driven efficiency and new agent products eventually offset the cash burn. The results crystallize a theme running through recent enterprise-AI coverage: the productivity story increasingly has to be weighed against the balance-sheet cost of building it.

Sources: CNBC, Fortune, Tech Times, Reuters

2. New Harness report: roughly a quarter of enterprise AI spend is wasted, and nobody owns the bill

Harness's 2026 State of AI in FinOps report, based on a survey of 700 engineering and FinOps leaders across five countries, finds enterprise AI spend has outgrown the governance built to manage it. Roughly one dollar in four spent on AI is estimated to go to waste, more than half of organizations have no single owner for AI costs, and only one in five can trace the source of an unexpected cost spike within hours. The findings sharpen a running thread in this week's enterprise-AI coverage — alongside Snowflake's new Cortex AI Gateway and McKinsey's cost-overrun research — that the productivity story is increasingly gated by cost visibility rather than model capability. Harness is positioning its own AI ROI tooling as the fix, tying spend directly to software-delivery outcomes rather than raw token counts.

Sources: PR Newswire, CIO Dive, ADVFN

3. The Conference Board: companies are training workers for today's AI job, not tomorrow's

A new Conference Board report, drawing on interviews with 35 enterprise leaders and a global survey of nearly 1,300 workers, finds that while 55% of employees already use AI weekly, only a third have received employer-provided AI training in the past six months. Just 48% of workers say their employer gives them enough time during work hours to build AI skills, and under half feel they have the tools and resources to do so. The report's core warning is that most companies are optimizing training for the AI tools employees use in their current role today rather than the reskilling that AI-driven job redesign will demand — a gap that risks concentrating productivity gains among a small, self-trained group instead of spreading them org-wide.

Sources: The Conference Board, PR Newswire, FairPlay Talks

4. ZipRecruiter: employers using AI are hiring more, not less — but the bar is rising

ZipRecruiter's "More Jobs, Higher Bar: The 2026 AI Employer Report," surveying more than 1,000 U.S. employers, complicates the AI-layoffs narrative dominating recent headlines (Visa, Monday.com, ServiceNow): 92% of employers report some level of AI adoption, and rather than shrinking headcount, 35% say AI will increase their total staffing while another 33% expect it to reshuffle roles rather than cut them outright. Fifty-seven percent of employers say they've already raised their baseline productivity expectations of employees because of AI, meaning the same job increasingly demands more output. Set against this week's layoff wave, the data suggests AI's labor-market effect is bifurcating — accelerating cuts at large, AI-native tech and finance firms while acting as a growth and hiring driver for many small and mid-sized employers.

Sources: MarketScreener, AOL, TechRSeries

5. ServiceNow's flat-headcount plan: let AI absorb attrition instead of backfilling

ServiceNow has cut several hundred roles in a global restructuring, part of CEO Bill McDermott's plan to end 2026 with roughly the same total headcount the company started with — not through a single mass-layoff round, but by using AI-driven productivity gains to avoid backfilling normal attrition while integrating recent acquisitions (Armis, Veza). McDermott has framed this explicitly as a cost-discipline strategy: "as you have attrition in the company, you don't have to backfill it," while still aiming to expand free-cash-flow margin. It's a distinct model from Visa's or Monday.com's headline percentage cuts — a slower-burn version of the same bet, where AI productivity shrinks the org organically rather than through one announced round, and a playbook worth watching as an alternative to the blunter layoff-and-cite-AI approach spreading across tech and finance.

Sources: Dealroom, People Matters, WhatJobs, CTech (Calcalist)
