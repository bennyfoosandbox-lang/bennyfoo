1. Workday's AI products bring in about $150 million of new contract value in a quarter, triple last year, but the stock falls on a softer outlook

Workday reported fiscal Q3 2027 results (quarter to 31 October) after the close on 24 November. Revenue was $2.705 billion, up 11.2%, and subscription revenue was $2.532 billion, up 12.8%. The AI numbers were the headline: AI products brought in roughly $150 million of new annual contract value in the quarter, more than three times the figure a year earlier. Annual recurring revenue from AI products passed $750 million, up more than 130% year on year and about 25% on the previous quarter, and more than 2,200 core customers have now bought at least one AI product. Workday raised its full-year subscription revenue guide to $9.958 billion and its operating margin guide to 31.5%, but its roughly 10% subscription growth guide for Q4 sent shares down more than 7% after hours. The pattern matches what ServiceNow, Salesforce and Zoom have shown this season: HR and finance buyers are paying for agents, but AI is not yet speeding up the core business enough to satisfy investors.

Sources: PR Newswire (Workday release), StockTitan, Investing.com (slides), Benzinga (call transcript), 24/7 Wall St.

2. Update: Sierra raises $1.75 billion at a $30 billion valuation as its customer-service agents pass $400 million in annual revenue

Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor's Sierra announced a $1.75 billion Series F on 23 November at a $30 billion valuation, led by Greenoaks and Lightspeed Venture Partners, with earlier leads Benchmark, Sequoia, GV and Tiger Global all investing again. That roughly doubles the valuation from its $950 million round in May, when we last logged the company at about $200 million in annual recurring revenue. Sierra says ARR passed $400 million in October, after adding more than $150 million in the third quarter alone, so revenue doubled in about six months. The price works out at roughly 75 times ARR, and Sierra didn't say whether the $30 billion is pre- or post-money. Customer service is the business function where enterprises are replacing work with AI agents fastest, and this round shows investors expect that to keep scaling.

Sources: Sierra blog, The Next Web, Dealroom, MLQ.ai, Bret Taylor on X

3. Anthropic's Claude Ship reviews, fixes and, under a team's own policy, approves pull requests

Anthropic launched Claude Ship on 23 November, a set of three agents for GitHub pull requests on Claude Team and Enterprise plans. Code Review, now generally available, checks its own findings and posts inline comments ranked by severity, but doesn't approve or block anything. PR Steward (beta) pushes fixes for failing checks, merge conflicts and reviewer feedback without approving them. Claude Approvals (beta) can approve routine changes, but only under a policy file the team writes, and it gates merges only if an organisation makes it a required GitHub check. Anthropic recommends starting it in observe-only mode. A /ship command in Claude Code opens the PR and then watches it for up to seven days. Coding agents now write far more code than humans can review, and review has become the bottleneck. Claude Ship moves AI into that step, with explicit policy controls, and Anthropic warns that the agents can still miss bugs and security holes.

Sources: MarkTechPost, Technology.org, Mad Robot, Tbreak, Claude Code docs — Code Review

4. OpenAI's OpenWorks pays local experts up to $8,000 a month to coach small businesses on AI for free

On 23 November OpenAI launched OpenWorks, a pilot in about 20 US cities that pays local AI experts to give small businesses free, hands-on help. These "OpenWorks Partners" are contractors who commit at least four days a month to working with local businesses, preferably in person, and run a monthly workshop. Coverage puts stipends at up to $8,000 a month, though OpenAI's help centre says no fixed amount is guaranteed. Participating businesses pick their own projects and get three months of ChatGPT Business. The pilot is limited to independently owned firms and excludes VC-backed startups, PE-owned companies and software firms. OpenAI hasn't disclosed the budget or how it chose the cities. The programme targets small firms' biggest barrier to AI, which is having someone who can set it up for them rather than access to the tools. It also pulls OpenAI into the local IT-consultant market that Microsoft partners have long served.

Sources: OpenAI Help Center, Yahoo Finance, MLQ.ai, Data Studios, Crypto Briefing

5. Burning Glass and RAISE US find AI-heavy industries are cutting entry-level hiring rather than laying people off

The report "Fewer Rungs, New Bridges" from RAISE US and the Burning Glass Institute, first reported by Semafor and Axios on 23 November, finds that AI's effect on jobs is showing up as less hiring rather than layoffs. In industries that use AI heavily, entry-level roles' share of job postings in the year to June 2026 was 3–8% below their 2021–26 average, while the share across the wider economy barely moved. White-collar industries have about 1.5 million fewer jobs than pre-pandemic trends would predict. That is a net figure, and the authors list AI as one of several causes, along with offshoring and other technology. Workers without a bachelor's degree hold 80% of the jobs the report rates as likely to be automated. "The missing workers aren't being laid off. They're not being hired in the first place," the authors write. For employers, the risk is that cutting junior roles to bank AI productivity gains leaves no pipeline of future managers and specialists.

Sources: Semafor, Axios, Digital Trends, Community College Daily
