1. Microsoft officially unveils its Copilot "super app," pitching Home, Code and Autopilot at business users

Microsoft on September 25 unveiled the finished shape of the Copilot "super app" it began merging into one experience back in August, converging the previously separate consumer and Microsoft 365 Copilot apps into a single tabbed product built around three modes: Home (chat plus the Cowork agent), Code (describe-and-build tools for non-developers), and Autopilot (the always-on agent formerly called Scout, which users can name, assign a role and goal, and let run unattended for days). Full versions of Word, Excel and PowerPoint now live inside Copilot itself so people can create and edit office documents without switching apps, and CEO Satya Nadella framed the bundle as "a new OS for work," explicitly aimed at business customers rather than consumers. The rollout is staged: Code is already in Microsoft's Frontier early-access program and expands more broadly within weeks, Autopilot enters private preview at the end of September, and a Microsoft 365 Premium/Pro preview follows later this year. This is a materially new step beyond the "merge begins" news this digest covered on Aug 11 and Aug 14 — Microsoft is now showing the finished product and committing to dates, at a moment it's fighting Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's ChatGPT Work for the same workplace-assistant budget.

Sources: Fortune, GeekWire, Gizmodo, PCWorld, PYMNTS

2. Adobe brings Acrobat's PDF tools into Claude and pushes Creative Cloud into Gemini

Adobe announced on September 24 that Acrobat has joined its "Adobe for Claude" plugin for the first time, giving Claude users direct access to PDF tools — organizing, splitting, merging and redacting documents, plus a new interactive editor for reordering pages, editing text and marking up files with comments — alongside the more than 80 existing Acrobat, Express, Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Lightroom, InDesign and Adobe Stock tools already wired into the plugin. The same announcement extended Adobe's assistant strategy in the other direction, bringing its creative tools into Google's Gemini for the first time, a sign Adobe is now betting on distributing its tools inside whichever chatbot a customer already lives in rather than pulling people back into Creative Cloud itself. The rollout is global and immediate across Claude and Claude Code on desktop, mobile and web, with existing Adobe-for-Claude connector users auto-upgraded to the new plugin. For business users it turns document and design busywork — redacting a contract, reformatting a deck, batch-editing a PDF — into something you can hand to a chat assistant instead of opening several separate Adobe apps.

Sources: Adobe Blog, Digital Trends, Computerworld, channelnews

3. OpenAI turns ChatGPT Voice into a business tool: plugins, model choice and spoken-to-document creation

OpenAI shipped three ChatGPT Voice upgrades starting September 23: plugin support so Voice can read and act on a user's email, calendar and Slack; the ability to choose which GPT-6 model powers a conversation (Astra, Sol or Luna, rather than voice being tied to a single fixed backend); and integration with ChatGPT Work, OpenAI's cloud-connected agent system, so Plus and Pro users can speak a request and get back a finished doc, slide deck, site or spreadsheet instead of typing it out. Free and Go-tier users get the plugin and connected-app support without the Work integration, and the changes rolled out globally the same day across web and mobile. The update is a direct pitch at business users who want to dictate work instead of typing it — summarizing a Slack channel, drafting an email, building a slide deck by voice — and lands in the same "voice as a work interface" territory Google and Microsoft have each staked out this year, intensifying competition for the same day-to-day productivity use cases rather than just chatbot mindshare.

Sources: 9to5Mac, Runtimewire, BigGo Finance, AI Weekly

4. Dataiku launches a product to find and police every AI agent a company has running, on any platform

Dataiku unveiled Agent Management on September 24 at its Dataiku Succeed conference — a standalone product that discovers every AI agent an enterprise is running regardless of which platform built it, tracks each one's business KPIs and technical performance, and tiers agents by the risk they pose, with general availability set for October. It connects into the platforms enterprise teams already use to build and run agents, including AWS Bedrock, Databricks Agents, Google Vertex, Microsoft Copilot Studio and Azure AI Foundry, Salesforce Agentforce, and Snowflake Cortex, and is priced per instance annually with monitoring metered per agent. The launch responds to a gap this digest has repeatedly flagged this year: agent creation is far outpacing organizations' ability to observe, govern and shut down the agents they've already deployed, and a growing share of enterprise AI budgets is shifting toward tools that manage that sprawl rather than build new agents. It's a sign the "who's watching all these agents" problem is becoming its own product category, not just a feature bolted onto existing agent-building platforms.

Sources: Dataiku, SiliconANGLE, BigDATAwire, AiThority

5. Ando emerges from stealth with $20M to rebuild team chat around AI agents, not just humans

Ando came out of stealth on September 24 with $20 million in seed funding from Accel, Index Ventures and Emergence Capital, after nearly a year building a team-messaging platform designed from the ground up to let AI agents participate in channels, threads and live conversations as first-class members with their own identity, permissions and shared context — rather than being bolted on as a bot inside Slack. The platform is agent-agnostic, so teams can bring whichever agents or coding harnesses they already use (Codex, Claude and Grok were named among them), and it's already in use by teams across roughly a dozen countries in software, real estate and financial services; Ando's own team has worked exclusively inside the platform since January. It's a direct shot at Slack's territory, staked on the bet that as agents take on more real work inside a company, the chat layer itself needs to be redesigned around human-agent collaboration instead of retrofitted with plugins — a thesis several other messaging and workflow startups have started testing this year as "AI employees" and persistent agents become a bigger share of how work actually gets done.

Sources: TechCrunch, GlobeNewswire, Yahoo Finance, MarTech Series
