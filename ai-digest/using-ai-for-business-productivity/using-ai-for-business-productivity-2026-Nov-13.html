1. Costco's CIO says AI has lifted productivity up to 60% on some tech projects, and is widening its India build-out

Costco's chief information and digital officer, Javier Polit, said AI has raised productivity by as much as 60% on certain technology projects, and that those gains are why the retailer is building more of its global tooling from its Hyderabad technology centre. The centre has a little over 70 staff today and could grow to several hundred over five to six years, though Polit gave no firm hiring target; one tool already built there is a geofencing feature that speeds member check-in and checkout. Costco also plans to shift its tech workforce towards about 75% employees and 25% contractors, from roughly half and half now. The 60% figure is the executive's own claim, with no breakdown of which projects it covers, and the outlets carrying it are repeating one Reuters-sourced story. Still, it is a rare specific productivity number from a retailer known for being cautious with technology, and it is tied to a concrete hiring and sourcing decision.

Sources: The Star (Reuters), Yahoo Finance, GuruFocus, The 420.in

2. Nebuly raises $21M to measure what enterprise AI agents are actually worth

Turin- and New York-based Nebuly closed a $21 million (about €19M) Series A led by Planven, with Exor Ventures, CDP Venture Capital, Tenacity Capital and others joining. Its platform plugs into agents built on Claude, ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot and tries to tie their use to business outcomes: for internal tools, whether work actually gets done, and for customer-facing assistants, AI-influenced revenue, churn risk and upsell signals. Coverage framed the pitch around a familiar problem, that companies make hiring and headcount decisions based on assumed AI productivity rather than measured results, because most only track usage. One report puts contracts at $100,000 a year and up, aimed only at large enterprises, and notes Datadog now sells a competing agent-measurement console. The round adds to a growing category of AI-value measurement tools, following Larridin's raise earlier this month.

Sources: SiliconANGLE, Tech.eu, The Next Web, Tech Funding News

3. OpenAI and Upwork study: AI-related freelance projects carry budgets about 52% higher, but hourly rates barely move

A joint OpenAI–Upwork study published 10 November matched ChatGPT usage data against Upwork job postings across 63 work subcategories to see how AI is changing what businesses buy from freelancers. AI-related fixed-price projects carried budgets roughly 52% higher than comparable non-AI work and involved around 40% more hours in their first 90 days, while the adjusted gap in posted hourly rates was only about 1.5%. AI-related briefs also became about 45% more varied in task mix over the study period. The authors suggest the bigger budgets largely reflect broader project scope rather than a pay premium, so firms are buying bigger bundles of work rather than paying more per hour. The study does not separate ChatGPT's specific effect from AI tools in general, and because OpenAI is a co-author, the results deserve some caution.

Sources: OpenAI, Let's Data Science, AI Weekly, BERI

4. SHL's "AI Rebound" survey: nearly 1 in 5 US workers replaced by AI were later asked back

Assessment firm SHL surveyed more than 1,000 workers each in the US and UK (fieldwork in June and July 2026) and found that 19% of US respondents who had been laid off or replaced by AI were later asked to return by the same employer, against 13% in the UK and 24% among US workers aged 18 to 24. SHL calls the pattern "AI Rebound", and it suggests some companies cut staff for AI faster than the technology could actually do the work. Returning workers are negotiating hard: 51% wanted a significant pay rise, 42% wanted written job-security guarantees and 40% wanted to know how AI would be used in their role. For leaders, the finding is a warning that rushed AI-driven cuts can mean rehiring the same people at a higher cost.

Sources: Fortune, IT Brief UK, People Matters, 24/7 Wall St.

5. Linux Foundation formally launches Agent Name Service, a DNS-based identity layer for AI agents

At KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America on 10 November, the Linux Foundation took on the Agent Name Service (ANS) as a governed foundation project, turning June's statement of intent into an open standard with its own technical steering committee. ANS uses existing DNS infrastructure to give AI agents verifiable identities and to let other systems discover them, so one company's agent can check who it is dealing with before exchanging data or acting. GoDaddy built the first working version on top of an IETF draft, eleven organisations back the project, and the foundation's AGNTCY Agent Directory Service can already verify ANS identities. For businesses putting agents to work across company boundaries in procurement, payments and customer service, shared identity is basic plumbing that has been missing. Critics, including The New Stack, argue it inherits DNS's old and fragile trust hierarchy.

Sources: Linux Foundation, Cloud Native Now, The New Stack
