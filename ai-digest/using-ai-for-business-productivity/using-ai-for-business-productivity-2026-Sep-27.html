1. EY: nearly half of big US companies are quietly bypassing their own AI governance rules

EY published survey findings on September 15 from 202 board members, C-suite executives and VPs at U.S. public companies with at least $1 billion in revenue, finding that 47% admit their organization has skipped internal AI governance processes to speed up an urgent deployment — even though 98% of the same companies say formal AI policies exist on paper. The gap is worse specifically around agentic AI: 49% of respondents whose companies use autonomous agents say governance frameworks were never updated to cover them, and 26% say they cannot reliably detect unauthorized AI agents running inside their own organization. Separately, 85% report that at least some agentic systems already take actions — flagging security threats, running code — without a human in the loop, and 36% say an AI system has already caused a materially negative incident such as data loss or operational disruption. It's a concrete data point behind a theme this digest keeps surfacing: deployment speed is consistently outrunning the oversight meant to contain it, and a written policy isn't the same thing as a followed one.

Sources: EY Newsroom, TechInformed, Dark Reading, Global Legal Post

2. California's second attempt at a "No Robo Bosses Act" now sits on Newsom's desk, deadline September 30

California's SB 947, nicknamed the "No Robo Bosses Act," cleared the state Senate 28-10 and the Assembly 53-14 this month and now awaits Governor Newsom's signature or veto by September 30, 2026. The bill would bar employers from relying solely on an automated decision system to fire or discipline a worker, instead requiring independent human verification before any AI-informed termination or discipline decision, and would take effect July 1, 2027 if signed. It's a rewritten version of SB 7, which Newsom vetoed in October 2025 for being "unfocused" and overlapping with existing employment law; this year's sponsors narrowed the language specifically to automated firing and discipline rather than broader AI use, and it arrives with bipartisan legislative support. For any business running AI-assisted performance management or HR decision tooling in California, the outcome by Sept 30 sets the first state-level precedent on how much human oversight automated employment decisions legally require.

Sources: Hoodline, MLex, Crowell & Moring, Bloomberg Law

3. AI coding startup Factory triples its valuation to $5B in five months

Factory, which builds a platform for enterprises to run their own autonomous "software factories" rather than a patchwork of disconnected coding agents, raised $200 million on September 15 at a $5 billion valuation — more than tripling the $1.5 billion valuation it held just five months earlier in April. The round was led by a mix of private-equity and venture investors including Blackstone, Sequoia Capital, Khosla Ventures and Insight Partners, alongside angel investors such as Marc Benioff, bringing Factory's total funding past $400 million. Founded in 2023, Factory pitches itself as giving enterprises a coordinated, self-improving software-development environment rather than a collection of standalone AI coding tools bolted onto existing workflows. The valuation jump is one of the clearest market signals yet that investors see autonomous software engineering — not just chat-based coding assistants — as the next big lever for enterprise developer productivity.

Sources: Pulse2, The Next Web, Tech Startups, Factory.ai

4. UK's consultation on AI workplace monitoring ("bossware") closes the same week as California's bill decision

The UK government's consultation on workplace monitoring technologies — covering location tracking, keystroke logging, biometric access and AI-driven performance evaluation and algorithmic management — closes for public comment on September 30, 2026, the same deadline Governor Newsom faces on California's AI-firing bill. Launched July 8 as part of the Employment Rights Act 2025 reform agenda, it's weighing three policy options ranging from non-statutory guidance to a statutory code of practice to new legislation, driven by concern that AI-enabled monitoring now lets employers automate opaque, large-scale decisions about workers faster than existing rules anticipated. Roughly a third of UK employers are already estimated to use some form of "bossware," and the consultation's outcome could produce anything from voluntary guidance to a binding legal framework governing how monitoring software must be disclosed and managed. Together with California's parallel bill, it's a sign that AI's productivity gains inside a company are now being matched, almost in lockstep, by governments moving to regulate how those same tools are used to manage the people doing the work.

Sources: A&O Shearman, Littler, The Register, Global Workplace Insider

5. Axya raises $17M CAD to bring AI procurement automation to manufacturers

Axya, a Montreal-based startup building an AI-powered procurement platform for manufacturers, announced a $17 million CAD Series A on September 24, led by McRock Capital with participation from Yamaha Motor Ventures and Canada's Business Development Bank Industrial Innovation Venture Fund. The platform automates sourcing and purchasing workflows — RFQs, supplier comparison, purchase-order management — tasks manufacturers have historically run through email threads and spreadsheets, and the company is using the round to expand internationally alongside a new banking relationship with CIBC. It's a smaller deal than this week's headline enterprise-agent funding rounds, but a concrete example of AI productivity tooling reaching a specific, unglamorous back-office function rather than only general-purpose chat assistants or coding agents. For manufacturers still running sourcing manually, it's the kind of narrow, workflow-specific AI tool this digest has repeatedly found delivers more measurable ROI than broad "AI everywhere" rollouts.
