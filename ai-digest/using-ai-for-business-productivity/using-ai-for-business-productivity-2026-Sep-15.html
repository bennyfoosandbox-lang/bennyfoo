1. AI's own leaders call for a slower pace — and Wall Street sells off AI stocks in response

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a weekend essay arguing "we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," a call quickly backed by OpenAI's Sam Altman, former Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis and Elon Musk. The essay landed alongside reports that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind have quietly held working-group talks since July on a joint industry standards body to audit and test frontier models — an idea Hassabis first floated in a July essay modeled on Wall Street's FINRA, which Altman has said labs may need to stand up themselves without government backing. Markets reacted fast on Monday: Nasdaq 100 futures fell roughly 1.6%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 5.9%, and memory-chip names led the rout — Micron and SanDisk down about 6%, SK Hynix down 7% — as investors read the pacing calls as a signal that AI infrastructure spending could cool, even though no hyperscaler has actually cut capex guidance. For businesses budgeting around AI vendor roadmaps and chip supply, it's a reminder that the executives selling the AI transformation are now the ones publicly questioning how fast it should go.

Sources: CNN, PYMNTS, 24/7 Wall St., Bloomberg, InvestorPlace

2. Microsoft becomes the first hyperscaler to publish a formal AI Code of Conduct

Satya Nadella announced Sept. 13 that Microsoft would open a six-week public consultation on a "Code of Conduct" governing its first-party MAI models, publishing the draft the next day and arguing that "any pursuit of superintelligence has to be grounded in the core principle that if the AI we build is not helping humanity and under human control, it's not worth pursuing." The framework commits Microsoft to broad access and choice across the AI stack, enterprise control over its own learning loops and models, and explicit support for "deliberate pacing" and independent evaluators — welcoming, rather than waiting on, the industry-coordination push described above. Nadella also argued governance of superintelligence "cannot be controlled by a handful of entities" and needs representation from academia and countries beyond today's AI leaders. For enterprise buyers, it's the first time a major model vendor has put a public, revisable rulebook — not just a blog post — around how its own frontier models get built and shipped.

Sources: Business Standard, Dataconomy, Unite.AI, Neowin

3. Small business owners say they'll use AI to replace staff — but only when the math forces it

A Sept. 14 Small Business Expo Research Team survey finds small-business owners taking a pragmatic, cost-driven stance on AI-linked staffing decisions: 45.2% say they would replace an employee with AI only if the business case became unavoidable, while roughly 1 in 4 say they already have or would. The survey splits owners into "pragmatic adopters" — the largest group, who deploy AI wherever there's a clear ROI case — and "boundary-setters," who are open to the technology generally but resist letting it directly drive headcount calls. That caution sits alongside continued adoption growth: broader industry tracking this year puts small-business generative-AI usage at 58%, up from 40% in 2024, concentrated in customer service and content drafting, suggesting owners are adopting the tools readily while staying deliberately guarded about the employee-replacement question specifically. For a segment that employs roughly half of the US private workforce, that gap between tool adoption and workforce substitution is one to watch.

Sources: Small Business Expo, Small Business Expo

4. Fortaegis raises $50M to secure AI infrastructure at the silicon level

Amsterdam-based Fortaegis closed an oversubscribed $50 million Series A on Sept. 13, led by Singapore's Serendipity Capital with participation from Tokyo Electron's venture arm, ASML, TNO and Prodrive Technologies, to commercialize chip-rooted security for AI infrastructure, defense, telecoms and autonomous systems. The technology exploits unique physical variations baked into individual silicon chips to generate encryption keys that are never permanently stored on the device, making them both harder to steal and, the company says, resistant to future quantum-computing attacks. Founded in 2023, Fortaegis already works with more than 25 companies and governments and plans to begin commercial chip manufacturing in 2027. As enterprises push more sensitive workloads through AI agents and third-party models, the round is a bet that trust in AI systems will increasingly need to be anchored in hardware, not policy documents alone.

Sources: The Quantum Insider, The AI Insider, WOWTALE, Fortaegis

5. Hong Kong's Qupital raises $300M to scale its AI-driven trade-finance engine

Hong Kong-based Qupital announced $300 million in combined new capital on Sept. 13 — a Series C round led by M Capital plus new asset-backed financing from Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group and Quester Capital — to expand the AI risk-scoring engine it uses to underwrite working-capital loans for cross-border e-commerce sellers on Amazon, JD.com, Tmall, TikTok Shop and Pinduoduo. The company says cumulative loans processed through its platform have passed $9.5 billion, and it plans to use the new capital to expand financing capacity across China, the US, Japan and Southeast Asia while eyeing an eventual IPO. It's a reminder that a meaningful share of this year's "AI for business productivity" capital isn't going into chatbots or copilots at all, but into AI models embedded inside decades-old back-office functions like credit underwriting.

Sources: Business Intelligence, Alternative Credit Investor, The Manila Times
