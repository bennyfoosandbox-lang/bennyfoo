1. Fed opens its AI-and-productivity inquiry: Warsh sees an idea boom, Jefferson says the gains aren't visible yet

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh opened a one-day conference at the San Francisco Fed on 16 November. It is the first public session of the Fed's new Productivity and Jobs Task Force, and speakers included executives from Stripe, Palo Alto Networks and Affirm, plus Brookings' Cecilia Rouse and Gina Raimondo. Warsh argued that AI could supercharge idea generation and growth, but he also set out a downside case: if expected returns on AI infrastructure don't arrive, valuations could fall and unemployment could rise. Fed staff are now modelling several AI scenarios. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson was more cautious. He said AI is clearly lifting efficiency on specific tasks, but it is too early to credit it for recent productivity gains. He cited survey evidence that AI had raised sales per employee by only about 0.5% over three years. For business leaders, the takeaway is that the central bank now treats AI productivity as an open question, not a given. A sustained productivity boom could even mean higher rates rather than lower ones.

Sources: CFO Dive, AP via WSB-TV, NewsChannel 9

2. Update: Nadella explains Microsoft's 60,000-person exit offer, and won't promise the jobs stay the same

Satya Nadella spoke publicly on 18 November about the October voluntary exit offer to about 60,000 Microsoft employees, roughly a quarter of the workforce. He presented it as part of rebuilding Microsoft into a company where everyone uses AI to do their work. He also said the company isn't simply swapping staff for AI. A day later he was asked directly whether the buyouts were a prelude to AI-driven layoffs, and he didn't rule it out. He said he wished he could say everything would stay the same, but he couldn't. Microsoft earlier said it expects fewer than 5% of eligible staff to take the offer. This updates our 16 November item on the Microsoft 365 outage and AI-era staffing. It is now the clearest statement from a big-tech CEO that AI adoption is expected to change who does the work, not just how quickly they do it.

Sources: TechCrunch, Windows Latest, Fortune

3. Banks put AI agents on a token budget as McKinsey warns agent spend could hit 20% of labour costs

Reuters, drawing on McKinsey's Global Banking Annual Review, reports that banks worldwide are now working to control fast-rising AI agent bills. Token spending could reach about 20% of a bank's labour costs. Several lenders have set up dedicated "AI FinOps" teams to own that spend. The first fix, routing employee queries to cheaper or pricier models by complexity, breaks down once agents call each other. McKinsey's Vik Sohoni told Reuters that token use then climbs quickly. McKinsey's own CEO guide says that orchestration, retries, monitoring and human review add to the bill, and that tuning models, context and infrastructure can cut a servicing agent's cost by more than 80%. Any company scaling agents should expect this: once AI does real work, unit economics need someone accountable for them, just as cloud costs did a decade ago.

Sources: Reuters via The Standard, Euronext (Reuters), Allwork.Space, Analytics Insight, McKinsey

4. Ex-Freshworks team raises $53M for Dreamteam, an AI-native CRM that fills itself in

Dreamteam was founded in 2025 by former Freshworks executives Anand Venkatraman, Ramesh Parthasarathy and Senthil Kanthaswamy. It has raised $53 million across seed and Series A rounds led by Accel, with Together Fund and Premji Invest also investing. Its pitch is to replace Salesforce and HubSpot rather than bolt agents onto them. A shared "Company Brain" learns from email, meetings and calls. Four named AI coworkers research prospects, draft follow-ups, flag deal risks and handle CRM admin. Their proposed changes appear as draft cards that a rep approves. TechCrunch reports that Plex's ad-sales team picked it over HubSpot. For sales organisations, this attacks one of the oldest productivity sinks, manual CRM data entry, though displacing incumbents at enterprise scale remains unproven.

Sources: The Next Web, YourStory, Inc42, IT Brief UK, AI Weekly

5. Genpact launches Onyx, betting that process know-how, not models, is the last mile for enterprise agents

Genpact made Onyx generally available on 16 November, after previewing it at its Agentic Operations Day in New York. Onyx is its platform for building, running and governing AI agents in enterprise operations. At its core is a "Process Intelligence Core". This knowledge layer captures the workflows, policies, exceptions and human judgment calls that Genpact has accumulated over decades of running outsourced finance, supply-chain and banking operations. Agents handle the volume while experts validate them and own the outcomes, inside a guardrail harness with configurable human oversight. CEO BK Kalra argues that the value lies in edge cases that public training data and prompt libraries miss. Several customers told Constellation Research that process work was a prerequisite for scaling agents. This is a services giant turning its operating playbooks into agent infrastructure, and it competes directly with SaaS vendors for back-office automation budgets. The launch is from earlier this week and was passed over in yesterday's digest.

Sources: Constellation Research, Genpact press release (Placera), Newsweek, Genpact
