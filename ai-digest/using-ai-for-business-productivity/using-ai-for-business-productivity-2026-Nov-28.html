1. SAP is reportedly weighing at least 10,000 job cuts as it reorganises around AI

Bloomberg reported on 26–27 November, citing people familiar with the plans, that SAP is considering cutting at least 10,000 jobs. That would be at least 9% of its workforce of more than 110,000. Every department has reportedly been asked to find savings, with product and engineering and sales under particular scrutiny. The final number isn't set and could change in talks with employee representatives. SAP declined to say whether cuts are planned and said only that it reviews its organisation continuously. The plan would come on top of a 2024 restructuring that affected roughly 9,000–10,000 roles, two executive-board reshuffles this year under CEO Christian Klein's AI-first strategy, and a July hiring freeze meant to free money for AI development. Some coverage notes that an employment guarantee SAP agreed with German labour representatives runs to 2028, so the cuts may fall mostly outside Germany. For enterprise buyers, the world's largest business-application vendor is now reorganising its own engineering and sales teams around AI, not just selling Joule agents to customers.

Sources: BusinessWorld (Bloomberg), CNBC, Taipei Times, Dataconomy, Investing.com, Technology.org, CXM World

2. Google opens Gmail, Drive and Docs to AI agents through official MCP servers, and will require agents to use them from February

On 24 November Google made generally available eight hosted MCP servers for Workspace, covering Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar, Chat and Tasks, with Keep listed as coming soon. It also released an official Workspace command-line tool, gwscli. Admins get new controls under Security › API Controls: they can trust, limit or block a specific agent's client ID, decide which services it can reach, and require agents to ask the user for confirmation before acting. Audit logs now mark actions taken by an agent and record whether the user approved them. The bigger change is the deadline. From 1 February 2027, any agent that reads or writes Workspace data is expected to go through these MCP servers or the CLI rather than calling the REST APIs directly. A free Standard tier allows 5,000 requests per day per project per product, and paid Scaled-tier billing starts in April 2027. Google says controls to block agents from specific Gmail messages or Drive folders are still in development, and analysts note the MCP servers don't yet do everything the REST APIs do. Companies with home-grown or third-party agents wired into Workspace have about two months to check that those integrations still work.

Sources: Google Workspace blog, TechRepublic, i10x analysis, Data Phoenix, Sulat AI & Tech News, i-SCOOP, Tech Stackups, DEV Community

3. J.P. Morgan Wealth Management estimates AI could displace 24 million knowledge-worker jobs in five years, mostly as roles left unfilled

A J.P. Morgan Wealth Management report published on 27 November puts a number on AI displacement: about 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over five years, or roughly 1.3% of the global knowledge workforce a year. Coverage describes it as a conditional scenario, not a forecast. It assumes half of enterprise AI spending goes on replacing people who would otherwise have been hired, and that the AI costs about half as much as those workers. If two-thirds of the effect falls on the US, about 16 million American workers would be affected. The report expects most of the change to show up as positions that are never filled rather than as layoffs. It also notes a large gap between exposure and use: about two-thirds of tasks could in theory be done with AI, but US workers currently use it for only about 6% of their working hours. Its more likely outcome is jobs changing shape rather than disappearing. That fits Anthropic's customer interviews (item 5): for workforce planners, the place to look for AI's impact is hiring plans and open requisitions, not layoff announcements.

Sources: Open Magazine, NewsBytes, Asianet Newsable, Particle, WebIndia123, Daily AI Brief

4. Black Friday reality check: Bain finds AI shopping agents handle just 0.4% of online orders, and many retailers are blocking them

Bain & Company research reported over Black Friday puts AI-agent purchases at about 0.4% of global e-commerce transactions and about 0.2% of their value. That is a sobering counterweight to the traffic numbers retailers and platforms have been promoting. A Human Forward test of ChatGPT, Gemini, Muse and other agents on 200 websites found that about half of big retailers and brands don't let AI assistants shop their sites. Amazon blocks outside agents (it is reportedly in talks to let Meta's Muse in), eBay, United Airlines and Kohl's restrict or block them, Walmart and Sephora are open, and Coach and Gap allow browsing but not checkout. Reliability is a problem too. Visa's own tests found agents failed 43% of shopping tasks, often by buying the wrong item, and only 23% of US shoppers say they trust AI to pay for them. Shopify told CNBC that agent-sourced sales were about 3.3 times last year's, but gave no dollar figure, so that is rapid growth from a very small base. For businesses investing in agent-ready commerce, AI is clearly working as a discovery channel, but the transaction layer, meaning permissions, checkout and accuracy, is still where agents fail.

Sources: The Next Web, CNBC, Seoul Economic Daily, Startup Fortune, AI Weekly, Digest AI, Capital Digest

5. Anthropic interviewed 58 business customers: two-thirds are hiring less because of AI, but layoffs are rare

Anthropic's economics team published "Working with AI" on 23 November. It is a qualitative study based on 66 interviews at 58 of Anthropic's enterprise customers. The authors say plainly that the sample is neither random nor representative, and that most findings are what participants described, not measured results. Even so, the pattern is clear. Domestic layoffs driven by AI were rare: only two companies said AI had fully eliminated a position. People at about two-thirds of the companies said they were hiring less because of AI, and ten companies were hiring more, mainly for implementation and IT support. The sharpest finding concerns contractors. Nine companies described cutting contracted developers, customer-service staff, legal counsel and back-office functions, many of them already offshore. The authors suggest this could be an early warning for the international business-services industry. Coverage also highlights that top-down rollouts raised surveillance worries and worker resistance. This is a fallback item from earlier this week that isn't yet in the knowledge base. Read alongside J.P. Morgan's estimate (item 3), it points the same way: AI is shrinking hiring pipelines and outsourcing contracts before it shows up in headcount cuts.

Sources: Anthropic, Runtime Wire, MLQ.ai, Mad Robot, Remuneration Labs, ExplainX
