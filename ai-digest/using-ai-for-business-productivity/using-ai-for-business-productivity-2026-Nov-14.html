1. Microsoft 365's near-24-hour outage takes Copilot down with Outlook and Teams

Starting on Thursday 12 November, a worldwide Microsoft 365 disruption cut access to Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams, SharePoint, Defender XDR and Microsoft 365 Copilot. Microsoft's own notices warned that Copilot Chat might not load or might be unable to reach email, calendar and files. Microsoft said the cause was a fault left by an earlier network capacity upgrade, not a cyberattack, and reported broad recovery around 17:50 UTC on 13 November, roughly a day after the problems began. Bloomberg called it one of Microsoft's longest outages and said it disrupted government offices, traders and businesses. On 13 November Claude.ai and Claude Code also had a separate login outage while Claude's Microsoft 365 connector stayed down. Together, the two incidents show that once AI assistants sit inside daily email and document work, an outage stops the assistant along with the inbox, so continuity plans need to cover the AI layer as well.

Sources: Bloomberg, BleepingComputer, CNBC, Euronews, BetaNews, Business Today (Claude outage)

2. Capgemini: nearly 6 in 10 large firms overspent on AI, and two-thirds of them took a cash-flow hit

A Capgemini Research Institute snapshot published 12 November surveyed 500 senior executives at companies with more than $1 billion in revenue, and 59% said their AI spending over the past year came in significantly above budget or expectations. Among those firms, spending ran about 24% over on average, about one in five exceeded budget by more than 30%, and two-thirds reported knock-on effects such as lower free cash flow or cuts to discretionary spending. Half said AI spending was approved without full visibility of its costs, and only 56% are fully confident they can forecast costs as usage scales. Most respondents plan to move to multi-model strategies, with 58% expecting to use more open-weight models, though so far only about one in five has actually changed models or routing. For leaders, the message is that the price of AI productivity is now a CFO issue, and controlling token and model costs is part of making the business case work.

Sources: Capgemini, CFO Dive, CFOtech UK, EuropaWire, EconoTimes

3. Google makes Gemini 4 Hydrogen generally available for faster, cheaper agentic work

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on 12 November, a faster, lower-cost model aimed at everyday coding and agentic workflows. It is generally available in the Gemini Enterprise web and mobile apps, Workflow Builder, the Gemini API and developer tools, and GitHub made it available in Copilot on 13 November. Google says early enterprise testers saw runs 2 to 2.8 times faster and used 12% to 23% fewer tokens than with its earlier Flash model. Google also added a multi-model Orchestrator to its Antigravity coding environment, which routes reasoning and execution between models within a single session to cut token use. Admins should note that the model is switched off by default in the Gemini Enterprise app, and Google describes it as a short-term GA release with at least 45 days' notice before retirement. Coming the same week as Capgemini's cost-overrun survey, it is another sign that vendors now compete on cost per task as much as on capability.

Sources: Google, Gemini Enterprise release notes, GitHub Changelog, InfoWorld, Digital Trends

4. Workday and Josh Bersin bring a 'Galileo' workforce-strategy agent to Workday customers

Workday and The Josh Bersin Company announced on 12 November that Galileo, Bersin's HR research agent, is coming to Workday customers through Workday's Sana AI platform. The agent draws on more than 50,000 pages of Bersin research and is meant to advise HR leaders on workforce strategy, including hiring approaches, skills development and organisation design, rather than make employment decisions itself. The Sana integration is expected in February 2027, but Workday customers can already buy Galileo. In coverage the same day, Workday's CEO was reported as expecting meaningful AI-related job displacement within five years. Together, the two show HR software vendors selling AI both as a tool for planning workforce change and as one of the causes of that change.

Sources: PR Newswire, Small Business Trends, Blockonomi

5. Gusto's 2026 State of Small Business: two-thirds of owners now use AI, and AI policies are spreading

Payroll provider Gusto's 2026 State of Small Business report, released 12 November, found that 67% of small businesses use AI tools. One in four businesses with employees now has a formal AI policy, up from 15% a year ago, and adoption is highest in professional services. Owners said they were more confident in their own companies than in the wider economy, with 86% saying their business is meeting or beating their launch expectations, while prices, borrowing costs and hiring remain challenges. The sample is limited to Gusto customers who answered the survey, so it is not a random cross-section of US small businesses. Even so, the rise in written AI policies suggests small firms are moving from trying AI out to managing it as a normal part of operations.

Sources: PR Newswire, Middletown Life (syndicated release)
