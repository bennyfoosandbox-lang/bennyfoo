1. Update: Citi and Citizens make customers liable for whatever outside AI agents do in their accounts, hacks included

In October, the big US banks started blocking outside AI agents from logging in to customer accounts. This week they began putting the risk on paper. Citi has added a section to its Online User Agreement that makes customers responsible for anything a third-party AI agent does with their account. That includes activity by hackers if the agent leaks credentials or is compromised, while agents Citi provides itself carry no extra liability. TechCrunch reports that Citizens emailed customers a similar warning this week, and secondary coverage says PNC updated its terms on 18 November as well. None of the coverage reports an actual customer loss so far, so the risk here is contractual. For any business using agents to run finance work like payments, reconciliations or treasury, the signal is clear: the institutions you connect to will not take on agent risk for you, so identity, permissions and audit trails have to be in place before you hand over the keys.

Sources: TechCrunch, Crypto Briefing, The Paypers, American Banker

2. Microsoft launches an AI Transparency Initiative with 40+ firms to make agent actions auditable across vendors

Microsoft used Ignite on 17 November to launch the AI Transparency Initiative with more than 40 organisations, including Accenture, Adobe, KPMG, Mastercard and Volvo Cars. The goal is open, cross-vendor standards for proving what AI models and agents actually did. Microsoft points to an "evidence gap": today's controls can't independently check a model's own account of its behaviour. Its proposed framework has three parts: See, Verify and Control. It records who authorised a request, which model and runtime ran it, what data and tools it used and what actions it took, and labels whether each piece of evidence came from the model, the runtime or an independent identity or policy system. Microsoft says it will expand its work with NIST on agent standards and build the evidence into Microsoft AI, Foundry and Copilot, readable by other vendors' security tools. For now it is a statement of direction rather than a spec: there is no draft standard and no delivery date. Still, it is aimed squarely at the governance gap that analysts keep naming as the main brake on scaling agents.

Sources: PR Newswire (Microsoft), Channel Insider, YourStory, Let's Data Science

3. LSE and Protiviti: AI saves users a fifth of their week, but nearly half fear being judged for not using it enough

The third annual AI-at-work report from the LSE's Inclusion Initiative and Protiviti came out on 17 November. It surveyed 3,001 professional employees worldwide between March and May. 74% now use AI at work, up from 29% in 2024, and users say it saves them about 19% of their working week. They put 72% of that time back into other work rather than taking it as slack. The headline finding is about social pressure: 48% worry about being judged for not using AI enough, against 20% who worry about being judged for using it. The researchers warn of "workload creep", where the capacity AI frees up is quietly filled with more output and wider responsibilities. They also found that strong performance still tracks qualities like conscientiousness and resilience more than AI use. For managers, the lesson is that AI mandates and usage dashboards can push people to perform usage for its own sake, and time savings can disappear unless someone decides where the freed hours go. The figures are self-reported and the report comes from its sponsors.

Sources: Protiviti (press release), Onrec, Webull News, LSE Inclusion Initiative (report)

4. Cognition launches Devin Bots, long-running manager agents that run teams of coding agents

On 17 November Cognition released Devin Bots: long-running "manager" agents that keep memory across a project, break the work into tasks and assign them to individual Devin coding agents over days or weeks. A Bot also chooses which model to use for each part of the job, sending routine code to cheaper, faster models, and Bots can hand work to each other, for example a QA Bot passing a bug to a Developer Bot. Fortune presents it as Cognition's answer to Anthropic's Duet and OpenAI's Dots, arguing that a manager built around a coding agent beats a general-purpose one. Beta testers report 4.5 times more merged pull requests per message and about 2,000 PRs a week. These are vendor-supplied figures, and coverage notes that cost, security and reviewer capacity are still open questions. Together with Replit Missions earlier in the week, it shows the industry moving from one assistant per developer to a hierarchy of agents. That leaves code review and oversight as the bottleneck.

Sources: Fortune, AI Weekly, AlphaSignal, Crypto Briefing

5. Nvidia's $115.8B quarter: AI demand from enterprises and smaller clouds is now growing faster than from hyperscalers

Nvidia reported third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on 17 November. Revenue was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier, and data-center revenue was $107.6 billion. Fourth-quarter guidance is $130 billion, which includes an extra week. The most relevant number for business adoption is the bucket covering AI clouds, industrial and enterprise customers. Coverage of the filing puts it at $47.5 billion, up about 195% from a year earlier, against roughly 71% growth from hyperscalers. Management expects enterprise revenue to more than double in fiscal 2028. It also said agentic workloads make up about 80% of tokens on OpenRouter and that agentic AI is creating CPU demand that outstrips supply. The quarter was overshadowed by a leadership change, with Colette Kress now interim CEO, and the segment breakdown comes from secondary write-ups of the filing. Even so, it is a strong sign that spending is moving from building frontier models to enterprises running agents in production.

Sources: NVIDIA Investor Relations, SEC 8-K exhibit, PYMNTS, Xenospectrum
