1. OpenAI launches GPT-6 Astra, its "frontier intelligence for work"

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on 3 September, explicitly positioning it as a model built to execute business work rather than just chat — filling out forms, updating CRM records, organizing calendars, researching the web and drafting the results into documents or emails. The rollout starts with a limited set of organizations before reaching all ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise users, and lands simultaneously through the OpenAI API, Microsoft Azure Foundry and AWS Bedrock — a broader synchronized enterprise launch than prior flagship releases. Enterprise administrators must opt Astra into their workspace since it ships off by default, reflecting continued caution about giving a more capable, tool-using model default access to company systems. API pricing lands at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, roughly 2.5x the prior flagship — a signal of how much more enterprises will pay for agentic, work-executing capability over plain chat.

Sources: OpenAI, Microsoft Azure Blog, CNBC, VentureBeat

2. OpenAI data: the gap between AI "frontier firms" and everyone else has tripled since January

OpenAI's latest Enterprise Signals report finds that organizations in the top 10% of enterprise AI usage now generate 8.3 times as many output tokens per active user as a typical firm — up from 2.6x in January, meaning the gap more than tripled in eight months. The report frames this less as a chat-volume story and more as a shift in kind: leading firms have moved from using AI for research, drafting and individual productivity toward wiring agents into repeatable workflows that execute across multiple systems while retaining institutional context. The divide varies sharply by industry — an 11.7x gap in information and technology versus 5.3x in manufacturing — suggesting the leaders aren't just spending more, they're restructuring how work gets assigned to AI versus humans. For the broader "AI and business productivity" story, it's a concrete data point that adoption breadth and adoption depth are diverging fast, and depth is where the measurable returns concentrate.

Sources: OpenAI Enterprise Signals, OpenAI, VKTR, citybiz

3. Enterprise "AI operating system" startup Wonderful raises $550M, doubles its valuation to $5B in six months

Amsterdam-based Wonderful closed a $550 million Series C led by Insight Partners, with Salesforce joining as a new strategic investor, at a $5 billion valuation — more than double the $2 billion mark it hit just six months earlier, and its third raise in about 20 months. Wonderful builds what it calls an AI operating system: a model-agnostic coordination layer that sits across an enterprise's agents, workflows and AI-native applications so companies aren't locked into a single vendor's stack as models and capabilities keep changing. Since its Series B the company has grown to roughly 650 employees and expanded into more than 35 markets, and it says the demand pattern is consistent — once AI reaches production in one part of a business, adoption quickly spreads to others, and enterprises want one governed layer to manage that sprawl rather than dozens of point tools. The new funding goes toward product development and expanding the deployment teams that get enterprise customers live.

Sources: TechCrunch, AIwire, CTech, Dealroom

4. Smarsh's Agentforce rollout shows what a mature enterprise customer-service AI deployment looks like

Compliance and communications-data firm Smarsh detailed results from scaling its two Salesforce Agentforce-built AI agents: Archie, a customer-facing support agent that's now the first point of contact for 22,000 Smarsh Central portal users, is hitting a 72% self-service deflection rate with a 2.6-out-of-3 resolution-confidence score, beating the company's own targets. Its newer internal agent, Emmy, gives support representatives instant account snapshots — ownership, status, case history — plus AI-assisted resolution guidance at case close, and is saving reps more than seven hours per case. Smarsh frames the deployment as proof that agentic AI can absorb real support volume without sacrificing the governance and security that regulated-industry customers require, pairing automation with human escalation rather than replacing it outright. It's a useful counterweight to this year's steady drumbeat of AI-pilot-failure surveys: a concrete, metered production deployment with real numbers attached, in a compliance-heavy vertical that's typically slow to adopt.

Sources: Smarsh, Businesswire, 01net

5. Workato and Lenovo wire agentic workflows into Qira, so a request can span Gmail, Slack, Teams and Outlook without app-switching

Announced at Lenovo Innovation World during IFA 2026, Workato and Lenovo are expanding Lenovo Qira — Lenovo's ambient, cross-device AI assistant — so it can interpret a natural-language request and carry it out across a growing set of connected productivity apps, including Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook Mail and Calendar, Slack, Microsoft Teams and Google Contacts. The integration uses Workato's connector and orchestration layer to let Qira ask for approval before taking an action, then execute it across whichever app actually holds the relevant data — the kind of "intent to action" execution most productivity-AI pitches promise but few ship with this many enterprise-grade connectors live at launch. It's a notable example of a device maker, rather than a software vendor, becoming the interface layer for cross-app agentic work, betting that ambient AI embedded at the device level — not another standalone app — is where knowledge workers will actually delegate tasks. For businesses standardizing on Lenovo hardware, it meaningfully lowers the integration lift for agentic workflows versus building custom connectors in-house.

Sources: Lenovo StoryHub, Businesswire, 01net
