1. Anthropic packages Claude for the factory floor with 17 manufacturing skills

Anthropic released Claude for Manufacturing on 12–13 November: a plugin for Claude Team and Enterprise plans with 17 ready-made skills for estimators, quality engineers, technicians and buyers. The skills cover quoting, quality, operations, supply chain and design. Claude drafts quotes, non-conformance reports, 8D problem-solving reports and production reports from a plant's own records, and staff review each draft. Four connectors link it to Autodesk Fusion, Infor, Microsoft 365 and Oracle NetSuite. Admins can require approval before a connector changes anything, or block changes entirely, and coverage stresses that those vendors' systems remain the systems of record. For business productivity it is another sign that the big model makers now sell job-specific bundles rather than a general chatbot, and the target here is the paperwork that slows down plant teams. Anthropic says more manufacturing skills are coming and has a technical webinar planned for 17 November.

Sources: The Decoder, Dataconomy, Manufacturing Digital, Yahoo Tech, Claude — Manufacturing

2. McKinsey: agentic AI in banking pays back in about ten months

Part two of McKinsey's Global Banking Annual Review 2026, titled "The AI disruption dividend," puts a price on agent projects. Across 52 agentic AI use cases, McKinsey found investments paid back in about ten months on average, with a three-year return of roughly 2.9 times. The figures come from client interviews in September and October 2026 and McKinsey's own AI ROI benchmark, and they assume the first year is spent building and ramping up with no benefit. The report expects early adopters to cut the cost of retail-banking functions by at least 15%. It also warns that these efficiency gains may not turn into lasting profit growth if competitors make the same cuts. Related McKinsey work on financial crime is a reminder that the first use case alone can take four to six months to reach production, and that first risk-governance approvals can take six to eight months, so the payback clock starts later than a project's kickoff.

Sources: McKinsey — The AI disruption dividend (PDF), McKinsey — Global Banking Annual Review 2026, SecureWorld

3. Vodafone Germany will cut about 2,300 jobs, partly because AI means it needs fewer staff

Vodafone said on 9–10 November that it will reduce headcount in Germany, its biggest market, by about 2,300 positions over the next two financial years. A Swedish wire report puts that at nearly one in five German employees. Customer service is being folded into the consumer and business sales units, and IT is moving closer to them. The company names three AI uses behind the change: analysing network faults and proposing fixes, creating and steering advertising campaigns, and supporting customer-service staff. Vodafone says these structural changes mean it needs fewer people, but it has not said which roles go or how much it expects to save. The cuts sit inside a wider plan to take more than €2 billion a year out of costs across Europe and shared operations by 2030. Revenue pressure in Germany is part of the backdrop, so this is AI-enabled restructuring rather than a clean AI-replacement story.

Sources: Light Reading, PPC Land, Börsen-Zeitung, Placera, The Irish News

4. JobAssist survey: half of workers use AI even when they think it slows them down

A JobAssist survey, reported by TechRadar on 9 November, found that 52% of workers have used AI on a task they believed they could finish faster without it. Some 63% say their employer encourages or expects AI use for at least some tasks. 39% have pointed out their AI use to managers even when they felt it didn't improve the result, and 31% kept using AI after deciding it hadn't helped with speed or quality. The business risk is a misleading picture. Usage dashboards measure whether people use AI, not whether it improves the work, and staff who are reluctant to report poor results can leave leaders with an overly rosy view of their AI programmes. The figures are self-reported and the coverage doesn't say how many respondents face hard mandates, but they add to the evidence that adoption metrics alone overstate productivity gains.

Sources: TechRadar, Tech Insider

5. Update: after the outage, Microsoft's AI-era staffing comes under scrutiny as its 60,000-person exit offer runs

This updates the 14 November item on the Microsoft 365 outage. By 15 November, Microsoft said access to Outlook, Teams and Copilot was fully restored after roughly a day and a half. It blamed an error made during a network capacity upgrade and said the fault was unrelated to AI or agents. Bloomberg reported that trading desks had to fall back on phones, and CNN cited an estimate that the outage could cost more than $7 billion. A Bloomberg Opinion column called the outage "a bad look" for AI-driven job cuts. Separate coverage notes that Microsoft's voluntary exit offer is open to about 60,000 staff at level 70 and below in the US, UK, Canada and India, including people on its Copilot and AI Experiences teams, with decisions due in early December. Microsoft says AI isn't behind the buyouts, and nothing ties the outage to staffing cuts. Even so, the episode shows that buyers of agent-heavy productivity suites need resilience plans for when everything runs on a single vendor.

Sources: Bloomberg Opinion, BusinessWorld (Bloomberg), Bloomberg, Insurance Journal, CNN, UC Today, RuntimeWire
