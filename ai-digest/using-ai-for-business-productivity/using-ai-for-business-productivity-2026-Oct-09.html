1. SAP Connect puts Joule Work at the centre of an 'Autonomous Suite' for finance, HR, supply chain and spend

At SAP Connect in Las Vegas (5–7 October), SAP positioned Joule Work as the main interface for its Joule assistants and agents, and extended new assistants across all five pillars of its Autonomous Suite: finance, supply chain, HCM, spend and customer experience. CIO reports SAP says Joule Work is already live with 110,000 of its own employees and claims about 20% productivity gains across finance, HR and procurement, a vendor figure not independently verified. General availability starts this month through the SAP for Me portal, though one analysis notes most capabilities land by early 2027. New supply-chain agents (including a Moody's supplier-risk feed in Ariba) are slated for Q4, and a new access-governance assistant extends identity controls to autonomous agents. Counts of 'live' agents vary by outlet, so treat catalogue-size claims with care.

Sources: SAP Connect 2026 top announcements, SAP News, CIO, SAPinsider, The New Stack

2. SAP agrees to buy Belgian work-intelligence startup TechWolf to feed skills data into SuccessFactors and Joule

On 6 October SAP said it will acquire Ghent-based TechWolf, whose platform continuously models the tasks, skills and roles inside a company from the HR and business systems it already uses. SAP plans to make it the 'intelligent core' of SuccessFactors and feed its graph into Joule for skills-based hiring, workforce planning and role redesign. TechWolf is expected to stay an independent entity under CEO Andreas De Neve and remain available to non-SAP customers; terms were not disclosed and closing is expected in Q4 2026, subject to regulatory approval. For productivity buyers it signals that vendors see an evidence-based map of what work people actually do as a prerequisite for deploying agents into HR processes.

Sources: SAP press release (Placera), The Next Web, Techzine, SAPinsider

3. Copilot Studio adds 'Hooks' so enterprises can enforce policy on agents instead of just asking nicely

Microsoft's Copilot Studio now offers Hooks, according to agent-news roundups from 6 October: deterministic checkpoints that run around an agent's execution to inspect, modify, approve or block actions at moments such as prompt submission, before and after a tool call, or on error. The point is the difference between guidance and enforcement, since a hook always fires when its trigger occurs while a prompt instruction can be ignored by the model. Hooks can reportedly be changed at runtime without rebuilding the agent. It sits alongside agent flows and the newer Workflows preview as Microsoft's answer to the predictability worries holding back wider agent rollouts. Details here come from third-party write-ups rather than Microsoft documentation, so check Microsoft Learn for shipped behaviour.

Sources: AI Agents Directory, 6 Oct brief, HubSite365: Copilot Studio Hooks, Microsoft: agent flows in Copilot Studio

4. ElevenLabs doubles its valuation to $22B as enterprise voice agents become more than half of revenue (from 30 Sep)

ElevenLabs completed a $300 million employee tender offer at a $22 billion valuation, double the $11 billion set in February, with Wellington and T. Rowe Price leading and Goldman Sachs among new holders. Because it is a secondary sale, the money goes to sellers and not the company, and ElevenLabs gave no updated revenue figure. The company says enterprise customers now make up more than half of revenue, driven by its ElevenAgents platform rather than the original voice model. Financial services is a lead use case: Better.com's loan agent reportedly handles nearly 100,000 mortgage calls a month and resolves 35.5% of borrower inquiries end to end. 5. Who pays when your AI agent strikes a deal nobody approved? Lawyers say the business does, for now

A SmartCompany piece published today warns Australian businesses could be held responsible for unauthorised deals, discounts and promises made by AI agents acting on their behalf. Wider legal commentary agrees the law is unsettled: questions of whether an agent's mandate covered the deal, whether a binding contract exists without agent legal personhood, and who absorbs fraud or error losses remain open. One analysis points to the US Uniform Electronic Transactions Act as a model that binds a person to their electronic agent's actions within the scope they set up. A UK Payments Association report found 58% of UK online merchants believe AI agents have already bought through their systems. Practical advice converges on tight agent permissions, logging of mandates and actions, and checking contract terms and payment-provider rules.

Sources: SmartCompany, Legal Technology: agent-to-agent marketplace, Bar & Bench, Dock.io
