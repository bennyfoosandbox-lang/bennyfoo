1. OpenAI buys a presentation startup, assembling an office suite one acquisition at a time

OpenAI confirmed on August 9 that it has acquired NextSlide, a roughly year-old startup whose software turns documents, prompts and other workplace context into a polished, presentation-ready deck, with the small team folding directly into the ChatGPT product organization. Terms weren't disclosed, and reporting suggests the deal itself closed earlier in the year with OpenAI only making it public now. NextSlide founder Ahmed Beshry previously built Caper AI, the smart-cart checkout startup Instacart acquired in 2021 — this is the second time his company has been absorbed into a larger platform. The purchase extends a pattern of OpenAI quietly buying up narrow productivity tools to build something closer to a full office suite inside ChatGPT, pushing further into the everyday document-and-slide creation work that has long been Microsoft 365 Copilot and Google Workspace's home turf rather than staying confined to chat.

Sources: Forbes, Yahoo Finance, The Next Web, Dealroom

2. OpenAI arms vetted "defenders" with a cybersecurity model that ignores its own safety refusals

OpenAI expanded its Daybreak cybersecurity program on August 10-11 with two access tiers and a new model, GPT-5.6-Cyber, purpose-built for vulnerability research and exploit development. In OpenAI's own testing, the model completes 95% of advanced cybersecurity tasks, versus roughly 1.5-2% for the standard GPT-5.6 Sol model with ordinary safety guardrails — a gap OpenAI argues is necessary because attackers already operate at that capability level and defenders don't. Access stays restricted to trusted partners, including Accenture, IBM, CrowdStrike and Cloudflare, split between "Daybreak Blue" for general defensive work like incident response and patch validation, and "Daybreak Red" for advanced red-teaming and exploit validation. The launch lands alongside this month's rush of AI-agent-security funding rounds, another sign that large AI labs and enterprise vendors alike are racing to keep pace with AI-accelerated attacks that are starting to outrun human-paced security operations.

Sources: TechCrunch, VentureBeat, Axios, The Hacker News

3. Anthropic pushes Claude deeper into enterprise IT: self-hosted Claude Code, wider compliance coverage

Anthropic shipped a cluster of enterprise-focused Claude updates in early-to-mid August. Claude Code v2.1.224 (August 7) added a self-hosted-runner mode, letting teams run Claude Code sessions — including scheduled and remote ones — on their own infrastructure instead of Anthropic's cloud, with custom tooling and internal network access. Days later, Anthropic extended its Compliance API to cover Claude Cowork (desktop, web and mobile) and Claude Code (CLI and desktop) in beta for Enterprise customers, so security teams can pull unified session content and audit logs from every surface Claude touches in one place. Anthropic also added beta scanning of third-party skills and plugins uploaded to Enterprise workspaces, checking for malicious content before they can run. Together the changes read as an attempt to close the gap between how fast employees are adopting agentic coding tools and how slowly security and compliance teams can typically vet new software — a mismatch several vendors have flagged this year as the real bottleneck on enterprise AI rollouts, not model capability.

Sources: Claude (official), Enterprise DNA, Releasebot changelog

4. Datadog: the fifth of its customers using AI tools now generate 80% of its revenue

Datadog's Q2 2026 earnings, reported around August 10-11, showed revenue up 36% year-over-year to $1.12 billion, with the company breaking out for the first time how concentrated its AI-driven growth has become: roughly 20% of Datadog's customers use its AI integrations — including its MCP Server, Bits AI Security Agent and GPU monitoring tools — but that fifth of the customer base now accounts for about 80% of annual recurring revenue. Non-GAAP gross margin held at 79.6% with a 23% operating margin, and Datadog raised full-year guidance to $4.45-4.47 billion, implying roughly 30% growth. It's one of the clearest public data points this earnings season for a pattern several enterprise vendors have described only anecdotally so far: AI-linked spend is concentrating hard in the customers who go all-in on agentic tooling rather than spreading evenly across the base, a dynamic that's starting to reshape how software vendors price and prioritize AI features.

Sources: GuruFocus, CryptoBriefing, StockTitan, Yahoo Finance

5. A new platform tries to put one dashboard over every AI tool a company uses

AI/R, an agentic-AI company led by CEO Gil Torquato, launched AI/Cockpit One on August 10 — a platform meant to be the single governance and cost-control layer over an organization's entire AI stack, spanning its own AI/Cockpit development tools, homegrown agents, and third-party platforms like Langflow, Flowise and n8n, rather than one more point solution. It adds enterprise SSO with granular permissions by user, team, project and resource, telemetry on what every connected agent is doing, and cost controls that break down token consumption by team or business unit with configurable budget limits. Torquato frames the launch as the company's shift from a developer tool into a business-wide operations layer, giving marketing, HR, sales and IT one shared place to see and control AI usage instead of each function running its own ungoverned tools. It adds to a fast-growing "AI control plane" product category this year, all racing to solve the same underlying problem: most enterprises can no longer see, let alone govern, the sprawl of AI tools their employees have quietly adopted.

Sources: GlobeNewswire, AiThority
