1. A landmark NBER survey says 90% of executives still see no AI productivity boost

The National Bureau of Economic Research surveyed nearly 6,000 senior executives across the US, UK, Germany and Australia using comparable questions, and roughly 90% said AI has not measurably improved productivity at their firms over the past three years — even as adoption keeps climbing (78% of US firms, 71% UK, 65% Germany, 59% Australia). The study found 89% of firms reported no measurable effect on labor productivity and more than 90% saw no measurable employment impact, directly undercutting the boardroom narrative that AI is already transforming organizational performance. Text generation remains the most common enterprise use case, followed by visual content creation and ML-based data processing — a pattern suggesting most deployments are still surface-level rather than workflow-redesigning. The research first circulated in late August and drew renewed, heavy coverage this week, making it a useful counterweight to the vendor-driven ROI claims elsewhere in this digest.

Sources: Fortune, Business Standard, People Matters

2. Google Cloud carves Gemini Enterprise into industry-specific agent suites, starting with banks and law firms

Google Cloud announced Gemini Enterprise for Financial Services and Gemini Enterprise for Legal on August 25 — the first in a planned series of vertical-specific packages built on its broader Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, which Google says nearly 90% of Fortune 100 companies now use. The financial-services package targets underwriting, compliance and other high-stakes workflows with "verifiable data explainability" so institutions can audit agent decisions, while the legal package automates end-to-end workflows across practice areas. Launch partner FlowX.AI is bringing specialized industry agents into the financial-services package via Google Cloud Marketplace, an early sign Google is building an ecosystem of vertical partners rather than shipping one-size-fits-all agents. The shift toward narrower, governed tools for specific regulated industries is a direct response to the trust and compliance gaps still holding back enterprise adoption more broadly.

Sources: Google Cloud Press Corner, PYMNTS, Google Cloud Blog, Business Insider / GlobeNewswire (FlowX.AI)

3. Bain says its AI engagements are delivering 30-50% productivity gains — double the market average

Bain & Company and Anthropic announced a global partnership on August 25, with Bain named a "Global Premier" partner in the Claude Partner Network — Anthropic's top tier, also held by Deloitte and Cognizant. Bain said engagements involving complex legacy codebases with limited architectural context have delivered productivity gains of 30% to 50%, compared with the 15%-or-less typically reported elsewhere in the market — a striking claim set against the NBER findings above that most firms see no measurable AI productivity effect at all. Internally, Bain has rolled Claude out to its entire 19,000-person workforce, with more than 7,000 employees actively using it within weeks of the firm-wide deployment. The partnership will focus on AI strategy, technology modernization and AI-enabled operations for joint clients, adding another major consultancy to the roster competing to own enterprise AI transformation work.

Sources: Bain & Company, MarketScreener, Unite.ai

4. Moving AI from pilot to production is running 3-5x over budget, Six Five Summit told

At the Six Five Summit's "AI Unleashed 2026" event opening August 25, industry data presented showed enterprises moving AI systems from pilot into production are seeing costs run three to five times their original projections, with the overrun widening as systems move toward autonomous, multi-step operation. Dell Technologies used its Summit session to argue enterprises need brutal prioritization to close that gap: the company said it narrowed roughly 900 candidate AI projects down to 13 targeted initiatives, a filtering exercise it credits with helping decouple revenue growth from cost structure. Intel separately urged enterprise buyers at the summit to evaluate AI purchases on ROI rather than hardware specifications, reflecting a broader shift in vendor messaging toward cost discipline as CFOs tighten scrutiny of AI budgets. Paired with the NBER findings above, the summit's message reinforces a theme running through this week's coverage: enterprise AI's economics, not its raw capability, are now the binding constraint on adoption.

Sources: Investing.com, Tech Times, Daily Political

5. Okta makes AI agents first-class citizens in enterprise identity systems

Okta announced general availability of Agent SSO on August 24, bringing the open Cross App Access (XAA) standard into the core identity product used by more than 20,000 customers, at no extra cost on existing SSO plans. The feature lets organizations register AI agents in Okta's Universal Directory as first-class identities alongside humans, so administrators can apply centralized access policies and issue short-lived tokens instead of relying on static API keys or one-off consent prompts when an agent needs to act inside another application. That addresses a governance gap running through this digest: as agents proliferate into finance and legal workflows (see Gemini Enterprise above), most still piggyback on shared human credentials, creating opaque, over-privileged access chains security teams can't audit. By folding agent identity into existing SSO infrastructure rather than a separate product, Okta is betting enterprises will govern agents the same way they already govern employees — a lower-friction path to the controlled autonomy vendors like Google and FlowX.AI are also chasing.

Sources: Okta Newsroom, TechNode Global, VMblog
