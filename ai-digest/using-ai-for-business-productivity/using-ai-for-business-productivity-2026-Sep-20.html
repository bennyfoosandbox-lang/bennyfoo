1. Anthropic's annualized revenue is on pace to top $100 billion in 2026, the New York Times reports

The New York Times reported on Sept. 18 that Anthropic's annualized revenue is tracking to exceed $100 billion this year, up from roughly $65 billion in July — more than 50% growth in about two months, and over 10x where the company stood at the end of 2025. The reporting ties the surge directly to broad enterprise adoption of Claude Code and Cowork for coding and general office work, and lands as Anthropic prepares for a stock-market debut as soon as November. It follows Anthropic separately telling shareholders it expects a second consecutive profitable quarter while gearing up for a roughly $2 trillion IPO valuation. For anyone weighing whether agentic AI actually pays for itself in the enterprise, this is the starkest public data point yet: a ramp this steep only happens if paying customers are finding real, repeatable productivity value rather than running pilots.

Sources: Bloomberg, Axios, Gulf News, Yahoo Finance

2. OpenAI launches Astra for Law, wiring GPT-6 into a 230-million-URL legal research index

OpenAI introduced Astra for Law on Sept. 17, a configuration of its GPT-6 Astra model paired with a legal-research index spanning more than 230 million URLs of U.S. case law, statutes, regulations, court rules and administrative decisions, updated daily. The product bundles access controls and workflow tooling built specifically for law firms, and ChatGPT for Word — with proofreading, suggested edits and formatting checks for legal drafting — became generally available the same day. Selected firms get initial access through a program with confidentiality protections for client work, and legal-AI vendors including Harvey and Legora, plus practice-management platforms Relativity and Clio, will be able to build directly on top of Astra for Law. It's OpenAI's most direct enterprise-verticalization push yet into a profession already reshaped by AI money — Harvey alone recently raised $550M at a $15.5B valuation — setting up a head-to-head with the legal-specific tools those startups have spent years building.

Sources: OpenAI, Unite.AI, LawNext, SiliconANGLE

3. Disney names ex-Character.AI CEO Karandeep Anand as its first-ever Chief Technology Officer

Disney named Karandeep Anand — the former CEO of Character.AI — as its first-ever Chief Technology Officer on Sept. 18, a newly created role overseeing enterprise technology, infrastructure, data and AI platforms, and product and engineering companywide, reporting directly to CEO Josh D'Amaro effective Oct. 2. The hire is notable given Disney sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist letter roughly a year earlier over unauthorized use of its characters in chatbots, underscoring how quickly adversarial and hiring relationships with AI companies are blurring for large media and entertainment firms. Anand's mandate spans consolidating Disney's fragmented tech stack and pushing AI adoption across a business that includes parks, streaming, studios and consumer products, rather than owning a single product line. It's one of the clearest recent signals that "AI platform leadership" is becoming its own C-suite mandate at legacy enterprises, not something bolted onto an existing CIO role.

Sources: The Walt Disney Company, TheWrap, Deadline, Disney Investor Relations

4. Abacus launches AI³, a governance-first push to get regulated industries adopting AI faster

Managed IT and cybersecurity provider Abacus launched AI³ (Abacus AI Acceleration) this week, a governance-first initiative meant to help organizations in regulated industries — healthcare, financial services and similar sectors — adopt and scale AI without outrunning their compliance and risk controls. Built on partnerships with OpenAI, Google and Microsoft while remaining vendor- and model-agnostic, the offering leans on Abacus's existing position running client infrastructure to layer continuous AI governance and management on top, rather than treating adoption and compliance as separate projects. It's a direct response to the adoption-outpacing-governance gap flagged repeatedly this month, including a Nationwide survey finding small businesses are buying AI faster than they can manage its risks. Abacus is betting regulated-industry customers will pay a managed-services premium specifically to close that gap rather than build the governance layer themselves.

Sources: PR Newswire, AIwire, CIO Influence

5. SCSK packages a vertical AI agent set for Japan's real estate industry

Japanese IT services firm SCSK unveiled a Real Estate Industry AI Agent Set on Sept. 18, a pre-built, vertically packaged suite of AI agents covering sales brokerage, rental management and leasing workflows, drawing on domain knowledge accumulated from more than 50 existing real-estate enterprise clients. Rather than offer a generic AI agent platform, SCSK embedded real-estate-specific business logic — compliance requirements, customer-matching patterns and leasing processes — directly into the agents' design, aiming to shift the industry from staff-dependent, tacit-knowledge-driven operations toward reproducible, data-and-AI-driven execution. The launch targets labor shortages from Japan's demographic decline and rising compliance demands in a sector that has historically resisted software standardization. It's a useful case study in how vertical AI agent vendors compete against horizontal platforms: by encoding years of industry-specific process knowledge that a generic LLM wrapper can't replicate out of the box.

Sources: SCSK (press release), Futurum Group
