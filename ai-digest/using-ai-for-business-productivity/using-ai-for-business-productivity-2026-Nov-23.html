1. Microsoft makes price its pitch: a study it paid for puts Claude at $205 a user, and Anthropic says the real figure is under $75

Microsoft is selling Copilot to businesses on cost. A study it commissioned and ran with GigaOm, testing in October, says the monthly total cost of an everyday user is $30 on Copilot, $81 on ChatGPT Enterprise and $205 on Claude Enterprise once usage charges are added to seats. For advanced users the figures are $96, $149 and $299. Anthropic replied over the weekend that its non-coding Claude Enterprise users spend under $75 a month on average. Analysts point out that Microsoft hasn't published the seat prices, usage rates or models behind each total, and that neither figure has been independently audited. The same week, Microsoft launched MAI Code 2 in GitHub Copilot and claimed about 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's coding-benchmark score at about 5% of the cost. That ratio uses a 50% launch price that runs until May 2027. For buyers, the takeaway is that the usage-based charges on agentic work, not the seat price, now decide what enterprise AI costs, and vendor TCO claims need to be checked against your own workloads.

Sources: Microsoft Copilot blog, FourWeekMBA, Yahoo Finance, GeekWire, gagadget, WindowsForum, The New Stack, InfoWorld, eesel AI

2. A coding agent leaked Together AI's credential, and a Dragos study found three AI hosting providers breached

On 21 November Together AI disclosed that an AI coding agent working in its public xorl GitHub repository had committed a credential. The credential opened a private Hugging Face repo of stored customer prompt samples, mostly from June and July. Automated scanning reverted the commit, but an outside party had already downloaded the data. Some of the credentials the intruder then tried turned out to be canary tokens planted by Dragos researcher HD Moore. Over five months Moore sent about two million tagged prompts to AI services and found that attackers were stealing data from three hosting providers, none of which knew until Dragos told them. Coverage reports that OpenRouter then removed those three providers. Together AI is adding secret scanning on pull requests, new rules for coding agents, and zero data retention as the default for new accounts. For businesses, this is a concrete case of an AI agent becoming the security incident. Agents that commit code need the same secret controls as human engineers, and prompts sent through third-party inference providers should be treated as data that may be kept.

Sources: StartupHub.ai, explainx, Shattered, Dragos, Unite.AI, AI Chat Daily

3. Signal now blocks screenshots by default to keep phone AI agents out of chats

On 20 November Signal turned on screenshot blocking by default so that AI agents built into phones can't read its chats. On iOS a new "Block Siri AI" toggle comes switched on. On Android, Signal turns on its screen-security mode when it detects an AI assistant reading the screen. The change follows Reuters reporting that Meta's Muse agent read Signal messages through permissions granted at setup. Signal says the fix is only partial, because agents can still reach files already on the device, and it has asked Apple, Google and Microsoft for a system-level way to opt apps out of AI access. This matters for workplace AI because the screen-reading agents being sold as productivity tools can also capture confidential messages. Commentators expect banking, health and enterprise apps to follow, which could make it harder for agents to work across apps. IT teams should decide which apps on work devices agents are allowed to see.

Sources: The Hacker News, Engadget, Cybernews, Reclaim The Net, Inside AI News

4. Littler: the share of European employers cutting staff because of AI has doubled to 28%

Littler's 2026 European Employer Survey, published 18 November, finds that 28% of European employers have reduced their workforces because of AI, up from 13% a year ago. The survey covered nearly 500 executives, in-house lawyers and HR leaders and was fielded in August and September. A wider group of employers has changed job responsibilities or slowed hiring because of AI, and that group has also roughly doubled. The survey also finds that employees are using AI against their employers: nearly three-quarters of respondents report more staff using it to draft complaints or legal claims. Meanwhile, delays to the EU AI Act's employment rules leave companies unsure what compliance will require. For businesses, the productivity gains are now showing up in headcount, and the legal and employee-relations side of AI adoption is growing faster than most governance plans. (Published earlier this week; included under the widened freshness window.)

Sources: Littler press release, Littler report, Littler report (PDF), HR Brew, Yahoo Finance

5. EY: buyers of IT services firms now pay for the ability to deploy AI at scale

EY's Technology Services Deal Insights for July to September, reported on 22 November, counts 247 announced deals, up 4.7% from 236 the quarter before. EY says acquirers are now favouring firms with proven AI-led revenue, strong data engineering, and the ability to help clients deploy AI at scale. Cloud services rose to 22% of deals from 18%, and data and analytics to 13% from 10%. Private equity interest is concentrated in data, enterprise applications, cybersecurity and managed infrastructure. EY India's Shivani Nagpaul describes buyers as wanting the data and application expertise that turns AI spending into measurable results. Many enterprises have struggled to show AI ROI, and the deal market is putting a price on the integrator skills needed to close that gap, which is likely to change which partners and consultancies businesses end up buying from.

Sources: EY, Social News XYZ, Punjab Kesari, The Hawk
