1. McKinsey: 60% of agentic AI costs go to response refinement — and 93% of enterprises are already over budget

McKinsey's July 2026 Quarterly report finds that even as per-token inference prices have collapsed — GPT-3.5-level capability fell from $20 to $0.07 per million tokens between early and late 2025 — enterprise AI spending tripled anyway, and 93% of respondents to McKinsey's May 2026 Enterprise AI FinOps Survey have exceeded their AI budgets. The culprit isn't the headline model price: 60% of total agentic AI spend goes to "response refinement," the iterative checking, correcting, and re-generating cycles agents run before delivering usable output, a cost center most 2025-2026 budgets never line-itemed. Consumption-based pricing that bills by answer length, plus routing simple tasks through expensive frontier models, compounds the overrun as pilots scale into production. McKinsey's broader 2026 State of AI survey (1,719 respondents) found one in five organizations have already throttled AI use purely because of cost — a reversal where economics, not capability or internal resistance, is now the adoption brake.

Sources: McKinsey & Company, MarketScale, The Economic Times

2. Enterprise AI's center of gravity shifts from developer speed to business execution

A new report circulating across Asian business press argues enterprises are moving past the first wave of AI adoption — measured almost entirely by how much faster developers could write code — toward a harder question: whether AI actually speeds up the business as a whole. The report's authors note large-enterprise software delivery is rarely bottlenecked by coding itself; governance, architecture, security, compliance, and organizational complexity are the real constraints on how fast a company can ship. It frames this as a shift from "prompt-to-code" tools toward "context-to-code" systems that surface governed, reusable enterprise knowledge at the point of execution, resting on three foundations: secure context, reusable intelligence, and governed execution. The reframing matters for productivity measurement broadly — coding-speed metrics that dominated 2024-2025 AI ROI conversations may be increasingly poor proxies for whether AI is actually accelerating the business.

Sources: ETCIO (Economic Times), The Hans India, Livemint

3. SAP survey: enterprise AI is delivering insights, not the cost savings CIOs budgeted for

An SAP survey reported by CIO Dive finds a widening gap between what enterprise AI programs were sold on and what they're actually delivering: companies see real, measurable value in customer insights and operational intelligence, but the cost and time savings written into original business cases — the numbers finance teams actually budgeted against — remain difficult to demonstrate. The finding echoes a pattern showing up across several reports this week (see McKinsey, above): AI value is real but is landing in places that are harder to put a dollar figure on than the headcount or IT-cost reductions executives were promised. That mismatch between "the ROI is real" and "the ROI isn't where the spreadsheet said it would be" is increasingly the story of enterprise AI's second year, and it puts pressure on CIOs to build new ways of measuring soft-value gains rather than retrofitting them into cost-savings frameworks that don't fit.

Sources: CIO Dive, MarketScale

4. 97% of companies have deployed AI agents — 55% call the result a "chaotic free-for-all"

A survey of 1,200 C-suite executives and 1,200 non-technical AI-using employees, run by independent research firm Workplace Intelligence for enterprise AI company Writer, finds a stark split between "performative adopters" — who rolled out AI and measured success by licenses issued and token usage — and a minority of "value creators" who scoped adoption narrowly around specific, measurable problems. Fifty-nine percent of companies now spend $1M+ a year on AI and 97% of executives say their company deployed AI agents in the past year, yet only 29% report seeing significant returns from generative AI, and 55% describe AI use inside their company as a "chaotic free-for-all." The performative pattern carries real cost: 67% of executives believe their company has already suffered a data leak or security breach from an employee using an unapproved AI tool, and 36% of companies still have no formal plan for supervising AI agents. The survey puts concrete numbers on a theme this digest has tracked all week — governance and measurement discipline, not model capability, is what separates AI spend that pays off from AI spend that doesn't.

Sources: International Business Times, Writer (survey publisher), Product Impact

5. IBM lays out an "AI-first enterprise" roadmap for Asia-Pacific, targeting 2030

At its Think on Tour Singapore event, IBM outlined an AI operating model built around four pillars — intelligence, action, operations, and trust — and cited its own research projecting AI could drive more than 40% productivity gains by 2030 as investment keeps accelerating. The announcement leans heavily on sovereign AI: IBM is partnering with Foxconn subsidiary Visionbay.ai to build a full-stack AI platform for Asia-Pacific enterprises, running on Red Hat OpenShift with watsonx handling AI development and governance. Foxconn's AI and quantum lead noted AI servers overtook smartphones as the company's largest revenue source last year, with Foxconn now manufacturing roughly 45% of the world's AI servers — underscoring how much regional infrastructure buildout is riding on the same productivity bet. The roadmap frames 2030 as the year AI moves from "a productivity tool" bolted onto existing operations to something embedded across every part of the business, a framing IBM will need real customer results to back up.

Sources: The Manila Times, Manila Bulletin, IBM Newsroom (APAC)
