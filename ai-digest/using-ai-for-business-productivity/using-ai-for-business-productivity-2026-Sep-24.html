1. Altman and Amodei tell the UN Security Council the industry can't govern AI alone

On September 23, OpenAI's Sam Altman and Anthropic's Dario Amodei addressed a UN Security Council session on AI, convened by France, urging governments to adopt international standards before agentic-AI risk outpaces oversight. Amodei proposed three concrete steps: narrow bans on specific harms such as AI-enabled bioweapons design, mutual verification systems so nations can check each other's safety commitments, and a common testing and incident-notification standard. Altman called for shared criteria to assess model capabilities and the safeguards companies use, while Yoshua Bengio and Hugging Face's Clément Delangue also briefed the council. For enterprise buyers, the appearance is another sign that the regulatory ground under AI vendor selection and compliance planning is still shifting fast — it landed the same day Trump dismissed AI oversight calls as a "globalist scheme," leaving companies with no unified global rulebook to plan procurement and governance against.

Sources: CNN, Bloomberg, UN News, Fortune

2. Go.AI raises $85M to sell regulated industries AI that never leaves their firewall

Go.AI (rebranded from Go Abacus earlier this month) closed an $85 million Series A led by Updata Partners on September 22, lifting total funding to $90 million, to scale its on-premises, "examiner-ready" AI infrastructure. Its customers — concentrated in financial services, healthcare, aerospace/defense and manufacturing — run fully on-prem deployments processing more than 12.5 million queries a day on Go.AI's Go.OS software and Go1 hardware, built around auditability rather than cloud convenience. The company says it has grown past 200 customers with annual recurring revenue up more than 8x year over year while remaining profitable, a notable claim in a funding market where many AI vendors are still burning cash to buy growth. The raise underlines a durable segment of enterprise AI demand: compliance-minded buyers who need AI capability without sending data to a third-party cloud, a gap Go.AI plans to keep exploiting as more industries face tightening data-residency and audit requirements.

Sources: BusinessWire, Yahoo Finance, Finovate, fintech.global

3. WSO2 ships a vendor-neutral control plane to rein in enterprise AI agent sprawl

WSO2 reached general availability on Agent Manager in mid-September — an Apache 2.0 open-source control plane meant to govern AI agents regardless of which model, framework or deployment target built them. The product targets a problem this digest has flagged repeatedly this year: as teams stand up agents on different stacks (Claude, OpenAI, homegrown), organizations end up with fragmented governance, duplicated risk reviews and inconsistent access controls. Agent Manager entered beta in June before reaching GA in September, giving security, IT and data leaders a way to standardize identity, policy and observability across agents without forcing every team onto one vendor's framework. It's a competitive entry into the same governance category ServiceNow, Cisco and Workday have all built proprietary tooling for this year — WSO2's pitch is that a neutral, open-source layer avoids adding further lock-in at exactly the moment agent sprawl is becoming a board-level risk.

Sources: WSO2, InfoQ, GlobeNewswire, IT News Africa

4. Zoom's AI "Revenue OS" pitches quota numbers, not just chatbot demos

Zoom unveiled an AI-powered "Revenue OS" on September 15, pairing a buyer-intelligence product (Common Room by Zoom) with two new Revenue Accelerator capabilities: Engage, which builds and prioritizes multichannel sales sequences across email and phone, and Forecast, which turns deal-level signals into real-time, roll-up-able revenue forecasts. Unlike many AI product launches that lean on hypothetical use cases, Zoom backed the announcement with customer numbers — Barracuda Networks reported a 15% increase in deals closed, a 5% improvement in deal velocity, and sales reps hitting 100%-140% of quota using the tools. IDC has separately named Zoom a Leader in its 2026 MarketScape for unified revenue orchestration, lending some third-party validation to the sales-productivity claims. For revenue and sales-ops leaders evaluating AI tooling, it's a useful data point in a market where proof of ROI, rather than feature lists, is increasingly the deciding factor.

Sources: Zoom, Yahoo Finance, GlobeNewswire, Telecom Reseller

5. Gartner to CIOs: don't trust your AI vendors' enterprise claims yet

At its September IT Symposium, Gartner analysts Daryl Plummer and Kristin Moyer told CIOs that AI and its leading vendors remain immature for enterprise use, arguing "trust in these vendors is not warranted yet" because many don't understand enterprise terms and conditions, liability, or operational-continuity requirements. Their core warning: the more AI an organization deploys, the harder it becomes to control or prove return on investment — a claim that lands alongside Gartner's own projection that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by 2027 over unclear ROI, escalating costs and inadequate controls, and a finding that 86% of CIOs already see AI-created risk growing faster than the value it delivers. The analysts' recommendations lean structural: an "AI central bank" function for systemic oversight, guardian agents empowered to kill misbehaving ones, and dedicated disaster-recovery teams for AI-caused messes. It reframes a familiar 2026 theme — AI adoption outpacing AI governance — as a vendor-trust problem specifically, pushing enterprise buyers to interrogate liability and support-continuity language in contracts rather than assume AI-specific terms are already standardized.

Sources: The Register, ARN
