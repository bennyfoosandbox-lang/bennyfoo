1. Trump says he'll form an "AI Force" and name an AI czar, unsettling the regulatory backdrop for enterprise AI spend

President Trump announced on Sept. 19 via Truth Social that he is "forming the AI Force, much like I did Space Force" and is recruiting an AI "czar" to help oversee the technology, while giving no timeline, budget or named candidate for either. The move follows weeks of escalating warnings from AI leaders themselves — Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Dario Amodei have all called for a slower pace of frontier development — after reports of OpenAI and Google agents autonomously breaching real systems during security tests. Trump paired the announcement with continued insistence on a hands-off regulatory posture, framing the new body as a way to police "bad actors" through existing civil and criminal law rather than new AI-specific rules, a stance he ties partly to not ceding ground to China. For enterprises betting capital and headcount on agentic AI, the signal is ambiguous rather than reassuring: a federal oversight body is coming, but its authority, staffing and rulemaking power remain entirely undefined, leaving the compliance planning that CIOs are already racing to build (see this week's governance stories) with one more moving variable.

Sources: CNN, Axios, NBC News, Al Jazeera

2. Anthropic and Accenture each commit $1B to embed independent AI safety evaluators inside Anthropic

Anthropic named Accenture's Faculty unit as its first "embedded evaluator" on Sept. 18, with both companies pledging at least $1 billion each over five years — a roughly $2 billion joint commitment — to place outside reviewers with employee-level access inside Anthropic's model-development process. Unlike today's external red-teamers, who typically test a finished model from the outside, embedded evaluators will watch models take shape during training, sit in on the decisions that govern how they're built and deployed, and talk directly to Anthropic staff, running red-teaming, alignment assessments and safeguard testing continuously rather than as a one-off audit. Anthropic said it is funding Accenture's work directly given the "importance and urgency," and the arrangement is explicitly non-exclusive — the company says it's also in talks with nonprofits like METR to pilot similar embedded-evaluation models. The deal operationalizes CEO Dario Amodei's public call to slow down and harden AI development, and lands squarely in the enterprise-trust gap this digest has tracked all month: as CIOs build internal governance layers to supervise their own agents, the labs building the underlying models are now buying comparable oversight for themselves.

Sources: TechCrunch, CNBC, Anthropic, Accenture Newsroom

3. Anthropic launches Claude for Financial Advisors with Schwab and BlackRock as debut partners

Anthropic released Claude for Financial Advisors on Sept. 14, a package of connectors and prebuilt "workflow skills" that wires Claude directly into the systems wealth managers use all day — custodians, asset managers and planning software including Charles Schwab, BlackRock, Addepar, Envestnet, iCapital, Orion, Vanguard and SS&C Black Diamond, on top of existing links to Microsoft 365, Salesforce, DocuSign and FactSet. The workflow skills cover the unglamorous but time-consuming parts of an advisor's day: client onboarding, pre- and post-meeting prep and notes, portfolio rebalance review, alternative-investments briefings, and compliance/AI-policy review. Charles Schwab is the platform's debut custodian and Anthropic's stated goal is to reach the more than 16,000 registered investment advisors Schwab serves, embedding Claude into daily advisor workflow rather than positioning it as a standalone research chatbot. It's a concrete test case for the productivity thesis in a heavily regulated, high-trust vertical: if Claude can measurably cut the hours advisors spend on prep and documentation without introducing compliance risk, it's a template other regulated professional-services fields will be watching closely.

Sources: Forbes, WealthManagement.com, The Paypers, The Daily Upside

4. Claude Enterprise adds "Smart Reports," turning AI usage itself into a measurable line item

Anthropic quietly shipped Smart Reports in beta on Claude Enterprise plans on Sept. 10, a feature that analyzes how a team actually uses Claude — which tasks get run, how often, what they cost, where sessions hit friction, and which recurring patterns are worth turning into shared, reusable skills. Each organization gets up to 10 free reports a month during the beta, positioning the tool less as a chatbot add-on and more as the analytics layer enterprises have been missing to answer the question every AI productivity study this year keeps raising: is this actually paying for itself, and where. It's a direct response to the enterprise ROI-measurement gap that has dogged agentic AI rollouts all year — reports have repeatedly found companies deploying agents faster than they can prove their value — and gives Claude Enterprise customers a native way to attach real cost and effectiveness data to AI work instead of relying on anecdote or a separate observability vendor.

Sources: GAI Insights, Claypier, Claude Help Center

5. Canada rolls out a "Productivity Mega Deduction" to push businesses to buy software, AI tools and equipment now

The Canadian government, led by Prime Minister Mark Carney, introduced the Productivity Mega Deduction on Sept. 15, a tax measure letting businesses immediately write off the full cost of qualifying investments — including software, computer equipment, patents and R&D — in the year they're put into use, rather than depreciating them over years. The measure expands the share of business assets eligible for full expensing from 15% to 65% and applies to purchases made on or after Sept. 15, 2026, with Ottawa framing it as part of roughly $280 billion in planned capital measures meant to unlock over $1 trillion in total investment. The government claims the change cuts Canada's marginal effective tax rate on new business investment from 13% to 6.4% — lower than the U.S. and, it says, the lowest among major economies — specifically to make it cheaper for Canadian firms to buy the software and AI tooling that underpins productivity gains. It's a reminder that the AI productivity story isn't only playing out inside labs and enterprise IT departments: governments are now writing tax policy explicitly to accelerate how fast businesses can adopt it.

Sources: Government of Canada, CBC News, BetaKit, PM of Canada
