1. Corporate America starts rationing AI as the token bill comes due

After a year of open-ended AI spending meant to signal ambition to Wall Street, technology and operations leaders at Uber, Meta, Microsoft, Salesforce and DoorDash are now actively rationing employee access to frontier models, according to new reporting. Some enterprises have blown through their entire annual AI budget in as little as three months, driven by frontier-model pricing (roughly $5 per million input tokens, $25–30 per million output tokens) and agentic workflows that chain dozens of steps together and burn far more tokens than a simple chatbot query ever did. The response is a bifurcated stack: routing routine work to cheap "mini-models," imposing per-user token budgets and usage dashboards, and reserving premium frontier models for a narrower set of high-value tasks. It's a sharp reversal from the "spend freely to look AI-forward" posture of the last year, and the clearest sign yet that AI costs are now getting the same budget discipline as any other line item.

Sources: Yahoo Finance, MarketScale, Media Copilot

2. Bessemer study: AI is now reshaping hiring, not just tasks, at fast-growing tech firms

A new global study from Bessemer Venture Partners finds AI adoption has moved well past pilot programs at high-growth technology companies, with more than half now making it central to operations across engineering, finance, HR, sales, marketing and customer support. Nearly nine in ten business leaders expect AI to significantly change how their organization functions within a year, and Anthropic's Claude is named the most widely adopted platform among participants. Critically, AI is now shaping headcount decisions directly: close to half of surveyed companies say they're achieving higher productivity without adding headcount, with some slowing recruitment for repetitive-task roles even as a smaller group creates brand-new AI-focused positions. Finance and HR are furthest along in day-to-day use (budgeting, forecasting, contract analysis, recruiting, offer letters), while sales and marketing teams still struggle to tie AI activity directly to revenue.

Sources: The Impressive Times, Economic Times, LatestLY

3. 7 in 10 IT leaders are racing to deploy AI without the governance foundation to do it safely

New research from Fleet Device Management, surveying more than 500 enterprise IT leaders, finds a stark gap between AI ambition and operational readiness: 46.5% rank AI-driven automation as their top investment priority for the next 12–24 months, but only 29.6% are prioritizing "infrastructure as code" — the version-controlled, auditable, human-reviewable practices that let AI make changes safely. Only 13% describe their endpoint management as fully autonomous, six in ten still lack complete visibility across their device fleet, and the average enterprise now runs 14 AI applications while IT can see into just four of them — with 78% of employees using personal AI tools at work regardless. Fleet's CIO frames the risk bluntly: "you can turn an AI agent loose, but you probably shouldn't" without the same version-controlled guardrails that made AI coding assistants safe for software teams. It's a concrete, numbers-backed instance of the "adoption is outrunning governance" pattern that keeps recurring in enterprise AI coverage this year.

Sources: Cybersecurity Insiders, PR Newswire

4. Woori Bank bets its second-half turnaround on AI agents across corporate finance and sales

South Korea's Woori Bank is centering its second-half 2026 strategy on AI-driven productivity, with CEO Jeong Jin-wan vowing to "dramatically narrow the gap" with rivals after a 58% quarter-on-quarter jump in Q2 net profit to 842.1 billion won. The bank is deploying AI agents across 29 core operations spanning corporate lending, wealth management, customer service, internal controls and workflow automation, while reorganizing sales territories using public data to sharpen personalized marketing. Woori has already hit 82.5% of its annual "productive finance" lending target of 21.8 trillion won (about $14.7 billion) in the first half, crediting AI-assisted underwriting and risk management for the pace. It's a concrete example of a large financial institution treating agentic AI as core revenue-generating infrastructure rather than just a back-office efficiency play.

Sources: BigGo Finance, The Korea Herald, Seoul Economic Daily

5. OpenAI launches Presence, a white-glove product for deploying enterprise AI agents with guardrails

OpenAI introduced Presence, a deployment platform (not a model) that packages the policies, standard operating procedures, guardrails, approved actions, simulations and evaluation tooling enterprises need to run voice and chat agents safely in production for customer support, sales development, procurement, IT and HR. Deployments are scoped individually to each customer by OpenAI's own Forward Deployed Engineers or select systems integrators, with no self-serve tier and no public pricing — an unusually hands-on, consulting-style go-to-market for OpenAI. The company says it's using Presence internally for its own English-language phone support line, where it now resolves roughly 75% of inbound issues without human intervention, matching or beating the quality bar set for human agents within weeks of launch. Early enterprise adopters include BBVA Mexico, SoftBank Corp. and IAG's Retail Insurance Australia unit, signaling this is aimed at large enterprises willing to pay for a managed rollout rather than build agent infrastructure themselves.

Sources: OpenAI, VentureBeat, Help Net Security
