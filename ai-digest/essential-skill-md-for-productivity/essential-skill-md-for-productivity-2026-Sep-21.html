1. Claude Opus 5 helped researchers chain two bugs into a full takeover of OpenAI staff accounts

Security firm Hacktron used Anthropic's Claude Opus 5 to drive an agentic pentest that chained an image-processing bug in the Discourse software behind OpenAI's public help forum with a weakness in OpenAI's shared login system, ending with control of several employees' ChatGPT and Codex accounts and reach into an internal OpenAI code repository. The team says the model-driven chain got from first look to internal access in under 72 hours; OpenAI shipped a fix about 14 hours after the report and paid a $6,500 bounty on September 1. It was disclosed, authorized research — the team proved access with a harmless pull request, touched no customer data, and stopped — but the researchers flagged that because staff connect other services to ChatGPT and Codex, the same chain could in theory have reached tools like GitHub, Slack and email. For anyone building or trusting productivity skills that give an agent standing access to internal tools, it's a concrete demonstration that agentic capability now cuts both ways: the same skill-driven reasoning that automates a workflow can automate an account-takeover chain just as fast.

Sources: The Hacker News, Bellator Cyber, InfoSec Today

2. Atlassian ships custom Skills for Rovo, letting teams turn Chat prompts into reusable playbooks

Atlassian's September 17 Rovo Chat update adds custom skills alongside visible memory controls, @mentionable agents and shareable chats — the headline change for productivity teams is that repeated work can now be encoded once and reused. A user describes what they want in Chat (Atlassian's example: "create a skill that generates a sprint summary from my board"), Rovo asks clarifying questions to nail down edge cases and desired output, and the result becomes a callable skill that can be refined and updated later in Chat or in Rovo Studio, all in natural language rather than a hand-authored SKILL.md. It's a notable data point for the "who authors the skill" debate running through this topic all month: Atlassian is betting non-technical teams will build their own skill libraries conversationally rather than importing files, the same direction Notion took with its September 15 Skills library launch two days earlier.

Sources: Atlassian Blog, Atlassian Community

3. Salesforce's DarwinX takes a browser agent from 43.5% to 93% by evolving its skills, not its model

Coverage this week of Salesforce Research's DarwinX framework highlights a result directly relevant to anyone assembling a skill library: on the 1,260-task WebArena-Infinity benchmark, DarwinX lifted a browser-automation agent's real-task pass rate from 43.5% to 93.0% purely by evolving the scaffolding around a frozen model — its prompts, tools, skills and control flow — through a natural-selection-style search, without touching the underlying model's weights at all. The authors position the technique for teams building on hosted models with no fine-tuning pipeline of their own, and recommend it specifically for long-horizon tasks with a large "behavioral surface" (prompts, skills, tools, memory, control flow) where outcomes can be scored with reasonable confidence. The underlying paper first posted to arXiv in August, but this week's press pickup is what surfaces it here — the practical takeaway for a productivity skill library is that iterating on the skill layer itself can be a bigger lever than swapping models.

Sources: VentureBeat, arXiv (HTML)

4. Harness survey: enterprises are confident about their agent and skill inventories, but rarely check

Harness's "State of Agent DLC 2026" report, based on a Sapio Research survey of 700 technology professionals across five countries, finds a consistent gap between how confident enterprises feel about governing AI agents and skills and the controls actually in place to back that confidence up. 77% say they have a complete inventory of every agent, MCP server and LLM in their environment, but only 44% run active discovery tooling to verify it — and the same pattern (confidence in the mid-70s, real controls 30 to 55 points lower) repeats across testing, security, cost and rollback. On testing specifically, 74% are confident their process would catch a production-impacting failure, but only 19% have a gate that automatically blocks a bad release. For teams treating internal skill libraries as production infrastructure, it's a reminder that "we have a skill catalog" and "we can verify what's actually installed and running" are two different claims — and most organizations surveyed can only back the first one.

Sources: Harness, PR Newswire
