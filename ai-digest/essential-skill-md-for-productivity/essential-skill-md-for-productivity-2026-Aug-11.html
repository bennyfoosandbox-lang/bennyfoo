1. Google is reportedly building a paid "Skills" replacement for free Gemini Gems, with an October 20 cutoff

A code teardown by TestingCatalog found an unreleased in-app notice, sitting behind a feature flag nobody has seen live yet, pointing to Google retiring Gems — the free, sidebar-based custom-instruction feature in the Gemini web app — on October 20. There's no announced automated migration: the notice implies heavy Gems users will need to manually rebuild each one as a Skill. The catch is that Skills currently live inside Gemini Spark, require a paid Google AI Pro or Ultra subscription, work only on personal (not work/school) accounts, and stay unavailable in the EU, UK, Switzerland and Nigeria — while Gems have always been free and universally available. Google hasn't confirmed the plan publicly, so the October date could still move, but if it holds this would be the most consequential move yet by a mainstream consumer AI assistant to convert a free personalization feature into a paid, reusable-skill system, building on the Skills-for-Gemini-web/desktop rollout this digest flagged back on July 25.

Sources: TestingCatalog, Android Authority

2. ContinualSkillBench finds explicit skill libraries barely beat an agent just remembering its own context

A new benchmark tests the core premise behind most SKILL.md tooling — that agents get measurably better by accumulating a persistent skill library — by running five domains of 100 interconnected, difficulty-ordered subtasks designed to reward cross-task skill reuse. Sequential execution does improve performance, but the paper's uncomfortable headline finding is that plain in-context learning — an agent simply carrying forward its running conversation and feedback, with no explicit skill file at all — performs comparably to agents that build and maintain a persistent skill library, averaged across models and domains. That suggests a meaningful share of the gains marketed as "the agent learned a new skill" may really be ordinary context adaptation rather than genuine reusable-procedure abstraction. The authors don't dismiss skills outright: explicit skill libraries still show selective, real advantages on tasks that demand precise, repeatable procedures or exact outputs, so the paper reads as a caution against overclaiming rather than a case against the pattern.

Sources: arXiv, Hugging Face Papers, AI Weekly

3. SkillClone shows a proprietary skill's behavior can be cloned without ever touching its file

A new paper defines Behavioral Skill Reconstruction (BSR) and introduces SkillClone, an attack that rebuilds a hidden, closed-source skill's functionality purely from its outputs — sending it valid task requests and studying the responses, with no access to the underlying SKILL.md, scripts, or ability to ask the agent to reveal them. SkillClone chains public skill advertisements, structured benign probing, executable synthesis and differential validation against the real skill's answers to construct a working functional clone; tested against 30 real skills spanning rule-based, tabular, procedural and algorithmic logic, the researchers catalog six recurring reconstruction bottlenecks and measure two candidate defenses. It's a different threat model from the credential-theft and prompt-injection campaigns this digest has tracked all month — Zenity's 1.7-million-install skills.sh campaign (Aug 9) and SkillCloak's scanner evasion (Aug 2) target the person running a skill, while SkillClone targets the intellectual property inside it, a live concern as paid skill marketplaces like Agensi and Tencent's SkillPay keep growing.

Sources: arXiv, arXiv (HTML)

4. AntiSkillBench: "persona skills" leak the private details of the person they were distilled from

Persona skills — artifacts that compress someone's chat history and behavioral patterns into a portable, reusable skill so an agent can act "as" that person — are a fast-growing personalization pattern, and a new benchmark called AntiSkillBench is the first systematic attempt to measure what they leak. Built from 7,500 persona-grounded dialogue traces across 50 behaviorally rich profiles, it tests three different skill-distillation strategies against three frontier agent backbones and finds privacy risk persists across all of them, extending past obvious facts like name and employer into communication style and personality traits detailed enough to enable impersonation. The researchers also tested four candidate defenses — both active, online risk-suppression and passive, after-the-fact provenance protection — and found every one had only limited, distillation-strategy-dependent effectiveness, meaning no current fix reliably closes the leak. It's a privacy angle distinct from this month's credential-theft and IP-theft skill stories: here the exposure is baked into what a legitimately built personalization skill inherently reveals about its source, not into malicious tampering.

Sources: arXiv, arXiv (HTML)

5. Multi-round study: self-evolving skills behave like sparse, unpredictable search, not steady improvement

Self-evolving skill systems promise that agents improve over time by turning execution feedback into persistent updates to their own skill files, without retraining the underlying model — but a new controlled study across five benchmarks and three models finds that promise doesn't hold cleanly. Holding the executor, optimizer, revision procedure and validation rule fixed while varying only which feedback the optimizer sees — successes and failures together, failures only, or successes only — the researchers found validation performance and downstream robustness/transfer results often favor different feedback views, with no single strategy winning consistently across settings. Their conclusion: persistent skill self-evolution is better understood as sparse, validation-filtered search with returns that depend heavily on the specific model and benchmark, rather than the smooth "more rounds, more improvement" story the field has assumed. It's a research-side complement to Microsoft's SkillOpt, which this digest covered treating skill files like trainable weights back on July 26 — this paper suggests that even with a working self-evolution loop in place, gains are neither guaranteed nor predictable round over round.

Sources: arXiv, arXiv (HTML)
