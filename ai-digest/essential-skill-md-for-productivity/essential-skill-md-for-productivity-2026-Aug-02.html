1. ESET's H1 2026 report puts hard numbers on the malicious-skill problem

ESET's newest global threat report, widely syndicated on August 1, quantifies how far the "essential skills" curation problem has grown: researchers scanned nearly 900,000 publicly listed AI agent skills over H1 2026 and found tens of thousands flagged suspicious and thousands confirmed outright malicious. The report ties the trend to a broader wave of AI-assisted attacks, including PromptSpy — the first Android malware ESET has observed using a generative model (Gemini) to interpret UI elements and adapt its behavior across devices — plus record levels of QR-code phishing and a growing catalog of "EDR killer" tools that disable security software mid-attack. For anyone maintaining a curated "essential" skill list, the report is a reminder that the marketplaces feeding those lists are getting noisier faster than they're being cleaned up, and that vetting has to scale with catalog growth rather than be a one-time check.

Sources: BleepingComputer, GBHackers, PRSOL:CC, WeLiveSecurity (ESET, official)

2. SkillRise trains agents to write their own next skill, not just use one

A new arXiv paper posted July 29 by researchers from Zhejiang University, the National University of Singapore, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Meituan proposes SkillRise, a reinforcement-learning framework that trains a single policy to do two things at once: solve the task in front of it, and compose a written skill document that gets handed directly to the next task in the sequence. Rather than authoring SKILL.md files by hand, the agent learns which distilled instructions actually transfer and folds that judgment straight into its own policy. Across three standard agent benchmarks — ALFWorld, WebShop, and ScienceWorld — SkillRise posted the strongest Pass@1 scores of any method compared, beating the best prior baselines by 2.3 to 8.5 percentage points. It's an early but concrete signal that "essential skills" may increasingly end up machine-curated rather than hand-written, with the model itself deciding what's worth keeping for next time.

Sources: arXiv:2607.26784, Tech Times

3. JetBrains ships a repository-intelligence layer so agents stop re-reading your codebase

JetBrains began rolling out "Context" through July and into August — a semantic index of a repository that coding agents can query instead of repeatedly grepping and re-reading files from scratch every session. A standout capability is multi-repo search, letting an agent pull in relevant code from other repositories across an organization even when they aren't locally checked out. JetBrains reports benchmarks on SWE-bench and internal monorepo tasks showing up to a 68% reduction in agent turns, 59% lower latency, and 48% lower execution cost. Context integrates with Claude Code, Codex CLI, and Junie CLI from JetBrains IDEs, Air, or VS Code, and is available now in early access at no extra cost with a JetBrains AI subscription — positioning it as a complement to SKILL.md-style instruction files rather than a replacement: Context supplies the codebase knowledge, skills still supply the procedure.

Sources: JetBrains Blog, JetBrains product page, daily.dev

4. SkillCloak shows most skill scanners can be beaten with a packing trick

Researchers at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology built SkillCloak, a tool that rewrites a malicious skill so scanners see something clean while the underlying behavior stays identical — and it works. Tested across eight commercial and open-source scanners against 1,613 real malicious skills pulled from ClawHub, the heavier "self-extracting packing" technique — which hides the payload inside a directory scanners typically skip, like .git/, behind a harmless-looking decoder — evaded every scanner more than 90% of the time, and most of them more than 99%. A lighter technique simply swaps flagged bytes for look-alike characters from another alphabet or splits a command across a newline. First reported by The Hacker News in early July and not yet logged in this digest, the research reinforces a pattern seen across multiple 2026 studies: static and LLM-based skill scanners share the same blind spots — limited context windows and selective file inspection — that a determined attacker can route around cheaply.

Sources: The Hacker News, TechVipul, Mallory

5. Dynatrace adds a no-code Agent Builder so ops teams can author their own skills

On July 27, Dynatrace announced "Agent Builder," a no-code tool inside Dynatrace Intelligence that lets customers create and deploy custom AI agents for workflows unique to their environment, alongside a new Autonomous SRE Agent for incident triage and remediation. Both ship as part of a broader push described as "moving from insight to action" — autonomous agents that resolve incidents and prevent disruptions rather than just surface dashboards, while keeping the human oversight and governance controls enterprises are demanding. Agent Builder and the Autonomous SRE Agent are slated for general availability in August 2026. It's another data point in a trend already visible across SharePoint, Salesforce, and Workday this summer: enterprise software vendors are converging on "build your own reusable skill without code" as the default way employees extend an AI agent, rather than asking them to write Markdown by hand.

Sources: Dynatrace (press release), Help Net Security, Channel Post MEA
