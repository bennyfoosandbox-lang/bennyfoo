1. Districts race out AI policies days before the 2026-27 school year, split on whether to trust detection tools

Florida's Orange County Public Schools — one of the state's largest districts — approved its first-ever AI policy on July 28 after collecting 20,000 survey responses and running a town hall at every school board seat; the policy takes effect August 11, the first day of the new school year. Its most notable feature is a refusal to let teachers use AI-detection software to police student work, on the reasoning that the tools are unreliable enough that teachers must instead find other ways to judge whether a student actually did the thinking. The same week, Florida's Wakulla County School Board approved its own AI guidelines barring students from using AI in place of original thought, while K-12 Dive reports that Idaho, Maryland, Oklahoma, and Virginia have all enacted new laws this year requiring every district to adopt a state-aligned AI policy — Oklahoma's version bars AI from grading or discipline decisions and lets parents opt students out without academic penalty. Together the moves show districts converging on policy just as students return to class, but arriving at very different answers on enforcement.

Sources: My News 13 (Orlando), WCTV, K-12 Dive

2. The EU's high-risk AI rules for student assessment were due this week — then got pushed to December 2027

August 2, 2026 was set to be the date the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations kicked in for AI systems used in education — including tools used for grading, admissions screening, and monitoring academic progress, all of which the Act classifies as high-risk and subject to transparency and human-oversight requirements that mainstream tools like ChatGPT do not currently meet. Earlier this year the Council and Parliament agreed, and by late June gave final sign-off, to a Digital Omnibus package that pushes that deadline for stand-alone high-risk systems out to December 2, 2027, with embedded high-risk systems getting until August 2028. The reprieve is about enforcement timing only: the underlying transparency and oversight obligations for any EU institution using AI to evaluate students or screen applicants are unchanged, and the Act's extraterritorial reach still pulls in non-EU universities running programs accessible to EU students. For institutions that had been racing to comply by this week's original deadline, the delay buys roughly 16 more months — not an exemption.

Sources: Gibson Dunn, Travers Smith, Uniwise

3. Global survey of 45,000+ students and faculty: AI use hits 88% and 77%, but faculty confidence is falling in the US and Canada

The Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey — 27,284 student and 18,114 faculty responses across 35 countries, one of the largest datasets assembled on AI in higher education — found 88% of students now use AI in their learning and 77% of faculty use it in their teaching. The headline adoption numbers mask a widening confidence gap: only 29% of students believe their instructors are well equipped to guide them on AI use, a figure that drops to just 17% among students in the US and Canada specifically, and in that same region faculty intent to keep using AI actually declined 9 percentage points year-over-year, from 76% to 67% — the steepest drop of any region tracked. Fifty-seven percent of students say the AI guidance built into their assessments is inadequate, and just 31% of faculty feel meaningfully involved in shaping their institution's AI policy. The pattern suggests higher education's AI adoption curve and its governance curve are moving in opposite directions in exactly the markets — the US and Canada — where usage is already highest.

Sources: Digital Education Council, EdTech Innovation Hub, Winn Solutions

4. Instructure's 2026 evidence report: 90% of college students now use AI, but 45% of educators got zero training on it

Instructure surveyed 1,125 higher-ed students, K-12 educators, and parents between June 12-15 and unveiled the results at InstructureCon on July 21: 90% of higher-education students now use AI at least occasionally, alongside 68% of K-12 educators, 61% of higher-ed educators, and 73% of parents. The adoption number obscures a support gap underneath it — 45% of educators surveyed reported receiving no formal AI training at all, versus just 8% who went through comprehensive training, even as usage becomes close to universal among their students. Respondents converged on where they think AI belongs: it was widely accepted for explaining concepts, brainstorming, and finding resources, and least accepted for grading or other academic decisions. Across students, educators, and parents alike, teaching AI ethics and building AI literacy ranked as the top two things they now expect schools to do — an expectation running well ahead of what most schools have actually delivered, and a finding that echoes the Digital Education Council's global data above from an independent, US-weighted sample.

Sources: K-12 Dive, Instructure, Winn Solutions

5. Nature investigates the AI-detection tools universities are betting on — and finds most can't tell hybrid writing apart

As lawsuits over false AI-cheating accusations multiply (this digest has tracked a Yale federal case and an Indian university scandal in prior runs), Nature examined how well the detection software universities actually rely on performs. A Southern Illinois University researcher who tested 16 detection tools found only 3 performed strongly across both AI-generated and human-written text, and detectors were consistently much better at catching text from older models like GPT-3.5 than from GPT-4-class models. The bigger failure mode is "hybrid" text — a student's own writing revised or extended with AI help — where detection reliability breaks down sharply even for the tools that otherwise perform well on purely AI-generated or purely human text. The investigation lands as more institutions move away from verdict-based detection toward process-visibility tools that log how a document was written in real time, a shift this digest's coverage of Orange County's no-detector policy (above) reflects at the district level too.

Sources: Nature, School Information System, Foundation of Earth
