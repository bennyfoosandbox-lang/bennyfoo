1. DC's schools get an official line on what AI can and can't decide

The District of Columbia's Office of the State Superintendent of Education released a new AI Model Policy for staff use ahead of the 2026-27 school year, sorting AI applications into a green/yellow/red framework rather than a blanket ban or free-for-all. The policy puts student discipline decisions, teacher-performance evaluations, and IEP or Section 504 eligibility determinations firmly off-limits for AI ("red"), while allowing AI-assisted grading, coaching for educators, and drafting (not deciding) IEP language under human-review safeguards ("yellow"). It's explicitly guidance rather than a legal mandate, joining a fast-growing pile of state and district frameworks — Ohio, Florida, Idaho — that are trying to give teachers a rulebook instead of leaving each classroom to improvise. The emphasis on keeping AI out of high-stakes special-education and disciplinary calls responds directly to the kind of student-privacy and accuracy concerns that tripped up similar tools (like the Amira reading assessment) elsewhere this year.

Sources: OSSE DC (official release), Hoodline, WTOP, WINS Solutions

2. An AI teaching assistant built for 1L exams expands into 20+ law schools

Cubby, a legal-education startup whose AI model is trained on real law school exams and case briefs, announced its largest release yet: a rebuilt student platform plus a new "Cubby for Professors" product, with faculty at more than 20 law schools — including Emory, Wake Forest, UT Austin, Case Western, and Quinnipiac — now bringing it into their courses. Unlike the general-purpose chatbots that law schools have spent the past year trying to police, Cubby runs in a closed environment trained on vetted course materials and is pitched as reducing hallucination risk while giving professors control over how AI shows up in their own classroom. The expansion lands squarely into an ongoing tension in legal education, where schools like Columbia and the University of Chicago have gone opposite directions this fall — one allowing AI with guardrails, the other banning devices outright in 1L classes — making Cubby's professor-controlled model a notable middle path vendors are now selling directly into. It's also a sign that "AI teaching assistant" products are increasingly specializing by discipline rather than staying general-purpose.

Sources: GlobeNewswire, The National Law Review, Yahoo Finance, The Manila Times

3. India open-sources the language infrastructure behind its AI-in-classrooms push

Bodhan AI, a Section 8 nonprofit incubated at IIT Madras and funded by India's Union Education Ministry, released four open-weight AI models built with AI4Bharat covering speech recognition (27 languages), text-to-speech and OCR (23 languages each), and machine translation — explicitly designed to let any developer, researcher, or state education department build multilingual classroom tools on top of them. The OCR model is meant to let AI systems read textbooks, worksheets, and handwritten student answers across Indian scripts, while the translation model targets moving curriculum content between languages, both framed as part of a broader "Bharat EduAI Stack" of sovereign public-good infrastructure rather than a commercial product. Bodhan is explicit that AI-generated output stays subject to teacher review — teachers can edit, regenerate, or discard it, but the models don't make classroom decisions on their own. The release matters beyond India: as countries like Kenya and the UAE mandate AI instruction at national scale, most have lacked the underlying language-model infrastructure to serve non-English classrooms, which is exactly the gap these open-weight models are aimed at closing.

Sources: Business Standard, The Tribune, NewsBytes, India Education Diary

4. Chicago's first elected school board race turns into a referendum on AI

Roughly half of the 45 candidates running in Chicago's first fully elected school board election have signed a pledge — pushed by the nonprofit Illinois Families for Public Schools and backed by the Chicago Teachers Union — committing to a three-year moratorium on generative AI use by both students and staff, a new district Chief Privacy Officer role, tighter screen-time limits, and a ban on biometric data collection. It's a notably different route to an AI ban than the administrative decisions that produced NYC's and LAUSD's restrictions this month: here, the policy is being fought out as campaign politics before a single vote on the board itself, with board-president candidate Victor Henderson among those refusing to sign because he worries a blanket pause would leave students without the AI fluency the job market increasingly expects. The split mirrors the broader national fight over whether AI-in-schools decisions belong with administrators, elected boards, or unions, and Chicago's outcome will double as a test of how far "pause first, figure it out later" can travel as an actual electoral platform.

Sources: Chalkbeat, GovTech, Axios Chicago, Educational Freedom Institute

5. New survey: students are souring on the AI hype, not on AI itself

A Quizlet-commissioned, Stacker-distributed survey of 2,017 respondents — 1,005 students aged 14-22, 501 high school and college teachers, and 511 parents, fielded in March-April 2026 — finds that enthusiasm for AI as a category is cooling even as concrete use keeps climbing: about 85% of students report using AI for schoolwork, and 71% of teachers say AI is effective in supporting learning, but fewer than one in five students say they're very confident in their study progress. The gap the survey highlights is less about whether to use AI and more about what for: 98% of students say it matters that study tools adapt to their individual progress, with 56% calling that "extremely important," suggesting demand is shifting from generic chatbot access toward personalized tutoring features. It lands alongside separate reporting that parental unease with classroom tech is rising — 61% of educators say parents feel there's too much technology in the classroom — a backdrop against which the survey's students are asking not for less AI, but for AI that actually adapts to them.

Sources: KRDO (Stacker/Quizlet), KVIA, KQ2, LocalNews8
