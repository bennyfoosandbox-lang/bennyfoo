1. Irish lecturers' unions: AI is an "era-defining challenge" for assessment, but banning it is not the answer

The Teachers' Union of Ireland and the Irish Federation of University Teachers met at TU Dublin's Grangegorman campus on 6 November to discuss what they call the new reality on campuses. Lecturers described students increasingly using AI as a shortcut for assignments and essays, and warned of lost creativity and curiosity and of cognitive decline. Both unions said relying only on AI detection tools or prohibition is no longer enough; IFUT's Frank Jones said the priority should be rethinking assessment, not just catching misuse, and TUI's Michael Gillespie called it an "era-defining challenge". They asked for more investment in staff training, regularly revised guidelines and a seat for staff representatives in decisions on how AI is used in higher education.

Sources: RTÉ News

2. Stanford puts $10 million behind department-level AI curriculum redesign and approves a proctoring bylaw

At its 5 November meeting the Stanford Faculty Senate heard about a provost-funded $10 million initiative of departmental AI teaching grants, with proposals accepted quarterly from January, and approved a proctoring bylaw. Provost Jenny Martinez said there is plenty of appetite to experiment but that departments should map how student skills progress across a major in the age of AI, which might mean AI-free assignments in some places and discipline-specific AI use in others. Surveys cited at the meeting found students facing different AI rules from class to class, sometimes none, and less likely to seek help from instructors or teaching assistants. The AIMES hub offers faculty a library of course examples, teaching strategies and a guide to AI course policies.

Sources: Stanford Report

3. Bett survey: 80% of UK teachers use AI, but only 35% of those say it has cut their hours

Bett's "State of AI in Education 2026" report, based on 1,033 UK teachers, finds 80% now use AI for teaching tasks, mostly to create lesson plans, worksheets and other resources (76% of users). Yet only 35% of users say it has actually reduced their working hours, though 51% say it has eased workload in some way. Enthusiasm is cooling: 47% expect to use AI more over the next year, down from 68% a year earlier. Support lags use, with 41% reporting no AI training from their school and nearly half saying there is no written policy on staff AI use, while 57% of secondary teachers suspect at least one pupil of submitting unauthorised AI-assisted work.

Sources: Winssolutions, Bett report page

4. Update: OpenAI says four Australian universities are rolling out ChatGPT Edu to build "AI-native graduates"

Building on September's UNSW deal, an OpenAI case study published 5 November names four institutions: UNSW Sydney (access expanding in stages to more than 80,000 students and staff), the University of Sydney (all students from Semester 1, 2027, after a trial), La Trobe (all 3,500 staff already covered, with universal access for 45,000 students and staff planned for Q1 2027) and Adelaide University. La Trobe also plans an AI Business Studio giving over 1,000 students consulting-style experience helping small businesses adopt AI by 2028, and offers courses built around OpenAI's Codex. The piece is vendor-authored and its survey figures are OpenAI's own framing, so it reads as a market signal rather than independent evidence of learning gains.

Sources: OpenAI

5. Higher Ed Dive: AI adoption "can't just be implemented from the top", and faculty surveys show why

A 6 November Higher Ed Dive deep dive on how campuses are handling AI rollouts leans on survey data showing a faculty-leadership gap. In an AAC&U and Elon University poll of over 1,000 instructors, about three-quarters said cheating spiked once generative AI spread, 95% worry about student overreliance, and 68% said their institutions had not prepared educators to use it for teaching, while just 13% had adopted AI literacy as a general learning outcome. Yet 87% of faculty have written their own AI rules for assessments. A California State University system survey found 61% of faculty and staff use AI tools regularly, even as about 52% are sceptical of its benefits and about 85% worry about ethics. The article argues for shared governance and student and faculty input rather than top-down mandates.

Sources: Higher Ed Dive
