1. OpenAI launches ChatGPT for Teens, betting Study Mode can pull it back from homework-answer machine to tutor

OpenAI introduced a dedicated ChatGPT experience for 13-to-17-year-olds, combining age-appropriate content safeguards, expanded parental controls, and a "Study Mode" designed to walk teens through guiding questions and step-by-step reasoning rather than handing over finished answers. Parents can link accounts and will now be alerted to a wider set of "high-risk" conversations, though the controls stop short of letting a parent read or monitor a teen's actual chats, a limit several outlets flagged as the product's central tension. The launch lands after roughly a year of lawsuits and regulatory pressure tied to teens' chatbot use, including a wrongful-death suit referencing a teen's suicide, and comes as OpenAI's own ChatGPT Edu push into classrooms continues to expand. For a topic already crowded with district-level policy fights over chatbot access, a safety-and-study-mode redesign aimed specifically at the age group schools are trying to regulate is a significant new input into that debate.

Sources: TechCrunch, Axios, The Hill

2. New survey: 70% of teens use AI for schoolwork, but just 30% say a teacher ever taught them to use it safely

Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute surveyed 13-to-17-year-olds and found 70% now use AI tools for schoolwork, with a majority of those admitting they use it to get direct answers rather than only for help understanding material — even though most also report using it for legitimate support like brainstorming or explanation. The same survey found only 30% of teens say a teacher has ever talked with them about using AI safely, more than a third don't understand their school's AI rules at all, and 28% say the rules vary sharply from one teacher to the next. Common Sense's head of research, Michael Robb, argued schools have been "bogged down" in a binary cheating-or-not framing that misses the more common, harder-to-see pattern of teens quietly outsourcing understanding rather than just getting caught. A companion CNN report tied the same research effort to a second finding — that AI-generated nude "deepfakes" remain widespread among teens while formal AI-safety instruction lags — reinforcing that student AI literacy, not just detection, is the gap schools haven't closed.

Sources: CNN, News3LV (AP)

3. Reach Capital closes its biggest fund yet — $265M to bet on AI that "expands human potential" in learning, health and work

Reach Capital, an 11-year-old San Francisco venture firm built around education technology, closed a $265 million fifth fund — its largest ever, up from $215M in 2023 and $165M in 2021 — explicitly widening its mandate from edtech alone to AI applications across learning, health, and work. The fund will write $1M-$10M checks from pre-seed through Series A into roughly 50 companies over the next three years, with limited partners including Capricorn Investment Group, the LA Fire and Police Pensions, the LEGO Foundation, and the College Board. Reach's stated thesis is investing in AI that augments rather than replaces people, a framing notable given the firm's portfolio already includes ClassDojo and Replit, and a recent exit — GPTZero, the AI-detection startup, acquired by Superhuman/Grammarly in June. A record-size fund closing "despite an edtech slowdown," as PitchBook put it, is a concrete signal that investor appetite for AI-in-education bets hasn't cooled even as districts themselves stay cautious about what to buy.

Sources: TechCrunch, PitchBook

4. "Venecky" instead of Kentucky: a Louisville middle school sends kids home with AI-hallucinated homework on the first day

Farnsley Middle School in Jefferson County Public Schools (Louisville, KY) sent students home on the first day of the school year with agenda booklets containing wildly garbled AI-generated educational content: a map of North America labeling Kentucky as "Venecky," Texas as "Taxas," North Dakota as "North Dahota," and Oklahoma as "Olkchoma," alongside a distorted periodic table and unreadable lunar-phase diagrams. Administrators later told teachers there were a "LOT of mistakes" on pages 19 through 36 and instructed students to physically tear those pages out of their planners; as of the reporting, JCPS had not confirmed whether AI was used or who produced the materials. The episode lands weeks after JCPS announced a district-wide rollout of Google's Gemini in a "secure, enterprise-level environment," making it an awkward real-world test case just as the district scales up its own AI tools. It's a small, almost comic story, but it's exactly the kind of concrete failure — AI content reaching students unreviewed — that's fueling parent skepticism in the broader debate over how much editorial oversight AI-generated classroom materials actually get.

Sources: WDRB, Fox5 (WAVE), Futurism, JCPS Insider

5. The Philippines' top law school bars AI and devices from 1L classes, saying it's already seeing the damage

The University of the Philippines College of Law adopted a policy restricting generative AI and electronic devices in its first-year JD core courses starting academic year 2026-27, citing what it called "a considerable decline in the quality of reasoning and argumentation" coinciding with students' regular AI use for case briefing, in-class queries, and research. The college also pointed to employer feedback on recent graduates who had become "not just AI-literate but AI-dependent," and argued generative tools' tendency to produce confident, oversimplified answers actively undermines the tolerance for uncertainty and critical distrust that legal training is supposed to instill. It's a notably blunt, evidence-first justification — an institution explicitly tying a classroom ban to observed skill decline rather than academic-integrity concerns alone — and it lands in the same weeks Columbia and University of Chicago's law schools staked out sharply different device-and-AI rules for their own 1Ls. Coverage of the policy is still circulating locally, with outlets this week framing it as a test case for whether other Philippine universities follow suit.

Sources: Rappler, GMA News, UP College of Law (official policy)
