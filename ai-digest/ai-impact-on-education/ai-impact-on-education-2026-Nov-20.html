1. The New York Times reports a growing anti-AI movement on US campuses, led by a student petition at Transylvania University

A New York Times report, picked up by international outlets on 19 November, describes a growing movement of US college students and professors who reject generative AI. The clearest example is Transylvania University in Kentucky. There, a student-led petition signed by about a third of the student body asks the school to drop roughly $120,000 in campus-wide ChatGPT and Google Gemini/NotebookLM licences and spend the money on human-centred teaching instead. The coverage links this to similar fights: San Diego State students protesting the Cal State system’s OpenAI contract, opposition to AI at Columbia commencement ceremonies, and a University of Kentucky staff member who won a religious accommodation not to use AI. Universities answer that graduates must be prepared to use AI well. Surveys still show most students use these tools, so this is an organised minority rather than a mass rejection. Even so, it is shifting the campus debate from how to use AI to whether institutions should be paying for it at all.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily (via NYT), The Rambler (Transylvania petition), AI Weekly (NYT summary), ThePrint (Columbia), KPBS (SDSU protest)

2. Korea’s tax-exam top scorer confesses to AI cheating, and the college entrance exam goes ahead under a ‘wearing glasses voids your test’ rule

The candidate who placed first in South Korea’s 2026 national tax accountant exam, a 34-year-old named Han, admitted in a video released through his law firm that he cheated in both rounds. He says he photographed questions with a miniature camera and sent them to a generative AI tool, and he has given a written confession to Seoul police. Separately, an IT YouTuber showed that AI glasses linked to a commercial chatbot could answer all 160 first-round questions correctly. The case turned smart glasses into a national issue in the days before the 19 November college entrance exam (CSAT). For that exam, simply wearing smart glasses counted as serious cheating, voiding that year’s scores and barring the candidate for a year. The ministry is seeking to extend the ban to three years and to refer cases to prosecutors. It also confirmed four smart-glasses cheating cases in ordinary middle and high school exams this year. No tally of CSAT-day detections had been published at the time of writing.

Sources: Korea JoongAng Daily, Korea JoongAng Daily (tax exam), TechRepublic, Al Jazeera, YTN (school-exam cases)

3. EdWeek: two-thirds of educators have had AI training, but say it skips cheating, drawbacks and hands-on practice

A new Education Week special report draws on a nationally representative survey of 852 educators. About two-thirds of teachers, principals and district leaders now say they have had at least one professional development session on using AI. In follow-up interviews, educators say the sessions are often one-off demos with no time to experiment alongside colleagues and no live feedback. Cheating, one of teachers’ most common questions, usually goes unanswered, and worries about critical thinking, jobs and environmental cost are barely discussed. Many districts neither require nor encourage attendance, so the people who turn up already lean toward AI and sceptics opt out. The report suggests that as districts pass AI policies, the bottleneck is moving from whether staff get training to whether that training covers what teachers actually struggle with.

Sources: Education Week, Education Week (teacher-prep context)

4. Update: Sarasota’s new school board adopts its AI policy 3–1 over student objections that it’s too vague

Weeks after students and parents pushed back (covered here in October), the Sarasota County School Board in Florida adopted a seven-page AI acceptable-use policy by a 3–1 vote on 17 November. It was the newly elected board’s first meeting. The lone dissenter, Beth Mayberry, and several high school students argued that the policy is vague and contradictory and doesn’t say which tasks teachers should or shouldn’t use AI for. Board members called it a work in progress meant mainly to bring the district into line with Florida’s new state rule on classroom AI, which requires parental permission for some student uses and gives districts until 1 July 2027 to adopt guidelines. Sarasota is one of many Florida districts writing policy now to meet that deadline, and the vote shows they are adopting minimum-compliance policies first and filling in the details later.

Sources: WUSF, Sarasota County Schools policy 4.24, WWSB (October pushback), WPTV (state rule)

5. Update: districts start building workarounds to Google’s AI-or-no-Search rule, from a Fairfax Chrome extension to LAUSD switching off AI Overviews

Google still says Search and AI cannot be separated for K-12 accounts. Districts are not waiting for it to change course. Fairfax County Public Schools in Virginia is testing its own Chrome extension that hides AI Overviews. Los Angeles Unified told EdSurge it has disabled AI Overviews for students. Chicago Public Schools says it is exploring every option to suppress AI Mode independently and will look at other search providers if it cannot. Education Week reports that Google’s statement promises “more choice, more controls and improved Search for learning” but doesn’t explain why Search can’t run without AI. Meanwhile, Fortune notes that AI Mode and AI Overviews still cannot be turned off officially. Workarounds like these could blunt Google’s leverage before the early-2027 switchover, but they also move maintenance and liability onto district IT teams.

Sources: EdSurge, Education Week, Fortune, Chicago Sun-Times
