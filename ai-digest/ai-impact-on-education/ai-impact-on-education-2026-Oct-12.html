1. Oklahoma's new school-AI law forces districts to notify parents, offer opt-outs and keep AI out of high-stakes calls

Oklahoma's Responsible Technology in Schools Act is now shaping how districts write their rules, and the state education department has posted an updated model board policy dated October 2026 for districts to adopt. Under the law, schools may not use AI as the primary basis for high-stakes decisions such as retention, discipline, placement or grading, and district policies must include student-data protections. Parents must be told about student-facing AI tools and can opt their children out, while the state's guidance requires educator oversight of all student-facing use. Reporting and the bill analyses disagree on whether policies are due in 2026-27 or 2027-28, so districts should check the enrolled bill.

Sources: HPPR, Oklahoma State Dept. of Education model policy (PDF)

2. Survey of 222 Washington teachers: English teachers are far more worried about AI cheating than math teachers

Three Central Washington University faculty surveyed 222 high school English and math teachers in May and published the results this month. 83.7% of English teachers voiced academic-integrity concerns versus 54.9% of math teachers, and 57.0% of English teachers said their attitude toward teaching has worsened since AI arrived, compared with 37.6% in math. English teachers were nonetheless more confident teaching responsible AI use (45.5% vs 24.4%). The authors suggest large language models' weakness at simple arithmetic may explain the gap. It is a small regional sample from the authors themselves, not a peer-reviewed study.

Sources: Washington State Standard (via News From the States), Seattle's Child, ETC Journal briefing, 4 Oct 2026

3. EdReports brief: only 1 of 10 curriculum vendors offers third-party evidence that its AI add-ons help students

A brief from the curriculum reviewer EdReports, reported by EdSurge on 29 September, finds that publishers and edtech providers are folding AI into instructional materials much faster than they are proving it works. Of the 10 vendors studied, just one supplied third-party evidence of improved student outcomes. The finding matters as districts buy AI-enabled products under new state rules requiring oversight and parent notice, and it echoes the AEFP review noting that few of the 800-plus AI-in-K-12 papers support causal claims. This item is about two weeks old, included because nothing fresher cleared dedup.

Sources: EdSurge

4. NYC students say AI is still showing up in classrooms despite the city's moratorium

Gothamist reported on 8 October that students in New York City schools say AI is still appearing in their classrooms even though the city has restricted student-facing generative AI. According to the report, students describe teachers and administrators using AI for slideshows, quizzes, posters and schoolwide email, and argue that this breaks the spirit of the policy even where it follows the letter. The account adds a student view to the ongoing enforcement debate over the district's ban for students through eighth grade. 5. International study of English-language teachers urges a rethink of AI policy, assessment and training

A global study of English-language teachers, reported on 12 October, argues that teachers need to be in the driver's seat as AI enters classrooms and calls for rethinking AI policy, assessment design and professional development. It warns that students sometimes turn to AI before attempting tasks themselves, undercutting the productive struggle that builds language skills. This fits the wider pattern in recent teacher surveys of enthusiasm outrunning training. I could only see a search-index summary of the report, so check the original for sample size and authors.
