1. Utah becomes the first state to put Google's Gemini in front of every public-school student

The Utah State Board of Education signed a statewide data-privacy agreement with Google to roll out Gemini for Education across all of Utah's public schools starting in the 2026-27 school year, reaching roughly 680,000 K-12 students and 28,000 educators. Rather than mandating use, the state is deliberately leaving the decision to individual districts and charter schools — "This is LEA choice," one state specialist said — with the board's real contribution being a single, consistent student-data-privacy contract instead of hundreds of districts negotiating their own terms with Google. Under the agreement, conversations and data entered into Gemini won't be used to train Google's models, and Google will run educator training sessions plus free AI courses for students and staff through December 2027. It's a notable model for other states watching the same problem: rather than picking winners among AI vendors, Utah standardized the privacy guardrails and let adoption decisions stay local.

Sources: KSL.com, Google (The Keyword), KUTV, Utah Policy

2. "Professor Claude?" College unions start bargaining over AI, not just policing it

At the University of Pittsburgh, faculty, staff and graduate students — the latter two negotiating a contract for the first time since unionizing — are pushing for explicit job-security, privacy and human-evaluation protections against AI in their new collective bargaining agreements, rather than leaving AI governance to administration policy alone. Contract talks that began last month for faculty and coaches across Pennsylvania's state-owned university system (PASSHE) are expected to raise the same demands. The approach echoes a K-12 precedent set by the St. Paul Federation of Educators' 2025 contract, which bars districts from eliminating union jobs as a "foreseeable result" of adopting generative AI. It marks a shift in the higher-ed AI conversation: from disputes over whether students are using AI to cheat, toward unions trying to lock in protections for the educators and staff whose jobs and evaluations AI could increasingly touch.

Sources: PublicSource, Open Campus

3. New research ties AI directly to the shrinking payoff of a college degree

A new working paper posted to SSRN finds that occupational exposure to generative AI explains roughly a third of the drop in the U.S. college wage premium, which has fallen from 0.626 in 2022 to 0.575 in 2026 — the first sustained multi-year decline in relative demand for college-educated labor in a series stretching back to 1914. A companion Census Bureau working paper, "Graduating into Disruption," tracks the effect down to individual majors: since ChatGPT's late-2022 debut, the most AI-exposed decile of college majors has seen new graduates' odds of initial employment fall five percentage points and starting quarterly earnings drop 13%, with both trends widening immediately after the AI-exposed occupations they feed into began slowing wage growth. The findings, picked up this week by Marginal Revolution, the Independent Institute and a Chalkbeat "Ideas" roundup asking whether AI is "eroding the college payoff," reframe AI's impact on education as no longer just a classroom-integrity debate — it's now showing up as a measurable dent in what a degree is worth in the labor market.

Sources: SSRN, U.S. Census Bureau, Marginal Revolution, Independent Institute, Chalkbeat

4. Teachers' real fear isn't cheating anymore — it's that AI is eroding critical thinking

A Challenge Success survey of 678 U.S. faculty and staff finds that the most widely shared concern about classroom AI, cited by 58% of respondents, is no longer academic dishonesty but the erosion of critical thinking — teachers report students aren't building the "intellectual stamina" that working through hard problems requires. Reporting built around the survey, syndicated across dozens of local outlets this week under the headline "The end of homework?", describes teachers catching students generating AI answers and then hand-copying them to disguise the source, and frames homework itself as under strain: if AI can produce a competent answer instantly, take-home work stops reliably proving anyone learned anything. The shift in emphasis — from catching AI use to worrying about what AI is doing to thinking itself even when use is permitted or undetectable — marks a more difficult, harder-to-police problem than the cheating debates that have dominated the last two years of coverage.

Sources: The 74, KVIA, Local News 8

5. UK report: schools are "bewildered" by AI and need a national plan by next September

A report co-authored by educationalist Sir Anthony Seldon and venture capitalist Tim Bunting, published with strategy consultancy Public First, warns that English pupils are racing ahead with AI on their own while schools remain unprepared, and that the system is still "preparing children for an AI-less world that no longer exists." Drawing on a Public First survey of over 4,000 people aged 11 to 24, the report — titled "Britain's Greatest Education Opportunity Ever" — calls the current rollout "chaotic and anarchic" and urges the UK government to publish a national AI framework for schools by September 2027, including a "two-lane" assessment model blending traditional exams with ongoing AI-supported evaluation of pupil understanding, dedicated teacher training, a public information campaign, and a federated pupil-data model with institution-level "data lakes." It's the most prominent UK call yet for centralized government action on classroom AI, landing in the same week that Utah and Pennsylvania's unions show state and institutional actors in the U.S. reaching for very different levers — vendor contracts and bargaining tables — to answer the same underlying problem.

Sources: BBC (via Yahoo News), Edexec, Info-Gov.uk, Public First (full report PDF)
