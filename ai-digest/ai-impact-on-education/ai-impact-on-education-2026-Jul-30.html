1. Teens from all 50 states draft the AI-in-schools policy adults couldn't agree on

With no national policy for AI use in schools, states and districts have been left to improvise their own rules — and disagree: national surveys this year find parents and teens split roughly down the middle on whether using AI for schoolwork is even ethical. On July 30, NPR reported that 98 teenagers representing all 50 states convened at the Edward M. Kennedy Institute for the United States Senate in Boston and produced a concrete set of policy recommendations, including a call for AI-literacy instruction to start as soon as students get classroom devices, disclosure rules for AI-assisted work, and tighter guardrails on chatbot access for younger grades. The project stands out because it reached consensus where legislators, districts, and parent groups largely haven't — more than two dozen states now have their own official AI-in-education guidance, and the patchwork keeps growing. It puts youth voice directly into a policy debate that has, so far, been dominated by adults arguing past each other.

Sources: NPR, WLRN

2. Federal agencies funnel hundreds of millions into university AI research capacity

Two university announcements this week show federal science agencies treating AI as research infrastructure to build, not just a tool students use in class. Ohio State University said (as of July 29) two of its research teams were selected for the Department of Energy's $293 million "Genesis Mission," which spans universities, national labs, and tech companies applying AI to more than two dozen science and technology challenges; each Ohio State team gets $500K-$750K in phase-one funding, with $6-15M available if they advance to phase two. Separately, the University of Tennessee, Knoxville secured a $20 million NSF grant (announced July 23) to build ATHENA, one of 20 new NSF-funded "AI-powered lab" hubs nationally, giving researchers remote access to autonomous, AI-run materials-science experiments alongside partners at Northwestern and Johns Hopkins. Together, the awards show how much national AI research funding is now routed through universities — reshaping "AI in education" to include research training and lab infrastructure for the next generation of scientists, not just classroom tools.

Sources: EdTech Innovation Hub (Ohio State), Ohio State News, EdTech Innovation Hub (UT Knoxville), UT Knoxville News

3. A wave of new grants is targeting the AI teacher-training gap, not just student tools

Recent surveys (Instructure, Microsoft) have repeatedly found the same split: most students and educators already use AI, but formal training for teachers badly lags, with over 40% of K-12 and higher-ed educators reporting they've received no formal AI training at all. New funding is starting to target that gap directly rather than adding more student-facing tools. On July 23, nonprofit aiEDU named the seven grantees of its 2026 Community Catalyst Program, funding rural and Indigenous-serving school districts and education nonprofits across six states with $25,000-$50,000 each over 12 months, aiming to reach more than 1,100 educators and 30,000 students. On July 29, Stevens Point Area Senior High (SPASH) in Wisconsin announced it will roll out new AI training for staff after receiving a major grant — one of several similarly-targeted local awards this summer, alongside a Pennsylvania graduate-credential AI program for teachers newly approved by that state's education department. The pattern suggests funders are shifting from "give schools AI tools" toward "train the adults who have to supervise them," the piece of adoption districts have struggled with most.

Sources: Morningstar / PR Newswire, WSAW

4. Gates Foundation-backed effort races to fund an open-source AI math tutor before its Friday deadline

Digital Promise's K-12 AI Infrastructure Program has an up to $8 million Gates Foundation-managed RFP open for teams to build an open-source AI model for K-12 math tutoring, with applications due July 31 — a deadline landing right inside this digest's window. The RFP is explicit about what's broken with today's AI tutors: they answer too quickly, talk too much, miss signs a student is losing motivation, and don't leave enough room for a student to work through a problem — the opposite of what research on effective human tutoring shows works. A single award is expected, with the 30-36 month project meant to start in November 2026, and the program is deliberately structured to require teams spanning AI engineering, K-12 classroom experience, learning science, and education research rather than an ed-tech vendor working alone. It's a notable bet that the fix for AI tutoring quality is shared, open infrastructure built with learning scientists at the table, rather than another round of proprietary vendor products.

Sources: K-12 AI Infrastructure Program (RFP), EdTech Innovation Hub

5. OECD uses AI to re-score Thailand's PISA math results at a level national exams never could

A new OECD working paper, published July 30, applies what it calls a "Collective Intelligence Model for Education" — combining AI-assisted scoring, expert review, and psychometric scaling — to Thailand's 2022 PISA mathematics data, producing diagnostic profiles across 12 content topics and 17 cognitive operators instead of a single national score. The AI-assisted analysis found relative strengths in geometry, measurement, and probability, but weaknesses in tasks requiring students to formulate real-world situations mathematically, interpret results in context, or work with functional and algebraic relationships — a level of granularity standard PISA score reporting doesn't provide. It's a concrete example of AI being applied to the research and measurement side of education rather than the classroom side: turning one aggregate assessment score into curriculum-level diagnostic evidence that education ministries can act on. If the method holds up, it offers a template other countries could apply to their own PISA data to pinpoint exactly which math skills need curriculum attention.

Sources: OECD
