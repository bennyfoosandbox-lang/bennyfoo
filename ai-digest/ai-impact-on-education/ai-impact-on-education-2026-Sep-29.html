1. College Board rewrites two AP courses around AI: chatbots embedded in CS Principles, banned from scored AP Seminar essays

Starting in 2027-28, AP Computer Science Principles will weave generative AI, machine learning and data ethics through its core units and add a new "AI Create Project" in which students build an AI-powered app, while AP Seminar students will write their scored essays in a secure environment with no access to generative AI. The changes follow surveys and focus groups with thousands of AP teachers from April 2025 to June 2026: more than 80% of CS Principles teachers said the new AI content would help students, and 68% of AP Seminar teachers rated ethical-AI instruction extremely helpful, but only 51% said the same about using AI for academic writing. Teachers also reported false positives from AI detectors and students evading digital monitoring, which is why the College Board chose controlled writing conditions over detection. The research adds that 63% of teachers at least sometimes use AI to build instructional materials while only 15% use it for grading, and roughly a quarter work in schools with no AI policy.

Sources: GovTech, College Board Newsroom

2. Stanford apologizes after its dining arm used AI to swap a Latino student for a Black woman in a promo photo

Stanford Residential & Dining Enterprises used generative AI to edit a 2024 Lunar New Year photo for promotional banners, slimming two students' faces and replacing Latino student Billy Ramirez with an AI-generated Black woman. A university spokesperson confirmed the edit and the lack of disclosure violated Stanford's own policy, which "strictly prohibits" AI to produce or alter images of Stanford people, events or facilities. The banners were removed, apologies went to the affected students, and staff will get additional AI training. The episode landed the same week campuses were being accused of double standards on AI (Dartmouth's provost row), and shows that institutional AI rules are now being tested against the institutions' own marketing and communications offices, not just against student work.

Sources: NBC News, EdSource, Stanford Daily, NBC Bay Area

3. Efekta survey: 73% of US teachers use AI, but only 40% say their school has integrated it well

A survey of 500 US teachers (plus 350 in the UK) commissioned by AI-teaching-platform maker Efekta Education and fielded June 24 – July 2 found 73% use AI in their teaching, though just 29% use it regularly and only 50% have had formal training. While 70% have received some school encouragement or approval, only 40% say their school has successfully integrated AI, and 49% call government guidance unclear. Among AI-using teachers, 72% use ChatGPT, 48% Gemini and 35% Copilot, versus 19% using education-specific tools, and 44% think current tools weren't designed with teachers in mind. Notably, 93% of teachers know or suspect students use AI for lessons or homework. Because the sponsor sells an education-specific platform, its call for purpose-built tools should be read as an interested party's framing, but the training and guidance gaps echo other 2026 surveys.

Sources: PR Newswire (Efekta Education)

4. Brandeis's "AI is prohibited unless your professor says otherwise" rule leaves most students in a fog

After Brandeis's Aug. 21 code-of-conduct update, instructors must publish an AI policy for every course, and if they don't, generative AI is prohibited; the university supplied four sample syllabus statements ranging from restrictive to permissive and warned that AI-detection software alone should not be the basis for an accusation. A small student-newspaper survey (31 respondents, so anecdotal) found only 42% correctly knew AI is barred absent written permission, 58% thought it was allowed to some degree, 77% said policies vary by course, and 58% of those who could use AI felt only slightly or not at all confident about how to cite it. Some departments, such as Journalism, have adopted shared policies and oral finals, and students asked for department- or university-wide standards. It is a compact picture of the course-by-course patchwork that many campuses now run.

Sources: The Justice (Brandeis)

5. Colleges add courses on what AI does to thinking itself — and a USF class finds students sour on AI

Forbes profiles "AI and the Future of Thinking," a University of San Francisco honors course co-taught by a theologian and a management consultant that pairs Descartes, Locke and Rousseau with questions about critical thinking and creativity. Instructors expected enthusiasm but found at least three-quarters of the students negative on AI, citing job loss, inaccuracies, peers cheating and a feeling that AI use made them "dumber". Similar philosophy-of-AI courses are running at UNC Chapel Hill, Arizona State and the University of Chicago. The story cites the Wharton "cognitive surrender" research and a Pew finding that 55% of under-30 adults are more concerned than excited about AI, suggesting the higher-ed AI conversation is shifting from tool training toward whether and how students should think with AI at all. The USF class is small and self-selecting, so it is illustrative, not representative.

Sources: Forbes
