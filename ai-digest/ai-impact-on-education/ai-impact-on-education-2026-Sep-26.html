1. Dartmouth's AI-ethics firestorm escalates: faculty and students now demand Provost Schnell's ouster

What began this week as an exposé — student journalists at The Dartmouth ran a detector called Pangram over op-eds and academic articles published by Provost Santiago Schnell in 2026 and found a median "AI-written" score of 96% — has hardened into a full-blown campus crisis. Faculty and students are now publicly calling for Schnell's removal, accusing him of violating the college's Academic Honor Principle just as Dartmouth marks its 70th anniversary as the birthplace of AI research. In a further twist, when Schnell submitted a statement to The Dartmouth defending his AI use, reporters ran that statement through the same detector and found it, too, flagged as 100% AI-written. President Sian Leah Beilock has since announced a "rigorous, objective review" of Schnell's use of AI in his published work, though she has set no timeline for the committee's findings. The episode is a pointed case study in how unsettled disclosure norms remain even among the administrators meant to set AI-ethics policy for everyone else.

Sources: The Boston Globe, The Dartmouth, The Dartmouth (opinion)

2. New York's biggest teachers union stages a statewide reckoning on classroom AI — with a chatbot-death survivor's mother as keynote

More than 500 educators, parents, lawmakers, medical experts and advocates converged on Albany on Friday for NYSUT's "Unplugged: Reclaiming Human-Centered Education" conference, the statewide teachers union's most visible push yet for limits on classroom AI and screens. Keynote speaker Jared Cooney Horvath, a neuroscientist and author of "The Digital Delusion," argued flatly that "tech will never be a learning tool," pointing to NAEP reading and math scores that fell one to two points a year following statewide digital-instruction rollouts. The conference's most sobering moment came from attorney and child-safety advocate Megan Garcia, who described how an AI companion chatbot contributed to her son's 2024 death after he had expressed suicidal thoughts to it. NYSUT President Melinda Person framed the union's stance as pro-technology-as-a-tool but opposed to designing education around the technology itself, urging attendees to start from children's needs rather than the tools. The event builds directly on NYSUT's June resolution calling for developmentally appropriate AI and screen-time limits, especially for the youngest students.

Sources: Fingerlakes1, Chalkbeat

3. Five-country study finds teachers' enthusiasm for AI and their worries about it run on separate tracks

A newly published study in Frontiers in Education surveyed 1,405 K-12 teachers across the US, India, Qatar, Colombia and the Philippines to identify what actually predicts a teacher's stance on generative AI. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory and Attribution Theory, the researchers found that AI readiness, institutional support and prior use of the tools explained roughly half the variance in teachers' positive beliefs about GenAI. But those same three factors explained only 6-7% of the variance in teachers' plagiarism and creativity concerns, which instead tracked with country-specific attributional and contextual factors. The asymmetry is the paper's central claim: a teacher's enthusiasm for AI and their worries about it appear to form along largely independent psychological tracks rather than as opposite ends of one attitude. The implication for policymakers is that pouring resources into readiness and institutional support — the current default lever — won't by itself resolve integrity and creativity concerns, which need their own, more localized interventions.

Sources: Frontiers in Education

4. Santa Barbara's new AI task force meets for the first time — after parents say they were shut out of forming it

Santa Barbara Unified's AI Task Force, convened by Superintendent Hilda Maldonado, held its first meeting of the school year on Thursday, opening a policy-writing process the district hopes to bring to the school board for adoption by November. Members split into four focus groups — academic integrity, teacher guidelines and professional learning, data privacy, and board policy — and were told to start from California's own model AI-in-education policy before deciding what to keep, cut or adapt for the district. The task force's formation has itself been a flashpoint: parents who pushed for it through a "Pencils, Not Pixels" campaign earlier this year, demanding stricter tech policies and a pause on classroom AI, say they were left out of the group the campaign helped create. Students, teachers and parents are now pressing the district for a seat at the table as the policy takes shape. The timeline puts Santa Barbara among a wave of California districts racing to finalize local AI rules this fall.

Sources: Santa Barbara News-Press, Santa Barbara Independent
