1. Australia's two biggest research universities ink major OpenAI deals days after staff struck over AI job security

In the same week, UNSW Sydney and the University of Sydney separately signed major ChatGPT Edu partnerships with OpenAI, with UNSW's deal covering more than 80,000 students, researchers and staff — OpenAI's largest education deployment anywhere in the Asia-Pacific region — alongside a new assessment framework that sorts coursework into 'can', 'can't' and 'must-use-AI' categories. The University of Sydney's own multi-year deal, which adds a $1 million university investment into an AI sustainability fund, landed just a week after roughly 2,000 USyd staff walked off the job for 24 hours in a strike led by the National Tertiary Education Union demanding enforceable protections against AI-driven job losses, workload changes, and unauthorized use of staff voice and likeness. Student groups at UNSW also said they were not consulted before the ChatGPT Edu procurement was finalized. Together the two deals and the strike make for a concentrated, real-time case study of the tension this digest has tracked all year between universities racing to deploy AI at scale and the academic workforce's fight over what that deployment means for their jobs.

Sources: unsw.edu.au, thedailyaus.com.au, itbrief.com.au, honisoit.com, honisoit.com, canberratimes.com.au, artiverse.ca

2. An FBI tip based on ChatGPT conversations led Argentine police to a teen allegedly plotting a school shooting

The FBI's legal attaché in Buenos Aires alerted Argentine Federal Police on August 19 after US monitoring flagged a 15-year-old in Quilmes discussing plans to kill former classmates in conversations with ChatGPT, including questions about what he would need to carry out an attack he was allegedly planning for 2027; police raided his home and detained him around September 10-11. Argentine juvenile-privacy law means the teen has not been publicly named. The case is a rare, concrete example of an AI provider's safety monitoring surfacing a threat before violence occurred, landing in sharp contrast to the wave of active lawsuits (including the Tumbler Ridge and Florida cases already logged in this digest) accusing OpenAI of failing to intervene when chatbot conversations turned violent. For a digest tracking AI chatbots and student safety, it's a notable counter-data-point on whether these safeguards can work as intended.

Sources: buenosairesherald.com, breitbart.com, yournews.com, yahoo.com

3. Hundreds of AI agents breached 395+ organizations in hours — and roughly half were US schools

Security researchers reported that a threat actor assessed as likely Russian-speaking deployed hundreds of orchestrated AI agents — built on OpenAI's Codex coding-agent harness paired with a DeepSeek model — to exploit two vulnerabilities in the widely used PaperCut MF/NG print-management software, compromising at least 395 organizations worldwide within hours starting August 31. Roughly half the victims were US education-sector organizations, since PaperCut is common in school and district print environments; in one case an American high school's network went from initial access to full domain-admin control in just seven minutes, and the campaign as a whole harvested credentials from 280 victims and admin privileges at a dozen. This is a distinct new front in AI's effect on education — not chatbot cheating or classroom policy, but agentic AI dramatically lowering the skill and time required to breach school networks at scale, a risk this digest hasn't previously tracked in this form.

Sources: theregister.com, bleepingcomputer.com, cybersecuritynews.com, thecyberexpress.com, gbhackers.com

4. A Utah district's two-year AI case study finds critical-thinking gains, cutting against this week's gloomier findings

Utah's Jordan School District released a two-year case study, publicized this week, analyzing nearly 14,000 logged student-AI conversations from 82 teachers using the SchoolAI platform, and found a 28% increase in measured critical-thinking skills along with more than a doubling of higher-order-reasoning responses across subjects and grade levels. The study was externally reviewed by Instructure and earned SchoolAI an ESSA Tier III evidence rating, one of only a handful of AI-education products with independently vetted evidence behind it. District officials attribute the gains to using AI deliberately to deepen student thinking and give constructive writing feedback, rather than as a time-filler or answer-generator. The finding lands squarely against the run of negative results this digest tracked just last week (OECD/PISA's daily-use score declines, Stanford's 'evidence base is thin' review), offering a concrete, named counter-example of what effective K-12 AI use might look like.

Sources: k12dive.com, efinstitute.org, schoolai.com, digitallearning.jordandistrict.org

5. Boston launches the nation's first cohort of district AI 'educator ambassadors'

Boston Public Schools and Mayor Michelle Wu formally launched the inaugural cohort of 'BPS AI Educator Ambassadors' this week — 25 secondary-school teachers who completed a five-day Summer Institute (ending September 1) on responsible classroom AI practice and will now lead AI-literacy projects at their own schools. It's the first concrete rollout under the $1 million public-private BPS AI Literacy Initiative that Mayor Wu, Superintendent Mary Skipper, tech entrepreneur Paul English and UMass Boston announced in March 2026, aimed at making Boston the first major US district where every graduating student is AI-literate. Notably, the program starts by training teachers as ambassadors and multipliers rather than mandating or restricting student-facing tools outright — a contrasting, 'teach it' model to the ban-first approach NYC and LAUSD adopted at the start of this same school year, and one of the only large-district AI-literacy programs actually rolling out (versus just being announced or debated) as of this week.

Sources: media.mit.edu, boston.gov, charlestownbridge.com, governing.com, wgbh.org
