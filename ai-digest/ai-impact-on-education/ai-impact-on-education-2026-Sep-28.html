1. Schools are experimenting with AI on students — with little evidence or policy behind it

NPR's Lee Gaines reports that districts are running AI pilots ahead of the research: Dayton Public Schools paid SchoolAI $72,600 last year (renewed at $78,650) for tools including "Jordan," an angsty social-emotional chatbot used at an alternative school, yet its own external evaluator says he can only measure perception, not outcomes. Wichita's AI specialist admits her district still has no agreed success metric, which is holding back a large student-facing rollout. Brookings' Rebecca Winthrop warns against wildly experimenting on marginalized students and would keep under-18s off general-purpose chatbots, while CRPE's Robin Lake calls the landscape "Wild West" and asks for state and federal leadership. The story also notes NYC's one-year ban on student-facing AI through 8th grade and LAUSD's ban on student AI use on district devices.

Sources: NPR, WUNC, WLRN

2. Parents say "guardrails, not bans": a new 1,528-parent poll backs AI literacy but 71% want chatbots off during the school day

A National Parents Union survey by Echelon Insights (fielded Sept 7–11, ±2.6 points) finds 85% of parents want schools to teach AI safety and 77% want students taught to use AI effectively, while 79% want all teachers trained in AI literacy and 81% want schools barred from buying tech not proven safe and effective. The same parents are wary of unfettered access: 71% support banning student chatbot use during the school day and 86% want tech firms barred from harvesting children's data. A class gap shows up too — 70% of parents with graduate degrees call AI skills essential versus 37% of those with a high-school diploma or less. NPU and Brookings' Rebecca Winthrop launch a 16-city parent listening tour on Oct 5 in Chattanooga.

Sources: National Parents Union

3. A Texas district hands 1,200 high-schoolers a free StudyFetch–Nvidia AI literacy course

Lake Worth ISD, a state-managed district near Fort Worth, will offer an optional, self-paced AI literacy course to any high-school student with parental permission, using 1,200 free licenses from a StudyFetch and Nvidia initiative that is distributing 250,000 licenses nationally. The curriculum covers prompting, research, machine learning, generative AI and agents, and stresses catching AI errors and checking claims against sources. Superintendent Ena Meyers says students shouldn't use AI to complete coursework but the tools aren't going away. Board president Tammy Thomas wants to see the contract and data-privacy terms first, and the district hadn't released the agreement at publication.

Sources: Fort Worth Report

4. UNC lands a $750,000 NSF grant to build AI tools with high-school science teachers

UNC-Chapel Hill School of Education researchers Ha Nguyen and Troy Sadler will co-develop AI tools with teachers to help plan lessons, give in-the-moment feedback and assess learning in science classes built around modeling and data analysis. Year one is design work with Missouri teachers; the next two years bring case studies in Missouri and North Carolina, reaching about 25 teachers and roughly 2,000 students. The teacher-centered design is a counterpoint to the evidence gap in the day's other coverage, aiming to produce a roadmap others can copy.

Sources: UNC School of Education

5. Turnitin's product chief: nearly one in five college submissions are 80%+ AI-written — so teach AI skills instead of just detecting

In a University Business op-ed, Turnitin chief product officer Annie Chechitelli cites the company's own analysis that nearly one in five higher-ed writing submissions contain 80% or more AI writing, and argues that stricter detection or outright bans miss the point. She says students often ask AI vague questions like "make this better," so digital fluency is not AI competency, and urges explicit per-assignment AI rules, assessments that require showing drafts and AI use, and a 'partnership over surveillance' stance. Note the source is a detection vendor pitching education-native AI tools, so the statistic and framing are self-interested.

Sources: University Business
