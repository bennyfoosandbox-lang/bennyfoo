1. LAUSD, the nation's second-largest district, bars every student from generative AI

Los Angeles Unified School District revealed at the first meeting of its new Generative AI Ad Hoc committee on September 2 that it has already switched off access to generative AI tools on all student-issued laptops and tablets — covering roughly 378,000 students across every grade level, a broader sweep than New York City's grades-2K-through-8 ban announced a day earlier. Board member Kelly Gonez, who chairs the committee, said the goal is protecting student health, wellbeing and development while the district spends the 2026-27 school year drafting a formal policy; teachers keep AI access for lesson planning and administrative work. The restriction extends screen-time and 1:1 device limits LAUSD adopted for younger students in June, and Schools Beyond Screens, the parent-and-teacher group behind that earlier push, publicly welcomed the new AI rules. With the country's two largest school systems now both restricting student AI access within 48 hours of each other, education officials elsewhere are watching closely for how far the trend spreads.

Sources: The New York Times, Bloomberg, The Washington Post, Los Angeles Times, K-12 Dive

2. NSW caps take-home HSC assessments to blunt AI-assisted cheating

New South Wales' exam authority, NESA, announced on September 1 that every HSC course will be limited to at most one take-home assessment task, worth no more than 15% of the school-based mark (7.5% of the total HSC mark), phasing in from Term 4 2026 for HSC courses and Term 1 2027 for Preliminary courses; courses built around a major project or practical exam are exempt. The change follows a direct request from Acting Premier and Education Minister Prue Car for NESA to examine ways to protect student learning as generative AI use has surged, and it pushes more assessment weight back into supervised, in-class settings rather than banning AI outright. As Australia's most populous state and home to its highest-profile senior exam, NSW's move is likely to become a reference point for other state curriculum authorities wrestling with the same take-home-integrity problem. Schools retain flexibility to choose which assessment format suits each course within the new cap.

Sources: NSW Government, ABC News Australia, Cyber Daily

3. Council of Europe tells 46 member states to build AI literacy into education

The Council of Europe's Committee of Ministers adopted a recommendation on AI and education on September 4 at a meeting in Munich, directing all 46 member states to build AI literacy into their education systems for students, teachers, parents and workers of all ages, and to help people recognize when AI use threatens human rights, democracy or the rule of law. The recommendation implements Article 20 of the Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law — the first legally binding international AI treaty — and builds on a draft AI-literacy framework the Council's Steering Committee for Education approved in March, covering the human, technological and practical dimensions of AI. Unlike the district-by-district bans spreading across the US this week, this is a continent-wide governance instrument aimed at "teach AI literacy broadly" rather than restrict access, though as a Council of Europe recommendation it isn't directly binding on national school systems the way domestic legislation would be. It signals European policymakers converging on literacy-and-rights framing as their preferred alternative to blanket restrictions.

Sources: EU News, Council of Europe

4. Canadian teachers call school AI policy a "Wild West"

A new Canadian Press report finds fewer than half of Canadian teachers in grades 6-12 say their school has any policy at all on student AI use, with just three provincial education ministries having published K-12 AI guidance so far — prompting teachers and researchers to call for coordinated federal or provincial direction instead of the current patchwork. Manitoba's education minister says her department is now drafting guiding principles, and individual boards such as West Vancouver have written their own rules from scratch in the absence of higher-level guidance, while Ottawa maintains that K-12 curriculum is constitutionally a provincial matter. The federal government's own C$2.3-billion AI strategy, announced in June, funds AI training and chatbot-safety legislation but stops short of a school-specific framework. The contrast with this week's sweeping district-level bans in New York and Los Angeles underscores how unevenly AI-in-schools governance has developed even between neighboring countries.

Sources: Yahoo News Canada, CKOM

5. University of Wyoming picks BoodleBox as its campus-wide AI platform

The University of Wyoming announced this week that it has selected BoodleBox as its official enterprise AI platform, giving faculty, staff and students secure access to more than 38 AI models through one institutional environment instead of scattered individual consumer accounts. The pitch centers on governance and cost control: BoodleBox's token-optimization layer reduces what gets sent to third-party model providers, which UW frames as protecting institutional data, with awareness and training activities starting this fall ahead of a full campus rollout targeted for spring 2027. UW positions the deal as central to a broader strategy of embedding AI across teaching, research and administration while building AI literacy campus-wide — echoing similar all-in enterprise rollouts like the University of Leicester's Microsoft Copilot deployment — on the bet that sanctioned, equitable access beats trying to police individual chatbot use. It's a small but concrete example of the "give everyone supervised access" strategy running in parallel with the K-12 restrictions dominating this week's headlines.

Sources: University of Wyoming News, Wyoming News

6. Tumbler Ridge shooting survivors file 30 new lawsuits against OpenAI

Lawyers representing survivors of the February mass shooting at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in British Columbia filed 30 new lawsuits against OpenAI on September 2, bringing the total to 37 claims against the company and Sam Altman over an attack in which a teenage shooter killed six people at the school after killing her mother and half-brother at home. The new plaintiffs — teachers, a principal, and students who were in the building but not physically shot — accuse OpenAI of aiding and abetting the massacre rather than merely failing to prevent it, alleging staff noticed the shooter's ChatGPT conversations about gun violence and attack planning and urged leadership to alert Canadian police, but were overruled. It is one of the starkest illustrations yet of the legal and safety stakes schools face as AI chatbots become embedded in students' daily lives, landing the same week two of the largest US school districts moved to restrict student AI access over safety concerns. The case will test how much liability an AI company bears for failing to escalate warning signs detected in a minor's usage — a question with direct implications for how schools and vendors handle chatbot safety monitoring going forward.

Sources: TechCrunch, CBC News, Al Jazeera, Global News, The Globe and Mail

7. San Francisco punts its AI policy vote to March 2027 as Amira controversy simmers

While New York City and Los Angeles finalized district-wide AI restrictions this week, San Francisco Unified School District confirmed it won't bring a formal AI policy to its board for a vote until March 9, 2027, with review not even starting until February. The delay has drawn fire from Tech In Check, a parent-and-educator coalition petitioning the district to disable generative AI on student devices, discontinue the AI reading tutor Amira, revert to shared computer carts for grades 3-8, and make paper homework the default through fifth grade. Amira — an AI-powered reading assessment and tutoring tool already in wide use in SFUSD classrooms — has become the flashpoint of the debate, showing how a single already-deployed edtech product can outpace a district's ability to write governing policy around it. The contrast underscores how unevenly US districts are moving: some enacting sweeping one-year moratoriums within days of each other, while others leave existing AI tools running unchecked for months longer.

Sources: Hoodline, The San Francisco Standard

8. OpenAI launches a Learning Lab to build real evidence on what AI does for learning

OpenAI Education launched the Learning Lab on September 4, a research network meant to build a rigorous evidence base for how AI actually affects learning as schools move from pilot programs into wider deployment. The initiative connects independent researchers, educators, students and builders across learning science, neuroscience, economics, sociology and policy to develop shared measures, run studies across different settings, and translate findings into product and policy changes rather than leave each pilot to generate one-off, incomparable data. Notably, OpenAI itself describes the existing research base on AI's effectiveness for learning as "early and mixed" and flags the risk that students could reach for answers without doing the cognitive work needed to build real understanding — an unusually candid admission from a vendor about the weak evidence behind its own education push. The move follows a wave of competing AI-in-education research efforts and comes as districts increasingly demand proof of efficacy before adopting AI tools at scale.

Sources: OpenAI Education Newsletter, EdTech Innovation Hub, Complete AI Training

9. Kentucky pushes to make AI fluency mandatory for every college student, not just an elective

Kentucky's Council on Postsecondary Education convened its Task Force on AI, Higher Education and the Workforce for a virtual public meeting on September 2, with officials pushing for every student in the state's public university system to get hands-on AI experience rather than treating it as a specialty elective. The University of Kentucky highlighted its new required course, TEK 100: Collaborative Intelligence — Understanding and Using Modern AI, which just enrolled what officials called the state's first "AI-native" cohort: students who were already in high school when generative AI went mainstream. Task force discussion cited a 2026 Gallup/Lumina Foundation report finding only about one in five college students use AI daily despite near-universal availability, framing mandatory coursework as a way to close that gap systemically rather than leave adoption to individual instructors. Kentucky's approach — mandating baseline AI fluency across every major — contrasts with the access-and-restriction debates dominating K-12 this week, and could become a model other state higher-ed systems look to as they weigh similar requirements.

Sources: Kentucky.com (Lexington Herald-Leader), Kentucky Council on Postsecondary Education

10. A New Jersey congressman pitches AI labs plus screen-time limits as the bipartisan middle path

New Jersey Congressman Josh Gottheimer unveiled a bipartisan "Back-to-School Agenda on AI and Screen Time in K-12 Education" on September 4, pairing new legislation to help schools build AI labs and train teachers with a parallel push for stricter classroom screen-time limits. The agenda's framing — "AI is a tool, not a replacement for critical thinking" — positions it as a middle path between the outright generative-AI bans New York City and Los Angeles adopted this week and the largely unrestricted access many other districts still allow, betting that investment in AI infrastructure and teacher training can coexist with tighter screen limits rather than requiring a straight ban-or-adopt choice. It calls on New Jersey specifically to dedicate resources to weaving AI literacy and ethics into K-12 curricula statewide, adding a federal-legislative dimension to a policy conversation that has so far played out almost entirely at the district and state level. Its bipartisan framing suggests AI-in-schools may be emerging as one of the few areas of common ground in an otherwise polarized Congress.

Sources: WRNJ Radio
