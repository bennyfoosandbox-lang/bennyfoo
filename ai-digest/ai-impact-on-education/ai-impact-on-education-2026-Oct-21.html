1. New Jersey districts write age-tiered AI rules instead of bans, and a bill would bar AI from replacing teachers

The Philadelphia Inquirer surveyed South Jersey districts and found them setting rules by grade rather than prohibiting AI outright. Haddonfield (about 2,700 students) bans AI in elementary school, teaches AI literacy from first grade, and gives sixth-graders a closed Google Gemini notebook tool with ChatGPT blocked. Cherry Hill's board approved a revised policy on Oct. 13 built on a 0-to-4 scale on which teachers state in writing how much AI an assignment allows, while Glassboro uses MagicSchool and Deptford added a high-school AI course. Assemblywoman Heather Simmons has introduced the Educators Before AI Act, which would bar New Jersey public schools from replacing teachers with AI. Experts quoted note there is no conclusive evidence yet that bans or tiers produce better outcomes.

Sources: The Philadelphia Inquirer

2. Students walk out in 31 states for an AI freeze, and the Massachusetts teachers' union president joins them

More than 45 student walkouts in 31 states, including 10 Boston-area campuses, demanded a freeze on frontier AI on Tuesday, according to organizers from Irreplaceable. At the Massachusetts State House, Massachusetts Teachers Association president Matt Bach said that “our schools are not feeding troughs for AI investors” and called “AI literacy” framing “code for more ed tech subservience.” He pointed to the union's opposition to an AI-driven classroom pilot in Springfield. The turnout at the State House itself was described as dozens of people, so the significance lies less in size than in a major teachers' union aligning publicly with student-led AI resistance.

Sources: Daily Hampshire Gazette, Boston Uncut, Techstrong.ai

3. University of Michigan finalizes six principles for AI in education, centered on human judgment

After a campus feedback process that began with a September report from its AI in Education Working Group, the University of Michigan has settled on six guiding principles. They call for responsible, human-centric AI that augments rather than replaces people, support for critical thinking alongside AI literacy, continuous improvement, ethics and transparency, and data privacy. Provost Laurie McCauley said the clearest message from the community was that human responsibility and judgment must stay at the core, and that the educational community should decide if and when AI is used. The principles set direction rather than hard rules, which leaves day-to-day course policy with instructors.

Sources: MLive, GovTech

4. San Francisco Standard: AI lands on a writing curriculum already in retreat, and teachers move essays back into class

A long San Francisco Standard report argues that AI arrived after California and the nation had already cut back on measuring writing: the NAEP last reported writing results in 2011 and none are scheduled through 2033. At Berkeley, 1,109 incoming freshmen had not met the writing standard last fall, more than five times the 2016 figure. A Lincoln High English chair now limits essays to handwritten in-class work because policing take-home AI use is impractical, which protects honesty but removes long-form practice. The piece cites a study of nearly 27,000 Chinese students in which AI use raised homework scores 18% while closed-book exam scores fell 20% within six months.

Sources: The San Francisco Standard

5. Riverhead, N.Y., adopts an AI policy that warns detectors may be biased against English learners

The Riverhead Board of Education approved an AI policy at its Oct. 14 meeting, reported on Oct. 21. Teachers must explain expectations for student use, staff need supervisor approval, and confidential student and personnel data may not be entered into generative AI systems. Students remain responsible for errors and must cite AI as teachers direct. The policy says students should not be penalized for being unable or unwilling to use AI, and it cautions that AI-detection tools may be unreliable, obsolete or biased against English language learners, so teachers who doubt authorship can ask students to demonstrate knowledge another way.

Sources: RiverheadLOCAL
