1. Reuters looks inside China’s push to make every pupil AI-literate by 2030, and finds no way yet to measure it

A Reuters investigation published on 15–16 November, based on school visits and interviews, examines how Beijing’s April 2026 “AI + Education” action plan is landing in classrooms. The plan aims for universal AI literacy among schoolchildren by 2030, but China has no standard way to measure AI literacy yet, so schools are left to experiment. About half of China’s provinces and municipalities have issued binding plans, and they are moving at very different speeds. Shanghai requires at least 30 hours of AI teaching per grade each year. Ningxia and Gansu intend to put AI literacy into entrance exams by 2027, Beijing plans 100 pilot schools, and Yunnan wants lessons in 14,000 classrooms recorded and analysed by AI. Reuters counted at least $31 million in procurement commitments over the past year and notes that studies linking heavy AI use to weaker results do not show the AI caused the drop. For anyone comparing systems, it is the clearest picture yet of a state-directed rollout running at the same time as the moratoriums now spreading through US districts.

Sources: US News / Reuters, Lufkin Daily News (Reuters), The Next Web, Global Banking & Finance Review

2. Update: three days before the Suneung, Korea puts a smart-glasses spotter in all 1,312 exam centres and refuses to delay the test

This updates our earlier coverage of South Korea’s AI-glasses crackdown with the final measures for the 19 November College Scholastic Ability Test (Suneung). The Education Ministry says at least one specially trained official will be at each of the country’s 1,312 test centres to check glasses when proctors are unsure. Smart glasses will be read out as a banned item at 8:05 a.m. in every centre, and simply wearing them will be treated as cheating. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin rejected lawmakers’ calls to postpone the exam, saying a delay would cause “even a larger disruption.” The pressure grew after a successful candidate in a state tax-accountant exam confessed to cheating with AI, the first such admission in a state-run exam. The ministry admits it has not fully solved how to detect miniature cameras, and some test-takers with thick-framed glasses worry they will be wrongly accused. For assessment designers everywhere, it shows the limits of policing a high-stakes paper exam once AI fits into an ordinary pair of glasses.

Sources: The Korea Herald, ABC News (AP), The Peninsula Qatar, Korea JoongAng Daily

3. Western’s Ivey Business School reviews a paid AI tutor that replaced a course textbook

Ivey Business School at Western University in Ontario is reviewing an AI tutor used in one course after students complained about its cost, reliability and privacy, The Canadian Press reported on 12 November. In Global Macroeconomics for Managers, the textbook was replaced this fall by a chatbot called “Ask GMM.” Students were asked to pay $72 for it on top of a $750 course-materials fee and to create accounts on an outside website. The two instructors say they are piloting the tool, that the fee covers building and running it, that any surplus will go to the HBA program, and that free access is available through the Ivey library. They did not answer questions about data security. Ivey’s associate dean for programs said the school is aware of the concerns. This is an early example of a cost problem universities will keep running into: when an AI tutor replaces a textbook, students need to know who pays for it, who checks that it is accurate and who holds their data.

Sources: CTV News London, The Canadian Press, CP24, Yahoo News Canada

4. DuckDuckGo pitches AI-free search to schools as Google’s January opt-in deadline splits districts

Google’s rule that K-12 districts must turn on its AI features or lose student access to Google Search is now pushing districts to choose sides and drawing in competitors. DuckDuckGo CEO Gabriel Weinberg has published step-by-step instructions for schools to change their default search engine. The company says it lets anyone turn off its AI chat features without losing search, and argues Google could do the same. Coverage dated 10 November puts Google’s opt-in deadline in January, ahead of rules expected to take effect in early 2027. Some San Diego County districts are going different ways. San Diego Unified is keeping generative AI blocked on its Chromebooks while it reviews the change, while Oceanside Unified plans to keep Google’s AI features turned on. No district has yet said publicly that it switched search engines over the dispute. Even so, this is the first time a rival has openly competed for districts’ default-search business because of a school AI policy. 5. Nigeria’s textbook shortage under the new curriculum is pushing teachers onto AI

Teachers in Nigeria are relying more on AI for instructional materials, research and lesson preparation because textbooks for the new national curriculum are in short supply, stakeholders told the News Agency of Nigeria in a report published on 12 November. Interviewees said generative tools are filling a gap that publishers and government supply chains have not yet closed. They also warned that teachers’ professional judgment and face-to-face classroom engagement should not be handed over to the technology. The report comes as state-backed schemes such as Naija Teacher AI, run with the Teachers Registration Council, try to reach hundreds of thousands of teachers, and as private-school associations hold “Human First” AI conferences. In much of the Global South, the report suggests, schools are adopting AI because they lack materials, not because policy told them to. That makes accuracy checks and localised content more urgent than bans. (From earlier this week.)

Sources: Gazette Nigeria (NAN), UNESCO SDG4 Knowledge Hub, EduTimes Africa
