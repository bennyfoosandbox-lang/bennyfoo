1. Massachusetts shows what "AI policy" looks like with no state mandate: a patchwork, three years running

With no binding statewide rule forcing a single approach, Massachusetts school districts are each writing their own AI policy from scratch — and landing in very different places. Boston Public Schools blocks ChatGPT outright on its network but has approved Google Gemini for teen use; Lexington tells students AI can help them write more persuasively; Medfield's superintendent said the district would rather work "in concert with staff and students" on appropriate use than lock the technology down entirely. Newton's superintendent told reporters the district is rewriting its AI guidance for the third year in a row because the technology keeps outpacing policy. Massachusetts education officials are separately weighing a proposed statewide requirement that high schoolers demonstrate responsible AI use to graduate — a step that would eventually move the state from loose guidance toward an actual mandate. The reporting is a useful snapshot of a dynamic playing out nationwide: absent federal or (in most states) binding rules, AI policy in American schools is still being written district-by-district, inconsistently, and largely in public view.

Sources: WBUR, New England News Collaborative, District Administration

2. Study: teens who lean on AI chatbots for emotional support are twice as likely to show serious emotional difficulties

A University of Ottawa-led study published in JAMA Pediatrics surveyed nearly 40,000 Ontario students in grades 4 through 12 and found the roughly one in five who had turned to an AI chatbot for help with their feelings or relationship advice were far more likely to be struggling: 57.7% of that group scored in the range for serious emotional difficulties, versus 29.2% of students who hadn't used AI that way. Even after researchers statistically adjusted for demographics, loneliness, a sense of "mattering," and academic AI use, emotional-support chatbot use was still linked to a 26% higher prevalence of clinical-level emotional difficulties, with the tendency skewing higher among older teens and among racialized and gender-diverse students. The researchers were careful to frame this as correlational, not causal — reaching for a chatbot when upset may be a sign a young person needs more human connection, they note, rather than evidence the chatbot itself is causing the distress. For schools now writing AI policy that touches counseling — several of which have piloted chatbots specifically to cover counselor shortages — the study is one of the largest data points yet in a debate that has mostly run on anecdote, and it lands as several districts continue expanding AI-driven "wellness" tools without much independent evidence either way.

Sources: CBC News, EurekAlert, Phys.org
