1. OpenAI's free "ChatGPT for Teachers" doubles down, adding 55 school systems and a 16-state privacy pact

OpenAI announced a major expansion of ChatGPT for Teachers, its managed, free workspace for verified K-12 educators, bringing in 55 additional school systems across 20 states and reaching more than 100,000 additional educators and staff — including new district partnerships with Virginia Beach City Public Schools, Kern High School District, and Hawaii's Kamehameha Schools. That brings OpenAI's total footprint to over 100 K-12 organizations across roughly 30 states and more than 300,000 educators, with free access to the tool now extended through June 2028. Alongside the expansion, OpenAI announced a data-privacy framework agreed with 16 states, giving districts a common baseline for evaluating the workspace against their student-data requirements — an attempt to defuse one of the biggest objections districts have raised about adopting a commercial chatbot at scale. The move keeps OpenAI ahead of Google and Anthropic in the land grab for classroom AI, though it lands amid unresolved skepticism from teachers' unions and privacy researchers over how much oversight districts actually have once these tools are deployed.

Sources: 9to5Mac, OpenAI, Honolulu Star-Advertiser, WHRO, BakersfieldNow

2. Florida moves to force every public college to write an AI policy — while its AG sues OpenAI over a campus shooting

Florida Education Commissioner Henry Mack approved a proposed rule requiring all 28 institutions in the Florida College System to adopt formal AI policies covering academic integrity, coursework, and grading, with the State Board of Education set to take up the rule at its September 16 meeting in Winter Haven. The measure extends Florida's K-12 AI-safety rulemaking (covered in this digest on August 11) up into public higher education, aiming for a baseline of institutional guardrails before the state's colleges are legally required to have one. The rule's release lands alongside a separate, high-profile Florida action against an AI company: Attorney General James Uthmeier's civil suit against OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman alleges ChatGPT was negligently designed and aided real-world violence, explicitly citing the April 2025 shooting at Florida State University, in which law enforcement said the alleged shooter had consulted ChatGPT on weapons and tactics. Together the two actions signal a state simultaneously trying to formalize AI's place in classrooms and to hold AI companies liable for harms tied to their products.

Sources: CBS Miami, News4JAX, WUFT, WUSF

3. MIT's AI committee tells the university to redesign courses around AI, not around banning it

MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training released its final report, the product of an eight-month charge to study how instructors and students are actually using generative AI and to propose an institutional response. Rather than recommend bans or blanket restrictions, the committee calls for "AI-aware" curricula that revisit course goals, adopt assessment formats less vulnerable to AI (oral exams, semester-long portfolios), and let departments build "AI-Intensive in the Major" courses; it also urges instructors to disclose their own AI use and centers recommendations on preserving in-person social connection alongside AI adoption. On the infrastructure side, MIT is asked to fund ongoing instructor training, AI "fellows," a pilot fund, and tracking metrics for AI use and student satisfaction, alongside guarantees of equitable technology access. As one of the most AI-forward research universities, MIT's approach — treat AI as a given and redesign around it rather than police it — is likely to be closely watched as a model by other institutions weighing the same choice this fall.

Sources: GovTech, MIT — AI and Education Report, Forbes

4. Update: NYC's AI purchasing freeze is now breaking basic school software, days before the first bell

This digest covered NYC Chancellor Kamar Samuels' July moratorium on new AI/edtech software purchases on August 9; new Chalkbeat reporting shows the freeze has hardened into a practical crisis as the city's September 10 start date approaches. Four school administrators told Chalkbeat they've been unable to renew subscriptions to electronic gradebooks, digital hall passes, and family-communication platforms — tools that have nothing to do with generative AI — because the purchasing pause has swept in routine, non-AI software along with it. Samuels had said tools needed for "mandated services or school opening needs" would be exempt, but principals say they've gotten no response to exemption requests, and the city still hasn't released the finalized AI policy the freeze was meant to buy time for. It's a concrete illustration of the operational cost of a blanket AI moratorium in a 1.1-million-student system: the caution meant to protect the district from AI risk is now threatening basic day-one functionality.

Sources: Chalkbeat New York, Gothamist, GovTech
