1. Two-year Khanmigo trial: access was nearly universal, but engagement was thin

A two-year cluster-randomized trial of Khan Academy's AI tutor Khanmigo, run across 18 Tennessee middle schools and published this week as an NBER/EdWorkingPapers study, found that while 96% of students tried the tool at least once, the median student messaged it on only about a third of practice days and in just 17% of sessions where they made a mistake — the moment coaching matters most. Many of the messages that were sent were off-topic or attempts to get Khanmigo to simply hand over the answer. Despite that thin engagement, assignment to Khan Academy with Khanmigo still raised math achievement by roughly 1.3 national percentile ranks per term, though those gains look similar to what Khan Academy's practice platform delivers without any AI tutor attached. The researchers' framing — "access was nearly universal but engagement was thin" — lands as a pointed caution for the wave of districts and states currently betting big on AI-tutor rollouts: distribution isn't the hard part, getting kids to actually use the thing is.

Sources: Chalkbeat, NBER Working Paper, EdWorkingPapers, Educational Freedom Institute

2. Update: NYC's chancellor still won't say whether there'll be an AI moratorium — with 2 weeks to go

This digest has tracked NYC's AI-purchasing standoff since early August; at the final Panel for Educational Policy meeting before the September 10 start of school, the fight moved from procurement paperwork to open political theater. Parents, educators, and advocates again demanded the two-year AI moratorium the panel has been pushing since spring, at one point performing original lyrics against classroom AI to the tune of the Village People's "YMCA." Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels said little to indicate whether NYC Public Schools would actually adopt a moratorium or release the comprehensive AI guidance officials had promised months ago, leaving the system's roughly 1.1 million students to start the year under the same purchasing freeze this digest covered yesterday, but now with no clearer sense of when — or whether — it resolves. The episode underscores how unusually contested NYC's approach has become compared with the state-mandated-policy path most other large districts are taking.

Sources: amNewYork, Yahoo News

3. A Springfield, MA middle school launches a teacher-less-adjacent AI classroom — its own union is trying to stop it

Kiley Prep, a Springfield Empowerment Zone Partnership school, this week launched a single sixth-grade pilot adapting the Alpha School model: about 25 students spend roughly two hours a day on AI-driven, self-paced coursework with a veteran teacher circulating for support, then shift to hands-on, project-based afternoons. Unlike the "no teachers" version of Alpha spreading to roughly 50 campuses nationally (covered in this digest on August 26), Springfield's adaptation deliberately keeps a certified teacher in the room during AI-heavy blocks — but the Springfield Education Association is still fighting it, filing a cease-and-desist letter arguing the program was "rushed" into a public school without adequate vetting or input from teachers, parents, and students. Union leadership specifically cites unresolved questions about the model's efficacy and how student and teacher data will be protected once an outside AI platform is embedded in daily instruction. It's a sharper, more localized version of the same fight over teacher displacement and data governance playing out nationally as Alpha-style AI classrooms expand.

Sources: WBUR, Western Mass News, Springfield Empowerment Zone Partnership

4. OpenAI's own usage data: 460 million homework messages a week, spiking every Sunday night

Timed to the back-to-school expansion of ChatGPT for Teachers covered in this digest this morning, OpenAI released a separate, privacy-preserving usage report quantifying just how much students actually lean on ChatGPT for schoolwork. In the U.S., prompts tied to classwork and homework peak above 460 million messages per week during the school year — with a measurable spike every Sunday evening — and even over summer break that figure stays above 180 million messages a week. The report also found up to 70 million conversations per week across all age groups specifically aimed at self-testing (misconception checks, extra practice problems), and on the teacher side, more than 1.9 million time-saving messages between January and mid-July, split roughly between report-card and progress-report drafting (about 900,000) and lesson planning (about 800,000). The numbers put a concrete scale on a dynamic districts have mostly had to infer: generative AI isn't a niche study aid for a subset of students, it's already a default homework channel used at a volume comparable to a mainstream edtech platform.

Sources: The University Network, Sherwood News, TheStreet

5. Catholic colleges start writing AI policy through the lens of the Pope's new encyclical

Several U.S. Catholic colleges and universities are now explicitly citing Pope Leo XIV's May 2026 encyclical Magnifica Humanitas — his first, focused on safeguarding human dignity in the age of AI — as they draft classroom AI policy for the new academic year. Institutions responding to the encyclical describe it not as a rejection of AI tools but as a mandate that classroom AI use stay accountable to human dignity, the common good, truth, and solidarity, shaping specifics like disclosure requirements, limits on AI in formation-heavy coursework, and faculty guidance on when AI use undercuts genuine learning. It's a distinct current in the broader AI-policy wave sweeping higher ed this fall: rather than starting from academic-integrity enforcement or state mandates, these institutions are grounding their rules in Catholic social doctrine, giving them a values-first rationale that's likely to diverge in emphasis from the compliance-driven policies emerging at public universities and colleges.

Sources: EWTN News, NCEA Talk
