1. NYC finally publishes the 38 ed-tech tools it barred, and vendors say the list is wrong

Chalkbeat obtained the Education Department's list of 38 products flagged under Mayor Mamdani's student-facing AI restrictions after more than a month of requests. It includes the Amira reading tutor used in over 200 schools and the city's own Microsoft-built teaching-assistant chatbot, both confirmed banned, plus Gemini and Coursemojo. Several vendors, among them HMH, Cengage, Discovery Education and Wordly, say their products use no student-facing generative AI and don't know why they were flagged. Others, such as Magma Math and All In Learning, switched off AI features within days and say they have been cleared. The department says it is reviewing products case by case and has not explained how it decided what counts as generative AI.

Sources: Chalkbeat

2. Georgetown is finalizing a Claude license, its third AI vendor deal

Georgetown told its Faculty Senate on Oct. 19 that it is in final negotiations with Anthropic over a Claude license, aiming to give some faculty access by the end of the fall semester. It would join Gemini, rolled out in March, and a ChatGPT premium pilot for faculty and staff that began this month. The CIO said terms and protections are still being worked out, and the university has not said whether students will get access. A nine-member steering committee, which includes Cal Newport, is due to publish core AI principles by the end of the semester, ahead of a university-wide framework for the board by spring 2027. Until then professors set their own class rules.

Sources: The Hoya

3. Anne Arundel parents and teachers ask the board to ban classroom AI as Maryland's policy deadline passes

Anne Arundel County Public Schools was among several Baltimore-area districts that missed this week's state-mandated deadline to adopt an AI policy. At a board meeting reviewing a first draft, parents from the Schools Beyond Screens coalition and teachers urged a ban until the effects are studied, with one art teacher saying AI has no place in the classroom. The draft requires tools to be vetted for educational value, bars AI from replacing employee duties, and leaves student-use limits to be set later. Board members resisted rushing, no AI supporters spoke, and adoption is planned for mid-December. Frederick County has already voted to ban student AI use outright, while Baltimore City, Baltimore County and Howard County have yet to introduce policies.

Sources: The Baltimore Banner

4. Australian education minister warns of a 'reckoning' over AI as a poll finds readers wary

Federal Education Minister Jason Clare said this month that a reckoning is coming for schooling in the age of AI, warning of "cognitive stunting" and comparing it to not giving kindergartners calculators; education ministers meet shortly to discuss screen-time limits. In a nine.com.au poll of 1,209 readers, 28 percent said AI should not be taught in schools at all, 45 percent opposed students using it for study, and 60 percent named plagiarism and lost critical thinking as the top worry. A UTS report cited in the piece warns that AI can short-circuit the effort needed for deep learning. New South Wales has capped take-home HSC assessments at one task per course, worth at most 7.5 percent of the total mark, and runs its own tutoring-style tool, NSWEduChat. Note that the poll is a self-selected reader survey rather than a representative sample.

Sources: Nine.com.au

5. A South Carolina first-grader's AI-aged portrait sparks a parent backlash over consent

A Charleston mother's Instagram post, now past 600,000 likes, showed an AI-generated image of her 6-year-old as a 25-year-old nurse that a specials teacher made and sent home on Oct. 16. She said the school's media consent form never mentioned AI. Charleston County School District said the principal told staff to stop similar assignments and that it will launch districtwide educator training on student privacy, approved tools and ethical use in the coming weeks. Experts quoted say there is little legislation or guidance on what teachers may put into AI models, and that teachers shouldn't enter identifiable student information. The story shows how consent forms written before generative AI are now being tested in elementary classrooms.

Sources: USA TODAY
