1. Springfield teachers’ union launches a petition for an AI moratorium after the Alpha School pilot is shelved

The Springfield Education Association, joined by parents and community members, is circulating a petition asking the School Committee to freeze any new student-facing or labor-facing AI technology until research shows its short- and long-term effects. The push follows plans to pilot a private Alpha School program at two Empowerment Zone middle schools, Kiley Prep and John F. Kennedy; that pilot is now on hold, partly because of the union’s pushback and partly because a September cyberattack shut the district’s network for a week. Union president Riley Hernandez objected that the model compresses academics into a two-hour digital block led by a non-certified “guide,” and a UMass Amherst political scientist said the available evidence is thin and skews toward wealthy private schools. This is an update to an August item on the pilot: opposition has now moved from complaint to organized policy demand.

Sources: MassLive

2. Turnitin’s proposed licence change to train AI on student submissions alarms UK and Irish universities

Turnitin has floated an end-user licence amendment letting it use student submissions, in aggregated and de-identified form, to improve or develop AI tools, which would effectively opt students in by default. Student unions argue that students’ intellectual property should belong to them, and Southampton, Westminster and Newcastle have said they will not renew their Turnitin contracts, citing the AI-data concern. Turnitin has responded that no EULA changes will take effect before September 2027 while it consults customers, and says it is talking with the university IT body UCISA; it also stresses that its models are classifiers for detecting AI-written text, not generative systems. The dispute shows integrity-detection vendors becoming a data-governance flashpoint for campuses.

Sources: The University Times (Trinity College Dublin)

3. Seven-country WISE–IIE study finds universities at sharply different stages of AI readiness

A policy brief from the World Innovation Summit for Education and the Institute of International Education, covering Colombia, Ghana, India, Kazakhstan, Qatar, Spain and the US, argues the global AI debate has been too fixated on cheating. It found wide gaps in policy and training: in Ghana, 64% of faculty at a private university reported a clear AI policy versus 36% and 27% at two public institutions, and 52% of Kazakh faculty named lack of training as the top barrier. Student sentiment diverged too, with 74% of Colombian students curious about GenAI while 76% of those in the Ghana and India case studies feared it would erode their critical thinking. Researchers recommend redesigning assessment so students critique AI output and show their reasoning, and building local AI capacity so universities in the Global South are not only consumers of foreign tools.

Sources: University World News

4. Moms for Liberty deploys AI to scan school board agendas in 13,000 districts

Moms for Liberty unveiled “M4L AWARE” at its national summit in Dallas, a system that scans school board agendas every 12 hours and alerts local chapters to items on topics such as DEI, gender policy, curriculum and Title IX, aiming to give parents about 72 hours before a vote. The group, which also runs an initiative calling for human oversight of classroom AI and says AI should never replace teachers in core instruction or grading, frames the tool as a search engine that points parents to source documents. Newsweek notes there is no independent evidence yet of how accurately the tool ranks consequential proposals, and that rival groups could build competing monitors. It is an example of AI reshaping how communities police education decisions rather than the classroom itself.

Sources: Newsweek, Fox 32 Chicago (Fox News Digital)

5. Korean schools teach pupils to catch AI hallucinations as errors creep into homework

The Chosun Daily reports that South Korean educators are turning AI’s tendency to state falsehoods into a lesson. An 11-year-old in Seoul described asking a chatbot about a Joseon-era artifact, getting a wrong answer and being confused when it contradicted his teacher. The Seoul AI Foundation has run AI ethics and usage classes since June for about 3,000 pupils in grades 3–6 across six elementary schools, and over 1,600 schools nationwide use programs where students deliberately encounter hallucinations and practise verifying facts. The article’s core message to students is that AI can make mistakes and every answer must be checked.

Sources: The Chosun Daily
