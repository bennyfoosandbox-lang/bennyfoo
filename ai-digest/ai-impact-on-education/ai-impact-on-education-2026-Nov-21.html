1. Update: Korea’s college entrance exam ends with 98 device violators, but only five caught actually using a device, one in smart glasses

South Korea’s education ministry has released its preliminary count from the 19 November College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT), the first sat under the new rule that wearing smart glasses voids your test. About 550,000 students sat the exam. Vice Minister Choi Eun-ok said 98 test-takers were flagged for electronic-device violations. Most, 93, were caught simply carrying a banned device. Only five were classed as actually using one during the test: one candidate in smart glasses in South Chungcheong Province, a 20-year-old repeat test-taker in Yongin found with a miniature camera and wireless earphones after the maths paper, one who photographed the question booklet with a phone, and two who used phones in the toilets. Several possession cases came to light when phones went off during an emergency alert, and two were caught by Wi-Fi signal monitoring. Officials have not confirmed that any of the five used AI to get answers. The total is up about 14 on last year, a modest rise given how much tighter supervision was. Final penalties are due before scores are released on 11 December, and the ministry is still pushing to stretch the exam ban for smart-device cheating from one year to three.

Sources: Korea JoongAng Daily, The Korea Times, The Korea Herald, Weekly Kyunghyang (Korean), The Standard (HK), Korea JoongAng Daily (emergency alerts)

2. University of California signs a $1.2M-a-year OpenAI deal giving every student ChatGPT Edu and Codex, over its Academic Senate’s objections

On 19 November the University of California Office of the President announced an expanded systemwide agreement with OpenAI. Students, faculty and staff on every campus will get ChatGPT Edu and Codex in the coming weeks. Reports put the cost at about $1.2 million a year under a one-year term starting 1 December. The deal includes unlimited use of ChatGPT’s base models, a 20% discount on OpenAI credits and a waiver of Codex’s $10 per-user monthly fee, plus AI-literacy and workforce training. More advanced models still cost paid credits. UC stresses that nobody is required to use the tools, that it is staying multi-vendor, and that faculty decide how AI is used in their courses. The Academic Senate’s chair, Susannah Scott, called universal access to a tool that can do the work required to earn a degree “unwise” before faculty concerns are dealt with. President James Milliken went ahead anyway, and UC says the senate was consulted. UC Irvine’s IT office posted on 20 November that it is still working out how to fit the tools into teaching. The deal arrives as Cal State faculty are pushing to limit their system’s $13M-a-year ChatGPT Edu contract to administrative work, so the country’s two largest public university systems are now fighting the same governance battle.

Sources: University of California (announcement), EdSource, Yahoo News (UC inks $1.2M deal), UC Irvine OIT, EdSource (Cal State senate context)

3. EU education ministers adopt conclusions putting AI literacy into curricula, assessment and teacher training across the bloc

Meeting in the Education, Youth, Culture and Sport Council on 19 November, EU education ministers approved Council conclusions on the skills and competences people need to engage with artificial intelligence. The text centres on AI literacy and critical thinking, including helping young people spot disinformation and online manipulation. It invites member states to build AI literacy into curricula, assessment, teacher training and adult learning. It points to the DigComp 3.0 framework and the joint EU–OECD AI literacy framework as reference tools, and to the AI Act as the legal basis for safe and ethical AI use in education. The conclusions also tie AI skills to digital sovereignty, warning against heavy reliance on AI tools built outside Europe. They build on May’s conclusions on teachers and AI, and come two weeks after the Commission’s education package proposed an EU AI Literacy Certificate for 15-year-olds, to be piloted in the 2027/28 school year. Council conclusions are not binding, but they give national ministries political cover and a shared framework as they write their own AI-in-school rules.

Sources: Lovguiden (Council meeting report), Council of the EU (draft conclusions, ST 14902/26), Poland Insight, Euronews (AI Literacy Certificate), Greek Reporter

4. Emory’s campus-wide Claude rollout draws questions about privacy and classroom oversight

Emory University has joined Anthropic’s Claude for Education programme, letting students, faculty and staff with an Emory account request a Claude licence. A secondary report says standard licences are free and premium seats cost $50 a month, paid by departments. Emory has not confirmed those terms in the sources available to this run. On 18 November the student newspaper, The Emory Wheel, reported that the community is split. Supporters welcome a secure, university-supported tool. Others question how Emory will protect privacy and oversee AI use in class, given earlier Wheel reporting that the university still has no institution-wide AI policy. Taken with UC’s OpenAI deal the same week, it shows how quickly free campus-wide chatbot licences are becoming standard, and how often they arrive before the academic-integrity and governance rules that are supposed to come with them.

Sources: The Emory Wheel, Welcome.AI (rollout terms), The Emory Wheel (policy context)
