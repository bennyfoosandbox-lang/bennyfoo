1. The University of Western Australia says it will not use AI detectors to catch cheaters

UWA Chancellor Diane Smith-Gander said in an Australian Financial Review opinion piece that the university has decided against AI detection tools. Her reasoning is that they would punish the many honest students in an increasingly futile attempt to catch the few who cheat with AI. She stressed that this does not mean condoning cheating, noting that dishonestly earned degrees existed long before AI. UWA says it will put its effort into rethinking assessment and teaching so that it measures genuine learning. It is a notable stance from a leading Australian university at a time when many institutions are still buying or debating detectors.

Sources: University World News, Australian Financial Review

2. 61% of Australian schools now have AI policies, yet many teachers still don't know what they may do

Campion Education's EdTech Adoption in Australian Schools 2026 report finds that 61% of schools have formal policies governing student use of generative AI. At CanvasCon ANZ in Sydney, Instructure's Rachel Orston and Professor Martin Bean argued that most strategies promise 'ethical and responsible' use without telling teachers whether they can draft a lesson plan, differentiate materials or prepare feedback with AI. Orston proposed treating guardrails as permissions, paired with AI literacy, so that teachers are not left waiting for direction or experimenting in secret. Student panellists added that automating note-taking can remove the learning, and that teachers who use AI without saying so erode trust.

Sources: The Educator K/12

3. As Year 12 exams start, Australian Catholic University experts warn that leaning on AI can cost marks

With NSW HSC written exams running from 13 October to 5 November and Victorian VCE exams starting on 27 October, ACU experts are urging students to use AI carefully in revision. AI tutor researcher Walayat Hussain suggests parents ask 'Can you explain this to me without it?' rather than 'Are you using AI?'. The advice leans on a PNAS study of nearly 1,000 high school maths students, where those given a standard GPT-4 scored 17% lower on an exam once access was removed than students who never had it. A version with built-in safeguards largely avoided that penalty, and students' own perceptions of their learning were overly optimistic.

Sources: The Educator K/12, PNAS study (PubMed)

4. Teachers vent on TikTok and Reddit that chatbots are making a hard job harder

Mashable's roundup of teacher posts describes a first-grade teacher in tears over a class where one of 34 students passed an exam, and r/Teachers threads about high schoolers who cannot write words and students who 'finish' work in seconds with ChatGPT and then cannot explain it. It sets the anecdotes against data: Pew found in February that 54% of US teens had used chatbots for schoolwork and one in 10 did all or most of it that way, while 2024 federal data showed 40% of fourth graders below Basic in reading. Teachers suggest moving work into class on pencil and paper, and the piece points to New York City's one-year ban on student-facing generative AI for pre-K to grade 8 as the kind of limit more systems may adopt.

Sources: Mashable (via Yahoo News), Pew Research Center

5. ASEAN teacher unions meet in Singapore and put teachers, not technology, at the centre of AI in schools

The Singapore Teachers' Union hosted the ACT +1 convention from 22 to 25 October under the theme 'Steered by Teachers; Anchored in Relationships; Propelled by AI', drawing educators from across ASEAN and South Korea. A Vietnamese presenter proposed a four-layer leadership model for AI in schools, arguing that AI can personalise learning and cut administrative work but raises problems of teacher capacity, unequal access, algorithmic bias, data security and overdependence. The conference resolution reaffirmed teachers' leading role in AI adoption, called for protecting teacher-student relationships and urged member unions to draw up national implementation plans.

Sources: VietNamNet
