1. UC Berkeley's Fields Medalist stops grading homework for correctness because AI keeps earning top scores

Richard Borcherds, who won the Fields Medal in 1998 and now teaches the graduate abstract algebra course MATH 250A at UC Berkeley, has switched to giving homework credit for completion alone after watching students use AI to collect top marks. He calls it "not a very good system" but says he has no alternative. Berkeley gives faculty no single AI rulebook beyond a syllabus statement, and the campus decided that Turnitin and Rumi detectors were not reliable enough to standardise. The case shows detector-free instructors quietly dropping assessment that AI can defeat, rather than policing it.

Sources: Hoodline, UNILAD

2. South Korea launches a nationwide check of schools for "AI glasses" cheating

South Korea's Ministry of Education is inspecting every elementary, middle and high school until the end of October to find students using or carrying AI glasses, which look like ordinary spectacles but have cameras and voice functions that can photograph test questions or relay answers. One middle school and one high school case have been confirmed so far, and both students received zero on the exam. The move follows tighter checks ahead of the College Scholastic Ability Test and earlier notices urging regional education offices to police student assessments strictly. It is a sign that wearable AI hardware is becoming an exam-security problem in its own right.

Sources: SBS News

3. Bihar enlists 11 IT firms, including Google Education, to train government school teachers in AI

Bihar's Education Department has partnered with 11 information technology companies, Google Education among them, to give government school teachers training in IT and AI, with support from the Union skill development department. A centralised training system is being built at the State Council of Educational Research and Training campus, where companies will teach batches of teachers nominated by principals. The state also plans to make computer education compulsory from class 6 next academic session and to set up smart classrooms in 700 model schools before extending the model. Education Minister Mithilesh Tiwari said AI will get distinct attention in the programme.

Sources: NDTV, DQ India, Analytics India Magazine

4. Oxford University Press: 66% of surveyed students use AI chatbots to prepare for exams, and only 3% of teachers use none

Oxford University Press's Digital Learning: Insights from the Classroom report surveyed students and teachers in South Africa, Brazil, the UK and Hong Kong, and MyBroadband highlights it as South Africa's matric finals are under way. Across the sample, 66% of learners use chatbots for exam preparation, and among teachers 82% use them for research, 74% to create resources, 66% for administration and 56% for marking. Students mostly judged revision and idea generation fair, but said copying answers defeats learning. Access is uneven: 18% of South African students lack a digital device in at least one setting, against 13% in the UK and 4% in Brazil.

Sources: MyBroadband

5. EPFL survey: over 90% of students use AI for coursework, but mostly as a learning aid

A survey by EPFL's student-delegate AI working group, answered by 359 bachelor's and master's students, found that more than 90% use AI for coursework, chiefly for scheduling study time, taking notes, practice exams and homework help. About three-quarters find it effective for explaining course material and two-thirds for weekly problems, and 85% report little or no pressure to use it. Computer science students use it more heavily while mathematics students are warier of its statistical answers. EPFL's teaching support centre has published two handbooks and its researcher Olga Kasatkina says the main worry is students outsourcing mental effort, adding that experience is still too thin to know how learning is affected.

Sources: Mirage News (EPFL release)
