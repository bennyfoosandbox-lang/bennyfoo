1. Dartmouth's AI-ethics provost is accused of using AI to ghostwrite his own columns on AI in education

Santiago Schnell, Dartmouth College's provost, spent the past year positioning himself as a leading public voice on AI in higher ed — including a Washington Post column urging colleges to redesign assessments around what students can do without AI. But an investigation by student newspaper The Dartmouth found that AI-detector Pangram flagged a median of 96% "AI-written" text across nine of his 2026 academic articles and opinion pieces, compared with 100% "human-written" scores for everything he published before ChatGPT's 2022 release. Schnell told the paper he uses AI for "language refinement, copyediting, improving clarity and organization" and takes responsibility for the final text, but Dartmouth's own policy requires disclosure of GenAI use, which the flagged pieces did not carry. The story, picked up by the Chronicle of Higher Education and prompting a student op-ed calling for his resignation, has become an awkward test case: the provost most publicly associated with rethinking assessment for the AI era appears not to have applied his own standard to himself.

Sources: The Dartmouth, The Chronicle of Higher Education, Inside Higher Ed (opinion), The Dartmouth (opinion)

2. New study: AI made college essays better and admissions worse for the applicants who used it

A working paper posted to arXiv on September 18 analyzed nearly 7,500 applications to a large U.S. public policy master's program submitted from 2020 to 2025 and found that in the most recent cycle, 56% of applicants submitted at least one essay judged likely AI-generated, despite an explicit ban on AI use. AI assistance measurably improved essay quality — especially for international applicants — but applicants whose essays were flagged as AI-written were admitted at lower rates than otherwise-similar peers who didn't use it. A companion experiment found admissions officers rated essays they believed were AI-generated lower even after quality was accounted for, and while AI narrowed writing-style gaps between higher- and lower-income applicants, admission-rate gaps for lower-income applicants widened rather than closed. The findings cut against the hope that AI would democratize access to strong application writing, suggesting instead that institutions are penalizing the applicants most likely to lean on it.

Sources: arXiv, EdAndClass, GradPilot

3. 63% of European teachers now use AI — but only 16% think it actually helps students learn

Sanoma Learning's 2026 European Teacher Survey, published September 24 and drawing on more than 20,000 teachers across 14 countries, finds classroom AI adoption has jumped to 63%, a sharp rise from prior years. Yet only 16% of teachers believe general-purpose AI tools improve learning outcomes, and between 75% and 93% of teachers, depending on country, say classroom AI tools need to be purpose-built for education rather than adapted from consumer chatbots. The gap between fast adoption and thin confidence in AI's pedagogical value echoes separate U.S. survey findings this year showing majorities of teachers using AI weekly while formal guidance and training lag behind. Sanoma frames the results as a signal to edtech vendors: usage numbers alone aren't evidence of classroom value, and teachers are explicitly asking for tools designed around teaching practice rather than repackaged general intelligence.

Sources: GlobeNewswire, Sanoma Learning

4. Seven in 10 provosts use AI weekly — almost none have an institutional AI strategy

Inside Higher Ed's 2026 provosts survey, published September 23 and based on 376 chief academic officers at mostly public and private nonprofit institutions, finds that 71% of provosts use AI at least weekly in their own work — mainly to summarize documents (71%), prepare presentations (66%), and draft communications (65%) — yet only about one in 10 institutions has a centralized AI strategy, and just one in five provosts believes their institution has a coherent vision for how AI will change teaching. Faculty and staff resistance is the most commonly cited barrier to broader adoption, named by half of respondents, followed by concerns about the effect on students (39%) and cost (29%). The survey lands alongside real financial strain: fewer than half of provosts say they have the resources to carry out their priorities, and nearly a third report near-term or active financial distress, underscoring that AI adoption in higher ed is unfolding inside institutions that are simultaneously cutting budgets.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, Inside Higher Ed (Quick Takes)

5. Digital Promise's $26M K-12 AI fund names its second cohort, pivoting to formative assessment

Digital Promise and its partners announced eight new grantees on September 21 for the second cohort of the K-12 AI Infrastructure Program, shifting focus from the first round's general tool-building toward AI-enhanced formative assessment — real-time insight into what students understand during discussions, writing and problem-solving. Named projects include a Maine Mathematics & Science Alliance/TERC effort building a multimodal dataset for AI-enhanced formative assessment, and "OpenLiteracy," an open-source speech-AI infrastructure suite aimed at early word-reading assessment. As with the first cohort, every output will be openly licensed and freely available across the education field rather than locked to a single vendor, continuing the $26 million initiative's bet that public AI infrastructure — not just more commercial edtech — is what schools actually need. It's a modest but concrete signal of where philanthropic AI-in-education money is shifting: from broad access toward the harder problem of measuring whether AI-assisted learning is working at all.

Sources: Digital Promise, K-12 AI Infrastructure Program
