1. Anthropic takes Claude for Teachers from individual educators to entire school districts

On August 28, Anthropic expanded Claude for Teachers — the free K-12 tool it launched for individual educators in July — into a full Enterprise offering that school and district leaders can roll out system-wide. Administrators verify their district, accept K-12-specific terms and a student-data-privacy agreement, then manage staff access centrally with role-based access control and single sign-on; qualifying organizations that sign up by June 30, 2027 get a full year free. Staff accounts receive the complete Enterprise product — Claude on web, desktop, and mobile plus Claude Code, Cowork, and Claude Design — layered with a K-12 teaching plugin and a "Learning Commons" connector carrying state academic standards, plus two new co-developed teaching skills: lesson preparation and a standards-aligned "check for understanding" tool (math only at launch). Anthropic is piloting the rollout this fall in Detroit Public Schools Community District to study its effect on educator wellbeing, and the release reads as a direct answer to the district-procurement contracts OpenAI and Google have both been courting with their own free-teacher offers this August. Rather than betting on individual teacher signups, Anthropic is now chasing the same centrally-managed district deals that determine which AI vendor becomes a system's default.

Sources: Claude by Anthropic, Unite.AI, The 74

2. Google's free AI Plus offer for college students jumps from 6 African countries to 27

Google has quadrupled the reach of its free student AI program in Africa, expanding eligibility for a complimentary year of Google AI Plus from six countries to 27 — covering higher-education students from Nigeria and Kenya to Rwanda, Senegal, and Zimbabwe. The subscription bundles Gemini access (including the newer "Omni" mode), double the usual usage limits, and 400GB of pooled cloud storage across Drive, Gmail, and Photos, alongside a growing set of AI study tools; eligible students verify through SheerID, and prior claimants can renew for another 12 months. The expansion lands the same week as Google's existing free-year AI Pro offer for U.S. college students (covered in this digest August 22), underlining a broader pattern of AI labs treating free, verified-student access as a land-grab tactic ahead of the fall term — this time extending that competition explicitly into African higher education rather than just the U.S. market. For a continent where the World Bank's 2026 development report (covered here August 11) flagged infrastructure gaps as the binding constraint on AI's educational upside, a subscription giveaway doesn't close the connectivity divide, but it does lower the cost barrier for students who already have reliable access.

Sources: TechMoran, CIO Africa, iAfrica

3. Update: University of Chicago goes fully analog for social-science classes, part of a widening Catholic-college AI reckoning

This digest reported August 27 that Catholic colleges were beginning to frame AI policy through Pope Leo XIV's encyclical Magnifica Humanitas; the concrete action landing this week goes further than most. The University of Chicago's Social Sciences Core is instituting a technology-free, "fully analog" classroom for its core social-science sequence starting this fall — no laptops, no AI tools — as first reported by the student paper the Chicago Maroon. A follow-up survey by EWTN News found the stance isn't isolated: Benedictine College updated its academic-misconduct policy to state AI "cannot be used in place of a student's own effort," while the University of St. Thomas in Houston, without a blanket ban, still keeps many core courses screen-free to protect students' development of their own analytical skills. Rather than writing detection-and-penalty policies like most public universities, several Catholic institutions are instead opting to redesign the classroom experience itself around sustained human attention, grounding the choice in formation and human-dignity arguments rather than academic-integrity risk alone.

Sources: EWTN News UK, Chicago Maroon

4. Update: Florida's pre-K-through-PhD AI rule gets its sharpest details yet — parental notice, privacy, academic-integrity duties

This digest covered the initial State Board of Education vote on Florida's mandatory AI-policy rule on August 27; new reporting out of the DeSantis administration this week fills in specifics that weren't yet public. The rule requires all 28 institutions in the Florida College System to adopt formal AI policies covering three areas: protecting student data privacy when AI tools are used, notifying parents of minors enrolled in college-level courses whether those courses involve AI, and addressing how AI intersects with academic-integrity enforcement. The parental-notification piece is the most novel addition — it extends beyond the typical "write a policy" mandate seen in other states (Ohio, Idaho, Maryland) into a disclosure obligation aimed specifically at dual-enrollment and early-college students, whose parents might not otherwise know their child's coursework involves AI at all. The rule is advancing on a separate track from, but the same week as, the state attorney general's lawsuit against OpenAI tied to a campus shooting — Florida is now regulating AI in education from both the classroom-policy side and the product-liability side simultaneously.

Sources: WLRN, Diverse: Issues in Higher Education, Islander News

5. Omaha-area school counselors start using AI chatbots built specifically for student mental-health triage

School counselors in the Omaha, Nebraska area have begun piloting purpose-built AI chatbots designed to help them identify causes and responses for students in mental-health distress, developed by University of Nebraska Omaha professor Thang Tran and a research team — one chatbot supports counselor decision-making directly, a second flags ethical concerns in how the first is used. The rollout responds to a structural problem this digest hasn't previously covered in depth: the American School Counselor Association recommends a 250-to-1 student-to-counselor ratio, but counselors nationally average 385 students each, leaving little time for early intervention. It's a narrower, more clinically-scoped use case than the general-purpose "AI companion" chatbots that have drawn lawsuits and safety concerns elsewhere (covered in this digest's deepfake-cyberbullying item August 11) — these tools are built to support the credentialed adult in the loop rather than replace them. Whether narrowly-scoped, counselor-facing chatbots can scale beyond a single Nebraska metro area without the safety controversies dogging student-facing companion apps is the open question the pilot is testing.

Sources: KIMT News, 3 News Now (KMTV)
