1. Hawley and Blumenthal press Google over its AI push into schools

Sens. Josh Hawley and Richard Blumenthal sent a letter to Sundar Pichai asking what Google knew about risks to children before it expanded Gemini into classrooms. The letter follows a Wall Street Journal investigation based on interviews with more than 60 teachers, parents, students and clinicians. It describes an eighth-grader who used Gemini for algebra and then failed his final exam, and a Maryland teen who came to treat the chatbot as a confidante and was hospitalized twice. The senators also cite an Economist analysis of 20,000 students in which AI users earned higher grades but scored 20% lower on exams, and an internal Google ethics report that flagged risks of humanlike AI for minors.

Sources: KTTN News

2. An AI tutoring vendor tells SUNY Geneseo students not to worry about professors' AI rules

CircleIn's chief executive emailed 224 Geneseo students on Oct. 19 saying that, because the university has a partnership with the company, they "don't have to carry all that anxiety" about assignments. Provost Mary Toale said she had not approved it, reaffirmed that each instructor decides whether AI is allowed, and ordered a clarifying follow-up and advance review of future messages. Faculty called the email an attempt to pit students against professors, while the company says its tutor gives hints rather than answers. The partnership is part of a SUNY-wide pilot that began in 2024 and requires faculty to opt out every semester, which is feeding complaints about weak faculty consultation on ed-tech buying.

Sources: The Chronicle of Higher Education

3. Andrew Ng: today's chatbots are "terrible for learning", and universities are slow to adapt

Speaking at the NDTV World Summit, Andrew Ng said student homework scores rise with AI use while closed-book exam scores fall. He cited a University of Pennsylvania and Wharton study showing a 17% drop and warned that the loss compounds over years of schooling. He framed the problem as the current generation of chatbots rather than AI itself, saying better learning-specific tools can be built. He also criticized universities for teaching years-old curricula and said it is still possible to earn a computer science degree without using an AI coding assistant or calling a frontier model's API.

Sources: NDTV

4. Colorado's Safe2Tell data shows students making explicit AI images of classmates

Safe2Tell reports from January and August 2026 each describe a student who created and shared explicit AI images of a peer. The school responses were parent contact and discipline, and Safe2Tell says no legal action followed in either case. The program does not track AI as a category, so it cannot say how widespread this is, and several Front Range districts did not answer the Denver Gazette's questions. National figures in the story include NCMEC CyberTipline reports rising from 4,700 in 2023 to 440,000 in the first half of 2025. A Common Sense Media researcher said only 13% of teens would feel comfortable raising such content with a teacher or counselor.

Sources: Denver Gazette

5. Luxembourg's secondary-teacher union calls for an AI moratorium up to age 16

The APESS union is asking for AI to be suspended in secondary classes and homework until students are 16, under the slogan "analog first, digital last". It argues the Ministry's AI compass, proposed in October 2025, is "actively fostering" the cognitive atrophy that its smartphone ban was meant to prevent. The union also wants legal protection for teachers who refuse to use AI or resist platform coercion. Its equity argument is that children from well-resourced homes build cognitive capital through books and conversation anyway, while public school is the only place others can build it.

Sources: RTL Today
