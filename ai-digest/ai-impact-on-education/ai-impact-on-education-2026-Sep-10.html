1. "We actually saved a kid": schools turn to AI chatbots as the counselor shortage bites

More than 200 U.S. schools have now deployed an AI chatbot called Kiwi to fill gaps in student mental-health coverage left by a persistent shortage of school counselors, according to reporting published September 10. The chatbot talks with students about everyday stress and struggles and is designed to flag a human counselor the moment a conversation signals real risk; at Corsicana High School in Texas, staff credit the tool with surfacing a detailed self-harm plan from a student, triggering an in-person intervention and a long-term support plan. A companion piece from the same outlet describes a broader, faster-moving trend underneath the school-deployed tools: researchers have measured a roughly 50% rise in the past year in teens turning to general-purpose AI chatbots for emotional support on their own, usually without telling a parent or counselor. Together the two stories capture the split emerging around AI and student mental health — vetted, counselor-linked tools built for crisis triage on one side, and an unsupervised, much larger wave of informal use on the other, with the safety, efficacy and oversight gaps around the second group far less understood.

Sources: EdSource, GV Wire, KIMT, EdSource (companion piece)

2. Google expands its free AI training program to all 6 million U.S. teachers, with new modules every month

Google for Education has expanded the AI Educator Series it launched for back-to-school 2026, moving from a one-time training push to an ongoing program that will add new Gemini-focused modules on the first Wednesday of every month, built in partnership with ISTE and ASCD. The free, badge-earning micro-courses — many completable in under 15 minutes — teach educators to use Gemini for Education and Gemini Notebook to automate administrative work, personalize student support and build interactive lessons, and are open to all 6 million K-12 and higher-ed teachers nationwide. Google is pairing the rollout with a live "Badge-a-thon" event on September 19, a free 10-hour virtual session offering hands-on training and official ISTE-aligned digital badges. The shift from a single seasonal push to a standing monthly cadence signals Google is betting on sustained, incremental teacher AI-literacy building rather than a one-off back-to-school campaign, at a moment when surveys keep finding large gaps between how much AI teachers use and how prepared they feel to use it well.

Sources: GCN, EdTech Innovation Hub, Techgenyz, Google Blog

3. Palo Alto father drops his $150 million AI-cheating lawsuit against the school district

Takashi Kato, who sued Palo Alto Unified in May after his son's AI-detection software flagged an English essay on "The Crucible" as largely AI-generated, has voluntarily dismissed the case, according to a September 2 court filing. The suit had sought $150 million and alleged the sophomore was forced to rewrite the essay in person — lowering his grade — and that he was discriminated against as a multilingual Asian male student, arguing the district's AI-detection process lacked adequate review or due process. The dismissal came with no settlement, no grade change and no payment from the district or its insurer; the family's attorney says the student has since transferred to a different school. It's one of a growing string of parent-vs-district AI-detection lawsuits nationally, and a rare data point on how one actually resolves: quietly, without establishing precedent on whether schools' AI-detection tools meet due-process standards.

Sources: Palo Alto Online, Palo Alto Daily Post, Paly Voice

4. UC Irvine wins $10 million federal grant to lead the first national research center on AI and college writing instruction

UC Irvine's School of Education has been awarded a five-year, $10 million grant from the U.S. Department of Education's Institute of Education Sciences — the sole award from a national competition — to establish the WRITE AI Center, the country's first dedicated research-and-development center studying generative AI's effect on postsecondary writing instruction. The center's initial work, which began September 1, includes a national study documenting how colleges are actually using generative AI to teach writing, a publicly accessible tool-comparison resource, continued development of a guided-writing platform called PapyrusAI, and a randomized controlled trial spanning six community colleges. Rather than starting from whether AI belongs in the writing classroom, WRITE AI frames its research question around what happens to learning, academic integrity, privacy, bias and student agency once it's already there — an evidence-building effort aimed squarely at the policy vacuum most colleges are currently operating in.

Sources: UC Irvine News, GovTech, EdTech Innovation Hub
