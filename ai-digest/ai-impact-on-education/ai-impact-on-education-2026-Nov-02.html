1. Audit: the meta-analyses behind 'AI improves learning' are too flawed to guide decisions

A new audit by Patrick O'Neill of Ivy Tech Community College examined 14 of nearly 40 meta-analyses that reported positive effects of AI on learning and found that all of them fail basic standards, pooling unlike populations and outcome measures such as motivation, engagement and perceived learning alongside achievement. The papers are widely cited (more than 3,000 times), and one of them was retracted a year after publication. Some of the underlying studies reportedly contain impossible statistics or likely hallucinated references. MIT's Justin Reich, Northwestern's Elizabeth Tipton and Stanford's Susanna Loeb said the field should define narrow questions first; Stanford's review of 800+ studies found only 20 with strong causal designs and none involving K-12 students. For schools buying AI tools, the takeaway is that the 'settled yes' in the literature is not settled.

Sources: Education Week, O'Neill audit (EdArXiv preprint, cited by EdWeek)

2. Two October studies, one headline: an AI math tutor helped middle schoolers, a campus AI assistant lowered college grades

Hechinger's Jill Barshay, republished by KQED, contrasts two studies from Jing Liu's Maryland center. In Arizona's Deer Valley district, middle schoolers who solved at least two problems a week on the Goblins AI tutor scored 0.11 standard deviations higher on a year-end math test, roughly three months of learning, though the design cannot prove causation and only 6% used it for 30+ minutes weekly. At the University of Maryland, a randomized trial of 30 professors found students in the 15 courses with the AI course assistant earned grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale, interacted less with course materials, and the effect was larger for first-generation students; just 15% of students used the assistant, mostly to get answers. Liu's point is that AI in education is neither all good nor all bad.

Sources: KQED MindShift / The Hechinger Report, Goblins study (EdWorkingPapers), UMD AI assistant study (EdWorkingPapers)

3. GoStudent report: 73% of UK pupils who use AI at school don't acknowledge it, and only 28% had a structured lesson

GoStudent's Future of Education Report 2026, covered by Kent Live, says about 73% of children who use AI at school admit not acknowledging its role in their work, while just 28% have received a structured lesson on using it properly. Meanwhile 44% of teachers are not confident that most of their pupils could write a full essay from scratch without AI. The company's CEO argues pupils need to be taught to use AI critically to deepen understanding rather than to do homework. This is vendor-commissioned survey data from an online tutoring company, so treat the figures as indicative, but it reinforces the gap between pupil use and instruction.

Sources: Kent Live, GoStudent Future of Education Report 2026

4. Samsung survey: 95% of US high schoolers use AI, only 16% say their school teaches it formally

Samsung's expansion of its Solve for Tomorrow program came with new research finding that 95% of high schoolers use AI while only 16% report formal AI education at school. Sixty-eight percent say their school offers no classes or instruction on AI, and 26% give their teachers a D or F on AI capability. Samsung committed to reaching 100,000 teachers over five years with free AI curriculum resources, training and classroom support, with partners including DonorsChoose, First Book and the Mark Cuban Foundation. As with other corporate surveys, the numbers come from the sponsor, but they add to evidence of a training gap.

Sources: Samsung Newsroom (US)

5. Utah's State Board creates a student AI task force so students have a say in classroom AI rules

KSL reports that after student requests for a voice, the Utah State Board of Education created an Artificial Intelligence Student Task Force to give input on how AI is used in schools. The piece profiles two high school seniors, Rahul Nair and Kennedy Mickelson, who hold differing views on AI's role, illustrating that students are not a single bloc on the question. It matters because most AI-in-schools rules are written by adults; a formal student channel at state level is still unusual. Details such as membership and timelines were not confirmed in the coverage reviewed.

Sources: KSL.com
