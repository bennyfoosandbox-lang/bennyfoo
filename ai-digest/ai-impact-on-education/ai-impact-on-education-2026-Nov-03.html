1. Google will switch off Gemini, Notebook and Search for K-12 student accounts unless schools opt in

Google announced on 3 November that, starting in early 2027, access to Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be entirely off for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn them on. Because Search now carries AI features, schools that want no AI at all would effectively lose Search for students, which critics are calling coercive. Google frames it as giving schools control after surveying administrators and teachers, and the move follows weeks of pressure over its August expansion of Gemini to younger students (including a WSJ investigation and a Hawley-Blumenthal letter). Detail on the rollout is promised to schools in the coming months, so districts that had banned or paused AI now face a real trade-off.

Sources: Google (The Keyword), The New York Times, TechCrunch, The Verge, The Guardian, AP via WRAL, Engadget

2. Google brings 'Guided Learning' to Classroom now and to Search in a pilot later this month

In the same announcement, Google said Guided Learning, which coaches students with questions and step-by-step breakdowns instead of handing over answers, is available globally in Google Classroom starting today. Teacher-assigned activities are anchored in class material and give teachers real-time signals about where a class is struggling. A Search version for student accounts, which would walk a student through a pasted physics problem or book-report request rather than answer it, begins as a pilot in select U.S. schools later in November before wider expansion. Google cites its own research claiming measurable learning gains, which has not been independently verified; the design bet is that 'productive struggle' can answer the cheating-versus-learning worry.

Sources: Google (The Keyword), Engadget, Forbes

3. Update: parents want AI taught with safeguards, but 43% of teachers report no AI training at all

EdSurge pairs the National Parents Union poll covered earlier (77% want schools to teach effective AI use, 81% want to bar purchases of tech not proven safe and effective) with new teacher data from Educators for Excellence's 2026 survey. Fewer than a quarter of teachers say they have had adequate training to help students use AI well and 43% report none, even though over three-quarters use AI in at least one way in their own practice. The piece sets this against bans in New York City (pre-K to 8) and Los Angeles (K-12) and quotes the poll's sponsor calling for 'a scalpel, not a sledgehammer'. The training gap is the practical constraint behind nearly every district policy debate.

Sources: EdSurge, National Parents Union

4. San Antonio districts approve AI tools for teachers but leave classroom use optional, raising an equity worry

The San Antonio Express-News reports that Northside ISD has approved 10 AI tools for educators and will roll out ChatGPT for Teachers in coming weeks, while North East ISD permits tools such as Gemini and Copilot. District contracts keep student data out of model training, and Northside's handbook bars feeding identifiable student information into AI for special-education plans or using it to determine grades. Teachers are not required to use AI, so technology leaders worry students of non-adopting teachers will miss instruction in a 95,000-student district; Northside's technology director calls it a 'conundrum' of standardizing AI education without forcing adoption. A UTSA researcher cautions against using AI for grading, cheating detection or predicting performance.

Sources: San Antonio Express-News

5. University of Sydney logged almost 3,000 alleged misconduct cases last year as AI wearables expose an exam 'blind spot'

The Australian reports that the University of Sydney recorded almost 3,000 alleged academic misconduct cases last year, and that experts see AI-powered wearables such as smart glasses as a 'massive blindspot' in exam security. The full article is paywalled, so only the headline figures from the publisher's summary are used here. It extends this week's thread on smart glasses in exams, following the Korean inspections and European data-protection debate, and suggests universities may need supervised or oral formats rather than relying on device bans alone.

Sources: The Australian
