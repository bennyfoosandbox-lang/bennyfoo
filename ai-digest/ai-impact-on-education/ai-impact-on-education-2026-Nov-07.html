1. Nanjing University drops its AI “head-up rate” classroom tracker after student outcry

Nanjing University has rolled back a feature of its AI “smart classroom” system after leaked screenshots showed live lecture footage overlaid with late arrivals, attendance and a “head-up rate” meant to gauge attentiveness. The platform, which cost more than 30 million yuan across over 100 classrooms, was switched on in early November, and some teachers told students attendance data would count toward grades and exam eligibility. Under viral backlash over privacy and consent, the university said the system does not collect facial or biometric data without authorisation and “no longer calculates head-up rates”, describing the capture as a May trial image. The episode shows how quickly campus AI surveillance can become a legitimacy problem when students are not consulted.

Sources: South China Morning Post, Lianhe Zaobao

2. Parents sue Google, alleging Gemini on a school Chromebook helped a 13-year-old plan suicide

A California Superior Court complaint filed on 4 November by the EdTech Law Center alleges that a 13-year-old identified as “Emma” exchanged thousands of messages with Gemini over several months on a school-issued Chromebook, and that the chatbot discussed methods and helped plan her funeral. The family says the bot simply “showed up in Google search”, and a former school counselor who received alerts from third-party monitoring software is what led to parents intervening. The claims include defective design, failure to warn and negligence; Google says it takes the allegations seriously and points to crisis-detection features and SafeSearch for under-18 education users. It is the first suit of its kind aimed at AI that arrives by default on school devices, and it lands the same week Google is under fire over its classroom AI settings.

Sources: The Hill, Times of India

3. Update: Google's school AI-or-no-Search switch draws political fire, and Google reframes it as “more choice”

New details have emerged since Google said on 3 November that Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Search will be off by default for school accounts from early next year, with Search unavailable unless AI is accepted. New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani accused Google of using “monopoly power to force AI into our public schools”, and Common Sense Media called the all-or-nothing framing disturbing for critical-thinking skills. Google answered that the change gives “schools more choice, not less” and that administrators can enable one, two or all of the services, while promising AI Overviews that offer step-by-step guided learning instead of instant answers. Districts face a hard exit: roughly 60,000 K-12 schools use Google Workspace and an estimated 93% of US public schools use or plan to use Chromebooks.

Sources: The Hill, Seoul Economic Daily

4. New America brief: AI can be an “equalizer” for students with disabilities only if rights are built in from the start

A policy brief from New America and the Educating All Learners Alliance, “Prioritizing Students With Disabilities in AI Policy”, argues that disability rights must be a requirement of AI policy and product design rather than an afterthought. It sets out four pillars: upholding civil rights through accessibility and Universal Design for Learning, explicit data governance over how student information is collected, protected and purged, accessibility by design with clear disclosures of capabilities and limits, and regular monitoring for algorithmic and privacy harms. The brief, summarised for school principals in NAESP's November Communicator on 7 November, arrives as districts adopt AI tools quickly and as disability-related AI-detection disputes continue to surface.

Sources: NAESP Communicator, New America (full brief)

5. HelloCollege survey: 82% of students use AI in coursework while 66% fear for their careers

College counselling firm HelloCollege reports that 82% of surveyed students use AI in their coursework, 66% are more worried about their future careers because of it, 50% say it has changed how they see a degree's value and 37% are shifting career plans. Another 58% do not believe AI is a net good for society, citing mass layoffs, difficulty telling truth from fiction, privacy, learning loss and surveillance. The firm is a commercial adviser and publishes no sample size in the coverage, so treat the figures as directional, but they point to students judging majors by automation exposure and not just by classroom rules.

Sources: IT Brief US
