1. Malaysia has trained 329,414 teachers in AI ahead of a 2027 curriculum rollout

Education Minister Fadhlina Sidek told the Dewan Rakyat on 29 October that 329,414 teachers have now received AI training and that about 1.5 million students have been exposed to AI through STEM, coding, robotics and digital-innovation programmes. The ministry will bring AI literacy and fundamentals into the school curriculum next year, including primary schools, with 27 AI-powered pilot classrooms already running. The rollout rests on AI Literacy Guidelines in force since 8 May that stress safe, critical and ethical use. It is one of the clearer examples of a national system training teachers at scale before changing what students are taught.

Sources: The Star, Business Today Malaysia, Sinar Daily

2. Princeton's campus-wide ChatGPT pilot splits students and faculty

Princeton will give every student, faculty member and staff member access to ChatGPT Plus or Pro under a one-year pilot, with student access starting in January before the spring semester. A university spokesperson said the OpenAI contract adds security and privacy protections and does not amount to an endorsement of any particular use. Supporters cite equity, lower costs and the chance to learn to use AI well, with nearly 90% of surveyed first-years already using an AI model. Critics, including history professor Kevin Kruse, argue the rush to embrace generative AI undermines humanities teaching, writing skills and the environment, and some students say it sits awkwardly beside strict anti-AI course policies.

Sources: The Daily Princetonian (news), The Daily Princetonian (opinion)

3. Long Island districts buy AI platforms fast, but many teachers barely use them

Long Island Press reports that AI teaching platforms are spreading across the region's 125 districts: 38 have bought Magic School AI (about 41% of Nassau County), and Brisk is used in more than 1,000 districts nationally. Actual use lags purchases, though. In Jericho only 223 of 808 teachers used Brisk last year and just 115 used it more than ten times, and in Wantagh 54 of 155 did. Levittown spends $39,000 on Magic School AI and Syosset licensed Gemini for $31,000. Concerns include lost productive struggle, student data privacy, screen time and union opposition to chatbots replacing human instruction for foundational skills.

Sources: Long Island Press

4. Loudoun County will pilot Gemini Notebook in eight schools in November

Loudoun County Public Schools in Virginia announced on 28 October that eight schools will trial Gemini Notebook, the tool formerly known as NotebookLM, starting in November. Students will use it on teacher-supplied or their own course materials to generate study questions, quizzes, glossaries and audio overviews. The division is using managed Google Workspace for Education accounts rather than personal ones, says student content will not train Google's public models, and asks teachers to coach source-checking and independent thinking. It has not named the schools, grades or an exact start date, so the real test will be how well the guardrails hold in classrooms.

Sources: OpenTools, LCPS announcement

5. GW professors: AI can short-circuit learning, so teach the process rather than the product

At a panel moderated by Dean Paul Wahlbeck, four George Washington University arts and sciences professors weighed AI's role in higher education. Psychologist Dwight Kravitz warned that people learn by doing and AI can short-circuit that, while urging universities to neither fully embrace nor reject it because effects vary by discipline. Political scientist Sarah Binder uses AI to analyse thousands of congressional speeches but flagged validity and reliability problems from hallucinations, and philosopher Tad Zawidzki argued educators should focus on process over product. The discussion reflects a growing faculty consensus that assessment must reward visible thinking.

Sources: The GW Hatchet
