1. NYC unveils its long-awaited AI policy: a ban through 8th grade, restricted access in high school

New York City's Department of Education, under Mayor Zohran Mamdani, released the nation's largest school system's formal AI policy on September 2, capping months of delay after a March draft triggered fierce parent backlash. The rules impose a one-year "pause" on student-facing generative AI — including chatbots and AI tutors — for roughly 600,000 students from pre-K through 8th grade, and add device time limits: no individual devices for pre-K through 2nd grade, a 30-minute daily cap for grades 3-5, and 45 minutes for middle schoolers. High schoolers get "restricted" access geared toward literacy and career-readiness programs, including five pilot programs at select schools, while AI is barred outright for grading, behavior monitoring, counseling, or drafting special-education plans. The policy follows a summer of parent-led protests, a City Council letter urging a pause, and a district-wide software-purchasing freeze that Chancellor Kamar Samuels ordered in July.

Sources: The New York Times, Chalkbeat, ABC7 New York, Gothamist

2. Texas rejected Alpha School's AI-driven charter bid — then approved it for taxpayer-funded vouchers anyway

A ProPublica investigation published September 2 finds that Texas charter regulators rejected an application from Alpha School — the Austin-based, no-classroom-teacher AI academy that charges up to $40,000–$55,000 a year — but state officials still cleared Alpha to accept students paying with Texas's new taxpayer-funded voucher program. Alpha's model swaps teachers for an AI tutoring platform plus "guide" facilitators, and the school has been rejected for public charter status in nine of the ten states where it applied, according to the report, exposing a gap between how regulators vet AI-driven schools for public accountability versus voucher eligibility. The story lands as Texas's first year of statewide vouchers gets underway and as Alpha expands into new markets, including Oklahoma and Chicago.

Sources: ProPublica, Texas Standard

3. UK survey: 95% of undergraduates now use AI, and universities are redesigning exams to cope

HEPI's Student Generative AI Survey 2026, based on 1,054 UK undergraduates and covered by Nature this week, finds AI use has become near-universal — 95% of students report using AI in some way, up from 92% in 2025 and just 66% in 2024 — and 94% now use generative AI specifically to help with assessed coursework, up from 51% a year earlier. Nature's companion reporting on "AI-proof exams" finds professors moving away from take-home essays toward in-person, oral, and process-based assessment after testing showed most commercial AI-detection tools cannot reliably distinguish hybrid human/AI writing. Russell Group students are most likely to feel their institution actively encourages AI use (39%), versus 30% at pre-1992 universities, pointing to uneven institutional guidance even as usage converges across the sector.

Sources: Nature, HEPI

4. European teens do homework 30% faster with AI — but a new "AI skills gap" is leaving poorer students behind

A Vodafone Foundation study conducted with Ipsos across seven European countries — surveying roughly 7,000 students aged 12 to 17 — finds that after adopting generative AI for schoolwork, homework scores rose 18% while time spent per assignment fell by about 30%, from 64 to 45 minutes on average, according to Al Jazeera's analysis of the data. The report also documents an emerging AI skills gap: 27% of children say they feel left behind on AI at school, with students from lower-income families most exposed, and fewer than half feel schools have adequately prepared them despite 74% expecting AI to shape their future careers. Vodafone frames the findings as evidence that faster homework does not straightforwardly translate into stronger learning, with weaker exam results suggesting a gap between AI-assisted speed and durable understanding.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Vodafone Foundation

5. IBM/Morning Consult: only 24% of K-12 educators think US schools are adapting well to AI

A new IBM-commissioned Morning Consult study finds a sharp gap between how fast AI is entering K-12 classrooms and how ready schools are to manage it: just 24% of educators and 16% of parents say the US education system is adapting "very well" to AI advances. The study also finds 77% of parents want a say in how AI is used in their child's classroom, yet only 20% say they clearly understand the AI guidance their child actually receives — a disconnect parents say could be fixed with clearer guidelines (51%) and more transparency about classroom AI use (48%). In response, IBM announced a new "K-12 AI Leaders Fellowship" aimed at superintendents and education leaders to help build responsible-AI adoption practices and improve parent communication.

Sources: PR Newswire, The AI Journal
