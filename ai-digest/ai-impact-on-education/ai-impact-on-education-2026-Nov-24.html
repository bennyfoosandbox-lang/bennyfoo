1. Maryland’s largest district drafts AI rules that leave the call to each teacher

Montgomery County Public Schools has released an 11-page draft AI regulation that would let individual educators decide if, when and how students use AI in their classrooms. The draft also sets out how staff and students may use AI and covers data privacy, vetting of tools and AI-literacy instruction, and it is open for public comment until December. It puts into practice the AI policy the board approved in May, under a Maryland rule that every district must have a local AI policy. Critics call the draft “careless” about AI’s harms to students and point to neighbouring Frederick County, which banned student AI use from pre-K to 12th grade. Supporters say teacher autonomy could work, but only if the district trains teachers to use AI responsibly.

Sources: Bethesda Magazine, Maryland Matters, WTOP

2. Most professors now use AI, but murky rules have turned disclosing it into a career risk

An Inside Higher Ed analysis published on 24 November asks what is driving what some academics call higher education’s AI “witch hunt.” Most faculty now use AI tools in some way, but there is no agreement on where acceptable AI-assisted writing ends and academic dishonesty begins, and fear of anti-AI bias keeps many from saying they use it. The piece follows Dartmouth provost Santiago Schnell’s October resignation over undisclosed AI use in his writing. It argues that AI unsettles writing as academia’s main measure of merit, and it cites a College Board survey finding faculty most negative about AI at institutions that admit fewer than a third of applicants. Each new controversy has brought calls for more transparency, including an Eastern Illinois student-senate resolution asking administrators to regulate how faculty use AI.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, Valley News (Dartmouth context)

3. Computer-science enrollment drops another 12% as colleges shift students toward AI credentials

Preliminary fall 2026 data from the National Student Clearinghouse show computer and information science enrollment down 12.4% at four-year colleges, 12.3% at two-year colleges and 10.4% in graduate programs, while total enrollment was roughly flat (+0.2%). Health professions (+6.2%) and engineering (+6.1%) grew. The decline is steeper than last year’s preliminary −7.7% (−8.1% final), though the early figures cover only 46% of institutions and final numbers arrive in January 2027. Local TV reports on 24 November say the fastest growth is now in AI concentrations rather than general CS. Tulane, for example, just approved a master’s certificate in AI, on top of its undergraduate AI minor and an AI concentration inside the CS major. Researchers, including Stanford economist Jacob Light, warn that the data don’t prove AI is the cause.

Sources: NSC Research Center, Clearinghouse blog, Forbes, WAFB, Fox 8

4. Tamil Nadu starts training 2,000 government-school teachers to teach with AI

Tamil Nadu launched a statewide professional-development programme on 24 November to train 2,000 teachers from government and government-aided schools in AI and ICT teaching methods. The course combines about 40 hours of online work with in-person lab sessions over three months. It is one of several teacher-upskilling pushes in southern India: Karnataka began AI training for teachers earlier this month, and Bodhan AI, an AI-in-education centre incubated at IIT Madras, aims to train a million teachers nationally by 2027. These programmes share a premise: AI in Indian classrooms will only work if teachers learn it first, not just students. For now this item rests mainly on one outlet’s report, so the details should be treated as provisional.

Sources: News Today, DT Next (Bodhan AI context)

5. New York’s new ban on “AI companion” features for minors leaves schools unsure whether it covers AI tutors

This one comes from earlier in the month, and legal analysis is still coming in. Governor Kathy Hochul signed S9051-B, now Chapter 368 of 2026, which bars chatbot operators from giving minors a list of “unsafe AI companion features.” Those include posing as a human, encouraging secrecy from trusted adults, producing sexual content and promoting self-harm. The attorney general enforces it, with penalties of up to $25,000 per violation, and most analyses give an effective date of 1 January 2027 (one outlet says 1 June). The open question for schools is whether purpose-built educational tools are exempt. Governing reads the law as exempting narrow-purpose AI that cannot drift into harmful behaviour, but other analysts say there is no education exemption at all. That matters for every district using AI tutors or chatbots with students.

Sources: Holland & Knight, IAPP, Governing, Future of Privacy Forum, News10, PPC Land
