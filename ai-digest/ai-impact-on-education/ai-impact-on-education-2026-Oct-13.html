1. The White House relaunches its student AI contest as the “Presidential Super Intelligence Challenge”

First Lady Melania Trump announced the 2027 Presidential Super Intelligence Challenge, an open call for K-12 students, educators and schools, together with a “First Lady Seal of SI Readiness” for participating schools. The contest replaces the Presidential AI Challenge, which The Hill reports drew more than 20,000 students last year, and students will be asked to build an “SI method or tool” that addresses a community problem. The rename follows a September executive order directing federal documents to say “super intelligence” instead of “artificial intelligence”, and the federal AI portal was rebranded at the same time, though coverage notes the switch is incomplete: the site still lives at ai.gov and some education links keep the old wording. The order has no legal force over private schools or companies, but it ties the federal K-12 AI push to a new vocabulary at the moment many districts are pulling back on student AI use.

Sources: Washington Times, The Hill, TokenPost

2. More than half of New York’s school districts have no board-adopted AI policy

New York Focus reviewed roughly 680 district websites and board policy manuals and found that most districts have no formal, board-approved AI policy; the count of districts with one is reported as around 270 in the body text and around 300 in the headline material. Where policies exist they mostly state broad principles such as academic integrity rather than specific permitted uses. Some districts without a policy still run internal guidelines, and Rockville Centre on Long Island has no board policy yet is pushing AI across its schools. The analysis counted districts that did not respond to reporters as lacking a policy, so the real figure may be somewhat lower. The finding lands weeks after New York City’s moratorium on student-facing generative AI through eighth grade, and shows how uneven the rest of the state’s rules are.

Sources: New York Focus, Route Fifty, The Highlands Current

3. Rest of World: inside the UAE’s mandate that makes AI a required K-12 subject

Rest of World looks at how the United Arab Emirates turned AI into a compulsory subject across public schools, following a May 2025 Cabinet decision. Close to a million children in government schools now work through seven curriculum strands, and about 22,000 teachers are being trained to use AI for teaching, assessment, curriculum analysis and lesson planning. The report frames the UAE as moving the opposite way from New York City and Norway, which are restricting student AI use. The “first country” framing is contested, since other sources say China also runs a compulsory national AI curriculum, and private schools outside the national curriculum are not covered, so treat the superlative with care.

Sources: Rest of World, Analytics Insight

4. DC-area private schools split between banning AI by default and building days around it

Washingtonian surveys how elite private schools around Washington are handling AI and finds no consensus. Georgetown Day School prohibits AI by default in its academic-integrity policy, so students need teacher permission before using any outside tool, while Alpha School’s Bethesda and Chantilly campuses compress core subjects into about two hours a day using adaptive platforms and AI tutors. Holton-Arms runs a student-led AI Innovators Club that mentors peers and teachers. American University’s Jennifer Steele calls the patchwork ad hoc but not chaotic, arguing teacher autonomy fits what private schools sell better than a top-down ban. A separate 5W AI Communications index that scores 30 local schools on AI curriculum depth is a vendor ranking with an obvious stake, so it is a weak guide.

Sources: Washingtonian, 5W Public Relations

5. East Carolina University maps an “ECU AI 2030” plan to make every graduate “AI-ready”

East Carolina University’s CIO presented “ECU AI 2030” to trustees on 24 September, with three priorities: AI-ready graduates, research, and operational efficiency; local TV followed up with an in-depth report on 12–13 October. The plan already includes a self-paced AI literacy course for incoming students and further mini-modules for graduating students and for presenting AI skills to employers. Leaders aim to build AI into every degree program and give every student access by next year, and ECU has begun rolling out 2,000 ChatGPT licenses in selected classes alongside Microsoft Copilot. Chancellor Philip Rogers says courses must be designed so using AI requires understanding the material. The trustee presentation is about three weeks old, so this item rests on a fresh local report of older news.

Sources: WCTI 12, ECU News, WITN
