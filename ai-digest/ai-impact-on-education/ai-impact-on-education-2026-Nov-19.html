1. Four Baltimore-area teens sue after an AI gun detector mistook a bag of chips for a weapon

More than a year after the October 2025 incident at Kenwood High School, four students (Taki Allen, Yui-Osa Ogebeide, Devon Thompson Jr. and Keith Winfield III) have filed a 31-page federal and state civil-rights complaint. They are represented by the Lawyers’ Committee for Civil Rights Under Law. The suit names Omnilert, whose Gun Detect system flagged a Doritos bag as a firearm, along with Baltimore County Public Schools, the police and individual officers. It alleges constitutional violations, negligence and gross negligence after officers ordered the teens to their knees at gunpoint, handcuffed them and searched them. The district holds a $2.6 million Omnilert contract that runs to 2027, and Omnilert already faces a separate suit from a Nashville shooting survivor who says the system missed a real gun. The case is an early legal test of who is liable when AI surveillance bought by schools triggers armed police responses against students.

Sources: theGrio, Fox Baltimore (background), IPVM (Nashville suit), Security InfoWatch

2. AutoSAT malware and a global exam-security arms race: how testing bodies are fighting AI cheating

New reporting, following a New York Times investigation, details how the College Board caught four students who used “AutoSAT” in the March SAT. AutoSAT is a $499 program, paid for in cryptocurrency, that bypassed the Bluebook app’s lockdown, sent on-screen questions to an AI chatbot and showed the answers. The College Board patched its code and sued the sellers in federal court in Pennsylvania, though the Boston Globe reports the suit was later dropped, partly because papers could never be served. The developers shipped a new version anyway, and AutoSAT is one of more than a dozen similar tools sold through online communities. In response, the College Board has banned AI smart glasses, started tracking keystrokes and cursor movement at some sites, and plans a new SAT schedule in 2027 to stop “time zone cheating.” A Korea Times comparison published on 17 November puts this alongside AI behaviour-flagging cameras at China’s gaokao, phone detectors in every French education region (where 8.5% of referred cheating cases involved AI), and Ofqual’s 2,225 phone and smart-device cases. Together these show high-stakes testing worldwide moving toward surveillance-heavy lockdowns.

Sources: Korea Times (global), Korea Times (US exams), The Korea Herald, Boston Globe, The Chronicle (briefing)

3. Inside Higher Ed survey: eight in 10 college students now use AI routinely, and a quarter write differently to dodge detectors

New Student Voice survey results published by Inside Higher Ed on 17 November find that about 80% of US college students now use generative or agentic AI routinely. Asked what responsible AI use should achieve, 65% put helping them learn, not just finishing the work, at the top. A quarter say they have changed how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors. That is a measurable chilling effect on student writing, and it adds to evidence that detection-led integrity policies carry real costs. The article’s framing is that AI is changing college while older challenges persist. For institutions, the data argue for clear course-level AI rules and assessment redesign rather than more detection.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed

4. Pakistan’s Punjab orders AI training for 25,898 public school teachers starting 23 November

The Punjab government in Pakistan has decided to train 25,898 public school teachers in artificial intelligence, with sessions starting on 23 November. This is a concrete first cohort under the province’s broader plan to train about 160,000 teachers for a new AI curriculum, which is being run with UNICEF and the province’s Office of AI. The step matters because earlier reporting found the Grade I–V rollout stalled: many government schools lack computers, internet access and trained staff. Getting teachers trained before AI is taught in classrooms is the bottleneck most national AI-literacy pushes run into. Details of the content and delivery are still thin in early reports.

Sources: 24 News HD, ProPakistani (160k plan), ProPakistani (rollout delays)
