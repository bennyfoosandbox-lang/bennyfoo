1. Dartmouth provost resigns after faculty committee finds he was not forthcoming about AI-written work

Dartmouth announced on 28 October that Provost Santiago Schnell is leaving after an advisory committee, whose report is dated 27 October, concluded he used AI to "meaningfully alter his writing" and was not forthcoming about it. The review began after his August Washington Post column on AI and cheating was flagged as entirely AI-written, and the student paper then found high AI rates in his other recent publications. Ars Technica reports the committee cited "insufficient cooperation" and said his public descriptions understated his actual AI use. Because the provost had been the face of Dartmouth's AI-in-education push, the case is now a test of whether disclosure rules for administrators are held to the same standard as those for students; Dartmouth held a campus forum on AI use in the days after. This is a material update to the September allegations already in the knowledge base.

Sources: AP via KSL, Ars Technica, Forbes, WMUR, The Observer (Notre Dame), Dartmouth advisory committee report (PDF)

2. Howard County, Md., makes every assignment AI-free unless a teacher says otherwise

Howard County's Board of Education approved an AI policy on Thursday, a week after the 22 October deadline set by Maryland's Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act; it is one of four Maryland systems that missed it. All assignments are treated as AI-free unless a teacher permits AI, and students must follow set boundaries and disclose or cite any use. The policy sets nine standards, bars staff from using AI for grading, counseling or entering personally identifiable information, and promises AI literacy instruction and teacher training. It sits in sharp contrast with neighbouring Frederick County, which voted this month to ban student AI use in nearly all cases, while Howard gave high schoolers Google Gemini access in January. Board members raised parent opt-outs and the risk of teachers wrongly accusing students of AI use, and the student board member said guidance is unclear to students.

Sources: GovTech (via Baltimore Sun/TNS), Baltimore Sun

3. Scale AI, UMSL and Lindbergh Schools launch a free 12-hour AI course for K-12 teachers nationwide

Scale AI, the University of Missouri-St. Louis and Lindbergh Schools launched "Fundamentals of AI for Educators" on 30 October, a self-paced, 12-hour course and microcredential available free to teachers across the US through UMSL's Advanced Workforce Center. Twenty Lindbergh teachers piloted it over the summer, and participants build a classroom-ready instructional artifact that integrates AI into a specific learning objective, earning a certificate and digital badge. The launch supports Scale's pledge under the White House's Pledge to American Youth to help train 1,000 teachers and equip 500,000 students with foundational AI skills by 2029. It is another sign that AI-company-backed teacher training is becoming a standard route for districts that lack their own professional development.

Sources: Scale AI, UMSL Newsroom, St. Louis Business Journal

4. EdWeek survey: nearly a third of school and district leaders use AI for no operations at all

An EdWeek Research Center survey of 852 educators, including 262 district and school leaders, fielded 26 August to 6 October, finds nearly one-third of administrators do not use generative AI for any school or district operations. Among those who do, the leading uses are school and district communications (47%), foreign-language translation (29%), strategic planning (24%), employee evaluation (23%) and class scheduling (17%). Leaders interviewed say the barrier is often staff who do not know how to use the tools well, and that their attention has gone to the screen-time debate, teacher training and student privacy. The numbers suggest administrative AI use is still concentrated in writing and translation tasks rather than budgeting, security or logistics.

Sources: Education Week

5. ECNU Review paper: AI is shortening the shelf life of skills and weakening the case for front-loaded degrees

A paper by Yilei Shao of East China Normal University's Shanghai AI-Finance School, published in the ECNU Review of Education and covered on 30 October, argues that human capital theory's core assumptions are failing as AI makes certain kinds of intelligence abundant. It cites an estimated skill half-life of 10 to 15 years in the industrial era, 5 to 7 in the information age and just 2 to 3 in the AI age, plus a World Economic Forum projection that 22% of today's jobs will be disrupted by 2030. The author contends that credentials earned up front no longer reliably signal ability and that education should shift toward lifelong learning, dual literacy and skills that conventional credentials do not measure. It is a conceptual argument rather than an empirical test, but it gives the ongoing debate over degree value a formal economic framing.

Sources: Earth.com
