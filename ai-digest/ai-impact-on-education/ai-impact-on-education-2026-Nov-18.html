1. Ofqual warns pupils that passing off AI work as their own coursework can cost them a whole GCSE or A-level

England’s exams regulator has opened a publicity push ahead of coursework deadlines. Chief regulator Sir Ian Bauckham told PA that pupils who submit undeclared AI-generated work risk “life-altering” penalties, which in extreme cases means losing an entire qualification. Ofqual’s figures show 100 proven cases of AI-related plagiarism across GCSE, AS and A-level work in summer 2025, up from 85 a year earlier and about three-quarters of all student plagiarism cases. A Teacher Tapp poll of more than 4,500 teachers for Ofqual found 70% do not think unsupervised written coursework should count toward final grades, and Bauckham suggested unsupervised extended essays may not survive in their current form. He also flagged wearables as a cheating risk in supervised exams. Together, these point to coursework in England moving toward supervised or redesigned formats rather than relying on detection.

Sources: Tes, The Irish News (PA), Resultsense, University Herald, Ofqual blog

2. A preregistered trial across 19 universities finds instructor-designed AI raised STEM exam scores

A new arXiv preprint led by Jun Li, with 51 co-authors, reports a preregistered randomized trial with about 7,200 students in 55 applied STEM classes at 19 universities, run from spring 2025 to spring 2026. Classes were randomly assigned to adopt AI innovations their own instructors had designed either immediately or one term later. Learning was measured with AI-proof assessments (proctored, in-person exams). Outcomes rose by 0.26 standard deviations at the class level and 0.32 SD at the student level. That is a large effect for an education intervention, and it supports the view that AI helps when teachers shape it to their course rather than when students use generic chatbots. The paper is not yet peer reviewed and has not been replicated.

Sources: arXiv (abstract), arXiv (full text), Michael Amicangelo (Substack)

3. EdWeek survey: most teacher-prep programs still have no stand-alone AI course

An EdWeek Research Center survey of 268 teacher educators, fielded in August and September, finds that most US teacher-preparation programs offer no stand-alone AI course for current or future teachers. About a third of respondents said their programs struggle to prepare teachers for AI because faculty or instructors don’t support it. Nearly a third said other priorities, such as material required for state and national accreditation, crowd it out. Close to a quarter said their prospective teachers don’t want AI preparation, though faculty overall want more AI professional development than they get. This matters because new teachers keep arriving in classrooms where most students already use AI, without having been trained on it.

Sources: Education Week, EdWeek Research Center report

4. CodeAI launches the 2026 Hour of AI around student building, with OpenAI as a partner

CodeAI, the nonprofit formerly called Code.org, has launched this year’s Hour of AI activities under a “Start Building” campaign. The activities are aimed at students in grades 3–12 and are timed for Computer Science Education Week. The pitch has moved from using AI to making things with it, so students learn what sits behind the tools. OpenAI backs the program through the partnership announced in August, and CodeAI and Microsoft offer a free 30-minute module to help teachers run it. The Hour of AI took over from the Hour of Code, which CodeAI says has reached tens of millions of students, so this is one of the largest single routes for introducing AI to schoolchildren.

Sources: PR Newswire (CodeAI), OpenAI, CodeAI, GeekWire

5. University of Alberta pilots a course-grounded AI assistant inside Canvas, as faculty critics object

U.S. News profiles the University of Alberta’s year-long LearnWise AI pilot, which puts an AI assistant inside the Canvas learning management system. The pilot runs through May 2027 and has three parts: a student tutor that answers only from course materials, an opt-in feedback assistant for instructors, and a campus-wide help assistant. The university says the tool has guardrails against writing essays or answering quiz and exam questions, is hosted in European data centres, and gives instructors a dashboard of common student questions and misconceptions. Faculty decide whether their courses take part, and the university says it will use surveys and interviews to decide whether the tool is worth continued funding. Critics on campus say the administration launched it without consulting the faculty association or General Faculties Council. They also question its learning value and environmental cost, a pattern seen in other campus AI rollouts.

Sources: U.S. News & World Report, University of Alberta, Arts Squared (critique)
