1. Nashville fires a teacher for secretly running AI-altered photos and videos of his students

Metro Nashville Public Schools terminated Mario Mumphery, a tenured exceptional-education teacher at Big Picture High School, after a board vote late last week following a July 7 dismissal letter from Superintendent Adrienne Battle. Multiple students reported that Mumphery photographed and filmed them in class, then ran the footage through AI tools to alter it without their permission — the resulting material allegedly included videos depicting students dancing in ways they found inappropriate, images inserting them into fabricated personal or family scenes, and "teddy bear style" cartoon versions of their photos. Battle's letter described the conduct as "unprofessional," and the board's unanimous vote makes this one of the first US cases of a teacher being fired specifically for AI-based misuse of student likenesses, rather than the more familiar pattern of students being disciplined over AI use. It lands alongside this year's wave of stories about AI mishandling student images and data, but flips the usual roles: here, the adult in the room is the one accused of the violation.

Sources: WSMV, Fox 17 Nashville, NewsChannel 5, WKRN

2. China orders AI instruction to become "basically universal" in every primary and secondary school by 2030

On July 28, China's Ministry of Education issued a formal notice directing primary and secondary schools nationwide to expand AI education, building on the "AI Plus Education" action plan the ministry launched in April. The notice takes a tiered approach by grade band: younger students are meant simply to experience AI in class, while high schoolers are expected to complete creative projects using advanced AI tools, with AI literacy also folded into teacher-certification exams. Implementation is already visible in specific schools — at Beijing No. 13 Secondary School, an AI "agent" analyzes each student's classroom participation, homework, and quiz results to generate individualized question sequences and after-class assignments, while in Wenzhou, Zhejiang province, students in AI-literacy classes built generative-AI digital avatars that present the city's culture in the local dialect. The plan's ambitions extend well beyond the classroom, envisioning AI eventually informing decisions like where to site new schools and which majors universities should offer — a considerably more centralized, top-down build-out than the district-by-district patchwork playing out in the US.

Sources: Georgetown CSET, CGTN, Guangming Online

3. A wave of universities launch undergraduate AI degrees just in time for fall move-in

Northeastern Illinois University announced it will become the first public university in Chicago to offer a Bachelor of Science in Artificial Intelligence, open to students "from all backgrounds with no prior experience required" and built with ethical reasoning and fairness woven through the curriculum; applications for domestic students closed August 1, one day before this digest. It's part of a broader crop of new programs launching this fall: Syracuse University's College of Engineering and Computer Science rolled out a full BS/MS "AI Science" portfolio on July 8, Stevens Institute of Technology is opening a dedicated AI bachelor's degree, and Michigan Technological University and the University of Central Oklahoma are each adding new AI-focused majors, minors, or concentrations for the same semester. The programs are explicitly framed around workforce demand rather than research-track computer science, with several schools citing employer pull for graduates who can build, deploy, and govern AI systems rather than just study them theoretically. Coming from public regional universities as much as flagship research schools, the wave suggests dedicated AI degrees are moving from an elite-school novelty to a standard offering across US higher ed.

Sources: PR Newswire, CBS Chicago, Syracuse University, Stevens Institute of Technology

4. More districts race out AI rules before the bell rings — this time Texas, Oklahoma, and Massachusetts

The back-to-school policy scramble this digest tracked in Florida last run has spread to more states: Katy Independent School District in Texas will bar direct student access to generative-AI chatbots for kindergarten through sixth grade starting this school year, while letting older students use AI under teacher supervision. In Oklahoma, Tulsa-area districts including Owasso and Broken Arrow are writing their own classroom-AI rules ahead of the state's Responsible Technology in Schools Act, which took effect July 1 and requires every district to have a formal policy in place by the 2027-28 school year covering approved and prohibited uses, data protection, and family transparency. Outside Boston, the Woburn, Massachusetts School Committee adopted the district's first-ever AI policy, applying only to high schoolers for now and reflecting a common pattern nationally: districts starting AI guardrails at the secondary level before deciding whether to extend them down to younger grades. Together with Ohio's July 1 policy deadline and last run's Florida coverage, the pattern is now a genuinely national one — districts converging on the need for a written AI policy even as they land on very different rules for who gets to use what.

Sources: Iowa Park Leader, Tulsa Flyer, Woburn Daily Times Chronicle

5. EdTech's AI shopping spree continues: Sanoma buys a Polish AI language-tutoring startup

Nordic education publisher Sanoma announced on July 2 it has acquired Fluentbe, a Polish AI-powered language-tutoring platform that has served more than 40,000 students through 600-plus active tutors and posted roughly €6 million in 2025 revenue; Fluentbe's founders and team join Sanoma Learning, giving the company an entry point into Poland's tutoring market and cross-sell potential across Sanoma's 2-million-user digital base. It's a small deal by dollar value, but it fits a much larger 2026 pattern of AI capability being bought rather than built: Coursera's $2.5 billion all-stock merger with Udemy closed in May, KKR completed a $4.8 billion take-private of Instructure, and Bain Capital closed a $5.6 billion acquisition of PowerSchool — all deals explicitly framed around consolidating AI-native learning tools rather than just adding scale. Analysts tracking the sector describe 2026 as edtech's strongest M&A year since the pandemic-era peak, with buyers favoring targets that already have "defensible AI moats" and measurable learner outcomes over pure user-growth stories. For schools and universities, the effect is fewer independent AI-tutoring vendors to choose between as the smaller ones get absorbed into a handful of larger platforms.

Sources: Manila Times / GlobeNewswire, EducationInvestor Global, XYZ.pl
