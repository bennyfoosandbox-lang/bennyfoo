1. California enacts the nation's toughest AI-chatbot child-safety law — with a school-laptop opt-out built in

Governor Gavin Newsom signed a package of bills on September 9–10, headlined by SB 1119 ("Adam's Law"), which requires AI companion-chatbot operators to build in crisis protocols for suicidal ideation, parental controls, and mandatory notifications if a child disables safety settings, plus first-in-the-nation independent child-safety audits and annual risk assessments. Named for Adam Raine, a California teen whose family says a chatbot "coached" him toward suicide, the law arrives alongside AB 1405 and SB 813, which create a state AI-auditor registry and a framework for third-party verification of AI systems generally. Its most direct education hook: the package lets families opt out of school-issued laptops and the AI-enabled software running on them, handing parents already fighting over classroom AI in districts like NYC and LAUSD a new, statewide lever. It lands the same week California's SB 928 — barring AI from replacing human instructors — took effect, cementing the state as the country's most aggressive AI-in-schools regulator.

Sources: Governor of California, Boston Globe, CNBC, Contra Costa News

2. New PISA data: US reading scores hit a 25-year low as AI adoption widens the gap with Asia

A CNN analysis of the OECD's PISA results — the same dataset behind last week's finding that daily AI use tracks with a "year and a half" of lost learning — shows US secondary students' reading scores have fallen to their lowest point in 25 years, even as students in China and other Asian systems extend their lead. The report ties part of the widening gap to how unevenly AI is being folded into instruction: systems pairing AI tools with strong foundational literacy teaching are pulling ahead, while systems leaning on AI as a substitute for direct instruction are falling further behind. It's a second, distinct data point from the same PISA release — this one on reading rather than science, and framed around a US-Asia comparison rather than daily-use frequency — complicating the "AI is transforming learning for the better" pitch that Google, OpenAI and others have been making to schools this back-to-school season. The 74 separately reports the share of OECD students below baseline performance climbed to 20% from 16% in the prior cycle.

Sources: CNN, The 74, ABC17News, KVIA

3. Schools quietly retire the A–F report card in favor of AI-graded "mastery" transcripts

Legend.org, the for-profit AI company that bought the nonprofit Mastery Transcript Consortium from ETS in July, is rolling out AI grading tools to the roughly 400 public and private schools using the consortium's alternative transcript — one that replaces A–F grades with tracked progress on dozens of academic and "soft" skills like collaboration and critical thinking. Teachers upload student work — papers, exams, recorded presentations, even photographed handwritten assignments or artwork — and the AI evaluates it against state, school, or teacher-set standards, handing back a draft rating that a teacher confirms or overrides; co-founder Matt Sornson said the AI never issues a final grade on its own. It's a notable bet that AI's evaluative power, distrusted when used to catch cheating, can be repurposed as trusted infrastructure for the "beyond letter grades" movement mastery-transcript schools have championed for a decade. The move also puts a for-profit AI company in charge of a once-nonprofit assessment model, echoing the same governance questions already dogging Amira Learning's state-mandated AI reading tests.

Sources: Santa Fe New Mexican, KVIA (Stacker), LocalNews8, KEYT

4. Largest global survey yet: 88% of college students now use AI, and most say guidance hasn't caught up

The Digital Education Council's AI in Higher Education Global Survey 2026 — drawing on more than 45,000 responses from students and faculty across 35 countries — found 88% of students and 77% of faculty now use AI in their coursework or teaching, both up sharply from the 2025 edition (faculty use alone rose 16 points). But adoption is outrunning readiness: 57% of students say their assessments come with inadequate AI guidance, only 29% believe their instructors are properly equipped to guide AI use, and just 31% of faculty feel meaningfully consulted on their institution's AI policy. Six in ten students worldwide worry classmates are using AI for an unfair advantage, a figure that jumps to 73% among students in the US and Canada specifically. It's the largest dataset yet on the topic, and it echoes at global scale the same gap last week's UK HEPI survey found domestically: near-universal use colliding with policy and pedagogy that hasn't kept pace.

Sources: Digital Education Council, EdTech Innovation Hub, The College Fix, Boise State News

5. A Tokyo startup raises seed funding to fuse AI tutoring with real classrooms for young English learners

Tokyo-based EdTechX closed a Series Seed round from PKSHA Algorithm Fund to expand X Junior, its AI-powered English-learning program for young children that pairs home tablet practice with in-person classroom instruction rather than replacing it outright. The company, led by CEO Naofumi Igarashi, says the funding will go toward building AI models that adapt to each child's individual learning state, validating the approach at its own company-run schools, and building infrastructure so home tutors and other institutions can plug into the platform. It's a small but telling data point in this week's broader pattern: as US and European debates fixate on banning or restricting AI in classrooms, education-focused AI investment in Asia is flowing toward hybrid human-AI models aimed at literacy and language acquisition in the youngest learners. PKSHA's backing extends a longer thread of Japanese AI investment reaching deeper into early-childhood and primary education than most Western programs have so far attempted.

Sources: Venture Pitch Online, PR TIMES, Third News
