1. California's biggest faculty-and-teacher union asks Newsom to pause student-facing AI in K-8 for three years

The California Federation of Teachers, which represents about 120,000 educators, sent Gov. Gavin Newsom a letter on 10 November asking him to use an executive order, before his term ends in January, to impose a three-year moratorium on student-facing technology with generative-AI features in kindergarten through eighth grade. President Jeff Freitas argued that teachers are not equipped to supervise student AI use and that children cannot reliably tell helpful tools from harmful ones, and said the state should pause the spread of the technology while more research is done. The letter backs the American Federation of Teachers' national recommendation of no student-facing AI in elementary schools; it targets student-facing generative tools rather than every AI-powered administrative or accessibility system. The request lands the same week State Superintendent Tony Thurmond held a statewide AI and education summit urging responsible classroom use, and as New York City and Los Angeles already restrict student AI in younger grades. There is no sign yet that Newsom will act.

Sources: Yahoo News (POLITICO), Yahoo News, GovTech, California Globe, Santa Barbara Independent (Thurmond summit)

2. Cal State faculty push back on AI on two fronts: a senate resolution and a strike vote

The California State University's systemwide Academic Senate has passed a resolution asking leaders to point the system's AI efforts at easing faculty administrative work rather than promoting AI in teaching and learning, citing worries about students' critical thinking and about meaningful assessment when chatbots can produce long-form answers on demand. Senate resolutions carry no executive power, but it will go to the chancellor, campus presidents and the Board of Trustees, which meets 17–18 November. At the same time, the California Faculty Association is running a strike-authorization vote that closes 19 November, and AI is one of its central bargaining issues: the union wants contract language barring CSU from using AI to evaluate or monitor faculty, which management rejected, offering only an expiring memorandum of understanding. The two moves come from the system that pays about $13 million a year to give ChatGPT Edu to all 470,000 students, and they show faculty trying to set the terms of AI adoption through governance and labour channels.

Sources: Yahoo News, California Faculty Association, CFA strike vote page, EdSource, Ellenador News

3. Alpha School's own data: about half of its K-8 students miss its “2x” math growth target

A New York Times review of Alpha School's internal data, now being widely re-reported after a Fortune piece on 14 November, finds that roughly half of K-8 students at the AI-driven private network's campuses missed its signature goal of learning math twice as fast, and about one in five missed the reading target. Only 42% met the “2x” goal in both subjects, and among students who started at or below the national 50th percentile in math, just 37% hit it by winter. The 2x figure is Alpha's own construct, calculated by dividing students' growth on NWEA's MAP tests by expected median growth, and the underlying data has not been independently reviewed. The findings matter because Alpha, which charges up to $75,000 a year in San Francisco and Palo Alto, is expanding fast (about 1,500 students across about 50 campuses, plus 2,600+ public-school students in Texas and South Carolina using its platform) and is often cited as proof that AI tutoring can replace much of traditional instruction. Alpha maintains that its students outperform peers on standardised tests.

Sources: Fortune, AI Weekly, Cognitive Resonance (Benjamin Riley), matr.net

4. Anthropic extends free Claude for Teachers to July 2028 for US K-12 educators and districts

Anthropic updated its Claude for Teachers announcement on 13 November to say that verified US K-12 educators, whether they sign up on their own or through a school or district, can now use the product free through 30 July 2028. The district-level Enterprise offering launched in August is also free for qualifying organisations through the same date. Earlier third-party guides described a single free year for teachers who signed up by mid-2027, so this is a substantial lengthening of the subsidy. Anthropic restates that it does not train models on Claude for Teachers conversations. The extension comes days after the company signed the AFT's enforceable safety and privacy terms, and amid a broader price war in which OpenAI's ChatGPT for Teachers and Google's Gemini tools are also free to schools, which makes how long vendors keep subsidising classroom AI a growing procurement and lock-in question for districts.

Sources: Anthropic, Claude for schools and districts, Claude Help Center

5. District AI policies keep landing: South Pasadena bars student AI unless a teacher assigns it, while Martinsville waits

South Pasadena Unified's board unanimously adopted Board Policy 0441 on generative AI at its 10 November meeting, after a contentious first reading in October. As reported, the policy keeps generative AI off student devices unless a teacher explicitly assigns it, requires students to disclose AI use and applies existing plagiarism rules, and requires staff who use AI to make class materials or rubrics to cite it in MLA or APA style. It also says AI-detection software cannot be the sole basis for discipline or grade penalties. In Virginia, the Martinsville City School Board tabled its own proposed AI policy until December, choosing between a short version that leaves specifics to the division and a detailed one listing permitted teacher uses, student expectations and required school-approved tools. Together with Kansas's new statewide mandate, the two boards show districts writing local AI rules, and limiting how far they rely on detectors, faster than state or federal guidance is arriving.

Sources: The South Pasadenan, WSET
