1. OECD outlook: a well-designed AI tutor beat in-class active learning, and a tutor co-pilot lifted pass rates

The OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 highlights two pieces of evidence that cut against the 'AI makes students lazy' narrative. In a randomised trial of 316 students in an introductory US physics course, learners taught by a generative AI tutor built on active-learning principles gained significantly more, in less time, than peers in a physical active-learning class, with an effect size of 0.63 that the OECD calls large and with higher reported motivation. Separately, Stanford's Tutor CoPilot, used by 900 tutors with 1,800 underserved pupils, raised pass rates by 4% on average and by 9% for the least experienced tutors. The report's message is about design rather than raw capability: a chatbot that hands out answers is a shortcut, while a tool built around learning objectives or one that coaches a human tutor can help. Coverage cautions that both studies concern a single course or programme, so they are not proof that AI tutors beat teachers in general.

Sources: India Today

2. Australia's education ministers agree to restrict generative AI in primary schools and secondary assessments

At this week's Education Ministers Meeting, Australia's federal and state ministers agreed to a national 'digital detox' that includes restricting generative AI in primary schools and in secondary school assessments, alongside setting or considering screen-time limits. Education Minister Jason Clare cited evidence that AI can undermine independent thinking, referring to 'cognitive stunting'. A new Australian Schools Digital Detox Taskforce will survey students, teachers and parents and advise on further steps next year, and Year 5 NAPLAN writing will return to pen and paper. Press reports describe the move as a ban on generative AI in primary classrooms and a restriction during high-school assessments. It is one of the most explicit national-level curbs on classroom AI to date, and the detail on enforcement is still to come.

Sources: Australian Ministers' Media Centre, The Australian, CathNews, Mirage News

3. Manchester study: lecturers think students use AI far more than students say they do

Researchers at the University of Manchester and the University of the Basque Country surveyed 45 academic staff and 86 students in a UK social-science school and found a wide perception gap. Staff rated students' academic AI use at 5.23 out of 7, while students rated their own use at 3.11, though the authors note students may under-report. Students were also more positive than staff about AI's benefits for productivity and learning, and mostly used it to produce or polish text and to find answers. Only 63% of staff had used any generative AI tool in the prior six months and 14% paid for one. The authors argue that universities should move beyond treating AI mainly as an integrity threat and avoid policy built on assumptions and distrust; the sample is small and dates from spring 2024.

Sources: University of Manchester, Frontiers in Education (paper)

4. EdWeek survey: one in three educators doesn't know if their school has a deepfake policy

An EdWeek Research Center survey of 852 educators, fielded this summer, found that 33% don't know whether their school or district has a policy on AI-generated fake images and video, 22% say there is none, and only 2% say one is being considered. Nine in ten said they had no training on detecting or responding to deepfakes in the last two years, and 13% did not know what a deepfake is. Experts quoted say schools may not need an entirely new policy but should add deepfake language to harassment and technology-use rules and write an incident response plan; distributing a sexually explicit fake of a minor is now a federal crime. The article ties the gap to how little skill or cost it takes for students to fabricate images or videos of peers and staff.

Sources: Education Week

5. Universities turn to standing AI committees: Drexel, Boston University and Missouri show three models

EdTech Magazine profiles how colleges are building cross-functional AI task forces to keep governance in step with fast-moving tools. Drexel's 2023 task force became a permanent committee that tells faculty not to rely on AI detectors because of false positives, requires syllabus statements on permitted AI use, is rolling out ChatGPT Edu to all students by fall 2026 and plans AI literacy in first-year courses. Boston University's AI Development Accelerator offers ChatGPT, Microsoft, Gemini, Claude and Llama through one portal, while the University of Missouri system launched Show-Me AI, a secure platform for course-specific assistants, with policies that bar AI grading and treat detectors as insufficient proof of cheating. EDUCAUSE's Jenay Robert says effective governance must include stakeholders from across the institution.

Sources: EdTech Magazine
