1. Australia’s national research body tells schools to use AI “sparingly” — and not at all in the first three years

The Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO), the government-funded national research body, issued advice to teachers, school leaders and policymakers after reviewing Australian and overseas studies on AI in learning. CEO Jenny Donovan warned of “cognitive outsourcing,” in which novice learners let a tool do the thinking and never build the knowledge they need, noting overseas studies where students who used AI could not reproduce their results once it was removed in an exam. AERO’s guidance is to avoid AI entirely in the first three years of primary school, use it carefully afterwards alongside explicit instruction, and favor purpose-built tools like NSW’s, which guide students through reasoning instead of handing over answers. Parents are urged not to give children unfettered access to general chatbots at home, and the advice arrives as Education Minister Jason Clare calls for a “digital detox” after Australia’s weak PISA results.

Sources: ABC News

2. Gallup: 59% of US K-12 educators used AI last year, and most of them say it bought back time

A new Walton Family Foundation–Gallup survey, run with The Mattering Institute among 1,952 US K-12 educators in late July and August, finds 59% used AI tools during the 2025–26 school year for tasks like lesson prep, materials, grading and administration. Among those users, 13% say AI gave them a lot more time for the parts of the job that matter most and 51% somewhat more time, while 7% say it cost them time and 29% saw no change. The survey’s wider point is that educators feel they matter most when working directly with students (85% cite helping a student through something difficult), and only 35% feel they matter a great deal at their school, so the AI time savings are best read as a way to shift effort away from administrative work. Use is highest among educators aged 40 to 59 and well below average for those 60 and older.

Sources: Gallup

3. Korean teachers push back on essay-style CSAT questions as students start citing AI to dispute grades

A survey of 2,427 Korean middle and high school teachers, released with a casebook by the National Secondary School Teachers’ Union, finds 73.5% oppose adding written-response questions to the national College Scholastic Ability Test, with only 1.8% in favor outright. Teachers say grading complaints already push them toward fixed-answer questions: 38.5% cited the burden of handling objections and 35.6% the difficulty of consistent grading. AI complicates this in two ways: students now argue that their answer is correct because a chatbot agreed with it, and teachers who tried AI grading said setting criteria and checking results added work and struggled with subject-specific concepts. The National Education Commission is weighing whether to delay a draft proposal that was due later this month, and a final plan was originally slated for March.

Sources: The Korea Times

4. Jesuit high-school panel at Fordham: use AI as the spotter, not the one doing the reps

At Fordham’s Ignatian AI Conference on Oct. 3, students and educators from four Jesuit high schools described very different working models. Brophy College Prep in Phoenix uses the Flint platform so teachers can build chatbots that prompt students to explain their own thinking rather than supply answers, and its principal says students used AI to help write grants that secured over $50,000 for sustainability projects. Cristo Rey New York runs a red/yellow/green traffic-light system for each assignment, while Fairfield Prep allows AI as a tutor but not a substitute, summed up as “don’t let AI do the reps for you… let AI be your spotter.” A Fordham Prep senior cautioned that schools tell students when AI is allowed but not how to use it well, and panelists agreed relationships between teachers and students have to stay central, with Brophy reintroducing recess for first-year high schoolers.

Sources: Fordham Now

5. Columbia Law and Paul Weiss sign a five-year partnership to build generative AI into legal education

Columbia Law School has named Paul, Weiss, Rifkind, Wharton & Garrison as lead partner of its Generative Artificial Intelligence Initiative under a five-year agreement, according to the New York Law Journal. The initiative aims to integrate responsible generative AI use into legal education through new curricula, research and multidisciplinary collaboration, and includes appointing a scholar-in-residence to connect academics, practitioners, judges, policymakers and industry leaders. It follows the school’s August AI policy, in which Dean Daniel Abebe said students must learn to engage with AI in ways that serve educational purpose while developing critical thinking and “technological fluency.” The full article is paywalled, so details beyond this summary are not verified here.

Sources: New York Law Journal (Law.com)
