1. America's two largest teachers unions strike a legally enforceable AI deal with Microsoft

AFT President Randi Weingarten, United Federation of Teachers President Michael Mulgrew and Microsoft Vice Chair and President Brad Smith stood together in New York on September 9 to unveil a National AI Safety & Privacy Standard for schools — ten legally enforceable protections that bar Microsoft from using student or teacher data to train its AI models except in narrow safety circumstances, and give districts contractual control over how that data is retained, used and erased. Framed explicitly as a response to the absence of federal or state rules governing AI in classrooms, the standard takes effect November 1 and will be offered free to every school district nationwide, with Microsoft newly liable for breach of contract if it violates any term. The announcement lands a week after Mayor Mamdani's NYC moratorium and amid LAUSD's own AI restrictions, and organizers say it's meant as a template other vendors can be pushed to match: the AFT says it is already in active talks with OpenAI and Anthropic to reach similar agreements. It's a notable shift in leverage — rather than districts negotiating AI safety terms one contract at a time, a national union is trying to set an industry-wide floor.

Sources: K-12 Dive, The Hill, Fortune, ABC7 New York

2. APA's psychologists tell schools: engagement with AI tools isn't proof of learning

A multidisciplinary panel of psychologists released the American Psychological Association's Expert Report on Children's and Adolescents' Learning with Educational Technology this week, warning that how engaged a student looks with an app or chatbot says little about whether real learning is happening — and that generative AI carries a particular risk of boosting immediate task performance without building the underlying knowledge or skill. The report argues that a much stronger signal of learning is whether a student can apply what they picked up outside the app or screen, and it synthesizes evidence across a wide range of digital learning tools rather than generative AI alone. Rather than recommending schools avoid AI, the panel's ten evidence-based recommendations push in the opposite direction: teach students to actively interrogate AI output — question its assumptions, spot gaps in its reasoning, and compare its answers against other sources — so they treat AI as something to test and challenge rather than as an answer machine. It's a research-driven middle path that arrives as districts nationwide swing between wholesale bans and open access, offering practitioners a framework for the classroom-level "how" that most current policy debates skip past.

Sources: Education Week, Phys.org, EurekAlert!, APA (full report)

3. Missouri becomes the first state to give Google's AI tools to every public school and college free

Google announced a statewide partnership on September 8 with Missouri's Department of Elementary and Secondary Education and its Department of Higher Education and Workforce Development, giving nearly 100,000 educators and more than 1.1 million K-12 and higher-ed students no-cost access to Gemini for Education, Gemini Notebook, the Google AI Educator Series and Google AI Professional and Career Certificates. Each Missouri district, college and university still decides individually whether, when and how to actually adopt the tools, and Google says conversations and data inside Gemini for Education stay private and are not used to train its models. The deal reaches beyond the classroom too: Google is opening its Career Certificates and AI courses at no cost to all Missouri residents through the state's local Job Centers, tying the education rollout directly to workforce development. It's one of the broadest state-level, single-vendor AI commitments logged so far this run, and a contrast to the restriction-first approach dominating coverage out of NYC and LA this month.

Sources: Google Blog, GovTech, Unite.AI, KTVO

4. Florida's public-nuisance case against OpenAI gets kicked back to a state jury

A federal judge remanded Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier's negligence and consumer-protection lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman back to the state's 10th Judicial Circuit in Highlands County on September 8, after OpenAI had tried to move the case into federal court. Uthmeier's suit, filed in June, alleges OpenAI released and aggressively marketed ChatGPT while disregarding internal and external safety warnings and misrepresenting the product as safe, in violation of Florida's Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act; the complaint cites the 2025 Florida State University mass shooting among its examples of alleged harm. The venue fight matters because Florida is explicitly seeking to have ChatGPT and Altman formally classified as a "public nuisance" — a designation OpenAI has been fighting to keep away from a state jury, which is generally seen as more favorable terrain for plaintiffs than federal court. With the case now headed back to state court, it becomes one of the more closely watched tests of how state consumer-protection law, rather than product-liability or First Amendment theories, might be used to constrain AI companies' marketing to institutions like schools.

Sources: Click Orlando, CBS12, Yahoo News

5. RAND: AI use in US schools is climbing fast, and poorer districts are getting the least guidance on it

A RAND survey report published this week, drawing on its American Teacher and American School District panels, finds AI use among US teachers up roughly 25 percentage points and among students up roughly 15 points year over year — with 54% of students and 53% of English language arts, math and science teachers now using AI for school during the 2024-25 school year, about double teacher-adoption rates from two years ago. Guidance hasn't kept pace: only 35% of district leaders say they provide students with AI training, only 34% of teachers report their school has an academic-integrity policy covering AI, and fewer than half of principals say their school or district has any AI-use policy at all, even though 26 states now offer some form of AI guidance. The starkest finding is distributional — teachers and principals in higher-poverty schools are both less likely to use AI tools and less likely to receive guidance on using them than their counterparts in lower-poverty schools, suggesting the "guidance gap" tracks existing resource inequities rather than closing them. It's a data point that complicates the current ban-versus-adopt framing dominating district-level headlines: the RAND numbers suggest the more urgent problem in most schools isn't whether to allow AI, but that adoption is already outrunning any policy at all.

Sources: RAND, CBS Austin, DATIA K12
