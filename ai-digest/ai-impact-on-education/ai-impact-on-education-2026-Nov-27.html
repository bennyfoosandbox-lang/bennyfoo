1. The Philippines will screen every Grade 2 pupil with an AI speech tool and extend its AI dropout warnings to 300 schools

The Philippines’ Department of Education (DepEd) said on 19 November that two pilots from its Education Center for AI Research (ECAIR) will scale up in 2027. The first is Project SABAY (Screening using AI-Based Assistance for Young Children). It uses short speech activities to flag Grade 2 pupils who may have learning or communication difficulties, and takes about 14.5 minutes per child. The Multi-Factored Assessment Tool now in use can take up to two hours. SABAY was co-developed with the Bureau of Learning Delivery, tested in 10 schools across five regions, and is set to go nationwide. The second is Talaan, an AI dropout early-warning system built with input from about 120 teachers and school heads in three Las Piñas schools; it will expand to 300 schools. Education Secretary Sonny Angara framed both tools as ways to support teachers rather than replace them. Together they move AI from lesson-planning help into screening and early intervention for children, a higher-stakes use. DepEd has not published accuracy figures, a budget or how the 300 schools will be chosen. This is a fallback item from about a week ago.

Sources: Daily Tribune, PhilstarLIFE, NewsBytes.PH, Asia News Network

2. Admissions offices turn AI on applicants: Caltech checks research projects for “authenticity”, Georgia Tech reads transcripts

CNN reported on 16 November that college admissions offices are now using AI themselves while scrambling to set rules for applicants who use it. Caltech’s dean of undergraduate admissions, Ashley Pallie, said “students are moving faster than admissions offices.” Caltech is launching an AI tool to check the “authenticity” of applicants’ research projects; even with 200 essay readers, the school had been sending decisions later and later. Georgia Tech is using AI to review transfer students’ transcripts. An MIT economist warned that if many colleges use the same models they could end up favouring a narrow type of applicant, and Spelman’s president worried that systems trained on past decisions could disadvantage students who don’t fit historical patterns. WGBH reported on 13 November that this is happening with little outside oversight, while applicants worry about being wrongly flagged as AI writers. The Common App treats submitting substantive AI-generated content as fraud but does not define how much AI help is acceptable, so rules still differ from college to college. This is a fallback item from about 10–14 days ago.

Sources: CNN via NBC Palm Springs, CNN via KRDO, WGBH

3. California county officials tell districts to run their own AI models instead of relying on vendors’ clouds

At the CITE26 conference in California, two Santa Barbara County education officials urged districts to build their own AI capacity instead of sending everything to commercial cloud services, GovTech reported. Their main argument was privacy. Models run locally or on district-controlled infrastructure could safely handle sensitive records such as special-education IEPs, which many districts will not send to outside vendors. They also suggested starting with administrative work rather than student-facing chatbots, because the payoff is clearer and the risk lower. The advice comes as California districts are split between moratoriums and managed adoption, and as the University of California and Cal State systems build their own AI infrastructure. Owning the infrastructure would also give districts more leverage in disputes like Google’s AI-or-no-Search policy. The details come from GovTech’s conference write-up, which could not be opened from the cloud runner, so this summary relies on its search-indexed excerpt. This is a fallback item from the past week.

Sources: GovTech (CITE26), GovTech (CITE26 companion session)

4. Buffalo makes AI training for teachers a regional workforce priority

Education Week profiled the Buffalo, New York region on 16 November as an example of a region treating AI training for teachers as economic policy rather than ordinary professional development. The nonprofit TechBuffalo is trying to build a pool of young people with AI and tech skills to support local growth. It is also connecting K-12 educators with University at Buffalo researchers to work out how best to teach students the AI knowledge and skills they will need for future careers. EdWeek ran the profile in a package on teacher AI training. That package found teachers want hands-on practice and frank discussion of cheating and other drawbacks, and that turnout is low when sessions are optional. The Buffalo approach links training to a regional jobs pipeline rather than one-off tool demos, a model other regions could copy as federal grant priorities favour AI-related teacher training. Only the article’s opening was indexed, so programme size and funding are not confirmed. This is a fallback item from about 11 days ago.

Sources: Education Week, Education Week (training package)

5. Malaysia’s classroom AI push meets a reality check over thinking skills, rural access and safeguards

The South China Morning Post examined whether Malaysia’s fast push to put AI in classrooms can actually teach children to think. The worry is that students will trade real understanding for quick answers. The ministry says more than 329,000 teachers have now had AI training, and it plans to bring AI literacy and basics into the school curriculum from next year. Critics are asking for safeguards first. A New Straits Times letter this month says students must be taught to question AI output, not just use it. Malay Mail commentators want more oral defences, in-class writing and projects where students explain their reasoning. Teach for Malaysia has stressed tools built specifically for children because chatbots make things up, and earlier reporting found rural schools still struggle with unstable internet. The debate is a common one across Southeast Asia, where national AI-education targets are moving faster than evidence on learning. This is a fallback item from this month. Only the SCMP article’s headline and summary were indexed, so the supporting detail comes from related Malaysian coverage.

Sources: South China Morning Post, New Straits Times (letter), The Star (teacher training), Malay Mail (assessment)
