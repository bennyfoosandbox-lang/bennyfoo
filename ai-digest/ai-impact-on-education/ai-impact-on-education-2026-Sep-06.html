1. England makes AI-generated deepfakes a mainstream school-safeguarding issue

Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE) 2026, the Department for Education's statutory safeguarding guidance for every school and college in England, took effect on 1 September 2026 with AI folded directly into its safeguarding framework for the first time. The guidance adopts "nudes and semi-nudes" as its umbrella term for under-18 imagery and explicitly extends it to self-generated, digitally altered, and entirely AI-generated content — including deepfakes — so a safeguarding incident can now exist even when no pupil ever took or shared an original photo. Schools must fold AI-enabled harms into existing safeguarding, online-safety, and digital-literacy policies and training, covering both support for victims and response to alleged perpetrators, alongside separate KCSIE 2026 changes making mobile-phone-free the default and tightening filtering, monitoring, and volunteer vetting. It's a notable shift from AI-in-education guidance being mostly about classroom use and academic integrity to treating AI-generated imagery of children as a routine child-protection scenario schools must actively plan for.

Sources: EdTech Innovation Hub, UK Safer Internet Centre, Farrer & Co, 9ine

2. A UK university goes all-in: Copilot for every student and staff member, campus-wide

The University of Leicester has switched on full Microsoft 365 Copilot access for its entire community — more than 21,000 students and 4,000 staff — starting this September, becoming one of the first institutions in the UK's "Microsoft Frontier University" program and one of the first UK universities to deploy Copilot at that scale rather than to a pilot group. The rollout is bundled with policy revisions, staff training, and assessment redesign, and Leicester is framing it as reinforcing its existing model of 100 hours of employer-linked, work-related learning built into every undergraduate degree, rather than a bolt-on productivity tool. It is a sharper bet than most universities are making: many institutions are still restricting or piloting generative AI access while running parallel academic-integrity reviews, whereas Leicester is wiring AI into every account by default and dealing with the governance, cost, and integrity questions as they arise. The move puts a concrete number behind the higher-ed AI story — a five-figure user base going live on one platform on one date — rather than the incremental campus-by-campus rollouts seen elsewhere this year.

Sources: Microsoft UK Stories, University of Leicester, EdTech Innovation Hub, Education Awards

3. A strike averted, and AI clauses land in a big-district teacher contract

Seattle Public Schools and the Seattle Education Association reached a tentative contract late on 1 September 2026, hours before the new school year, resolving all 52 issues on the bargaining table and averting a repeat of past strike disruption. Alongside pay, staffing, and safety provisions — including new school psychologist positions and expanded counselor funding — the union says the deal establishes clear guidelines on how AI can be used in teaching and in schools, one of the first times a major-district labor contract has spelled out AI terms rather than leaving the question to district policy alone. The agreement still needs ratification votes from both union membership and the Seattle School Board before it's final. It's a concrete data point in a story mostly told through surveys until now — Education Week has noted that AI is reshaping teaching faster than labor contracts can keep up with — and shows a teachers' union treating AI use as a bargainable working condition rather than solely a classroom-policy matter for administrators to set unilaterally.

Sources: KING 5, Capitol Hill Seattle, Fox News, The Center Square

4. A viral AI-generated "Cat in the Hat" threat trend forces schools across three states into lockdown-style responses

Tennessee's Office of Homeland Security is tracking a spreading online trend of unsettling AI-generated images and videos depicting the Dr. Seuss "Cat in the Hat" character menacing homes and schools, some posts naming real children or showing real addresses, after similar posts prompted heightened security at schools in Oklahoma and Texas. In Tennessee, a Warren County High School student has been criminally charged following an investigation into an AI-generated image tied to the trend, while in Oklahoma, Hugo Public Schools swept its campus and added police presence after a Snapchat post referencing the character and listing area schools. Authorities are urging anyone who encounters the content to report it through local law enforcement or Tennessee's SafeTN system, treating AI-generated imagery invoking real students and locations as a credible-threat workflow rather than a meme to ignore. The episode is a fresh, concrete instance of a pattern schools have been bracing for all year — AI image and video generation tools turning into vectors for hoax or intimidation threats that still trigger real lockdowns, police sweeps, and now criminal charges.

Sources: WSMV Nashville, Fox17 Nashville, Fox Baltimore

5. Update: California makes it law — a human has to be the instructor of record, not an AI

Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 928 on 27 August 2026, moving California's "no AI professors" bill from the "sent to his desk" stage covered here last month into actual law: every California State University course must have a human being as its instructor of record, closing the door on AI systems being used to formally replace teaching faculty. State Senator Sabrina Cervantes, the bill's author, framed it as protecting "the personal connection and the guidance that CSU faculty deliver," with the bill sponsored by the California Faculty Association and the California Federation of Labor Unions after passing the Legislature with unanimous Senate support. It's a direct, legislated answer to a fear that has shaped much of this year's higher-ed AI coverage — instructors being displaced rather than assisted by AI — and gives California's 23-campus CSU system, one of the largest public university systems in the country, an explicit statutory floor under how far AI can go in the classroom. Unlike guidance or frameworks that schools can interpret loosely, this is now a hard legal requirement CSU campuses must comply with.

Sources: Senator Sabrina Cervantes (SD-31), Office of Governor Gavin Newsom
