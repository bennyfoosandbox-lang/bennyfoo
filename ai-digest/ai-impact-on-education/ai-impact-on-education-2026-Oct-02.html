1. Texas's education agency helped pilot Alpha School's AI platform after the state board balked

A Texas Tribune/ProPublica investigation reports that Education Commissioner Mike Morath and his staff helped steer Alpha School–linked AI programs into public districts even after the State Board of Education voted 10–3 against the company's charter bid over AI teaching, screen-time and test-score concerns. Emails show agency staff drafting a one-page explainer for districts and offering introductions, and at least three districts (Houston, Fort Davis and Aldine) launched pilots, including about 800 students in Fort Davis and roughly 300 across four Aldine middle schools. At least five other districts were approached and declined. The story matters because it shows a state agency advancing an unproven AI model through a side door while Alpha's own Austin school reportedly saw only 21% of students pass state exams.

Sources: Houston Public Media / Texas Tribune–ProPublica

2. Boston bets on AI fluency for every student while New York City pauses AI through eighth grade

GovTech lays out a stark split between two big districts. Boston is building AI literacy for all students, with a $1 million public-private partnership, a nine-member industry advisory board, 25 trained teacher ambassadors and a UMass Boston–designed 7–12 curriculum, while recommending no direct AI use before grade 3. New York City has imposed a moratorium on student-facing generative AI from pre-K through eighth grade for nearly 600,000 students, disabling AI in 38 core curriculum products and nearly 40 other approved programs. NYC high schoolers will instead get five vetted pilots capped at 45 minutes a week under teacher supervision, making the two cities a live comparison of fluency-first versus study-first policy.

Sources: GovTech

3. Google's AI is nearly impossible to wall off from US schools, even where districts ban it

Tech Brew, summarising a Wall Street Journal report based on interviews with more than 60 teachers, officials, clinicians and parents, argues that moratoriums in New York City and Los Angeles are undercut by Google's reach: its education software serves over 170 million students and teachers, and AI summaries and AI Mode sit atop ordinary Search results. Google does not currently let schools turn those features off, and Los Angeles reportedly had to build its own tool to block the Search chatbot. A children's-safety nonprofit's test found the integrated AI completed every homework assignment typed in, a result Google disputes. Gigazine's roundup adds teacher anecdotes of students treating AI summaries as their primary source and of rising resistance from parents.

Sources: Tech Brew, Gigazine

4. Survey of 797 Indian government-school teachers: 72% positive on AI, but over half have entered student data into tools

A JanAI survey (an initiative of the Head Held High Foundation) of 797 government-school teachers across Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka, Jharkhand and Maharashtra found 72% feel positive about AI and 26% already use it regularly, mostly to simplify concepts and create examples and worksheets rather than to grade. Adoption is running ahead of infrastructure, with many teachers using personal phones in schools without computer labs. Some 64% worry about student overdependence, and more than half of AI-using teachers have typed student names, marks or photos into tools, a privacy gap the authors say only clear institutional rules and training can close. Concern about AI replacing teachers was nearly absent.

Sources: YourStory

5. C&EN: STEM instructors weigh AI's help against lost problem-solving practice

Chemical & Engineering News surveys how college STEM instructors are coping, noting problem sets that once took hours now finish in minutes and lab reports that can be drafted wholesale by a chatbot. It cites a Digital Education Council survey of 3,174 faculty in 35 countries in which 35% focus on teaching critical, responsible use and 20% actively integrate AI, plus a Gallup–Lumina finding that 52% of students have some classes with no clear AI policy. A review of 19 studies suggests AI can raise student interest through tailored content, so instructors are talking openly about why to set AI aside while building skills, reserving it for non-core tasks. The takeaway: policy clarity and explicit conversation matter more than blanket bans in the sciences.

Sources: Chemical & Engineering News
