1. Houston's biggest suburban district maps a grade-by-grade AI on-ramp — no chatbots before 7th grade

Katy ISD, one of the largest school systems in the Houston metro, is rolling out a new AI framework for the 2026-27 school year that bars K-6 students from generative AI chat tools like Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini entirely. Seventh graders get supervised access only when a teacher directs a specific task, and eighth-through-twelfth graders can use district-approved generative tools for narrowly defined educational purposes with prior teacher permission. Elementary classrooms aren't AI-free, though: teachers can still use district-approved instructional platforms — reading tools like Amira Learning and Writable — that use AI behind the scenes to personalize lessons, just not chat interfaces students interact with directly. The framework was presented to the board on July 20 but has driven a fresh wave of local coverage this week as the district year gets underway, and it lands as a middle path between blanket bans (NYC, LAUSD) and open-access policies elsewhere — a phased, grade-gated model more districts appear to be converging on.

Sources: Community Impact, Houston Chronicle, ABC13 Houston, WinsSolutions

2. New NBER study: an AI math tutor that forces kids to slow down beats one that just hands over answers

A randomized experiment with more than 6,000 Tennessee middle schoolers, circulated as an NBER working paper on August 17 by researchers from the University of Toronto and Penn's Wharton School, tested an AI tutor called "Numi" on fractions practice. When Numi walked students through a mistake step-by-step and then required them to solve the same type of problem correctly three times in a row before advancing — a mastery-learning design — students learned modestly more than under a faster mode that simply showed the correct answer and moved on. The researchers think the gain came specifically from forcing students to slow down and process their errors, not from AI being faster or more available; one co-author cautioned the study tested only four tutor configurations, so it isn't proof that mastery learning is the single best way to pair AI with math instruction. It's a useful counterpoint to the fall's gloomier AI-and-learning headlines: the technology's effect seems to hinge heavily on the pedagogy it's wrapped in, not on the model itself.

Sources: The Hechinger Report, FutureEd, KQED MindShift, EdWorkingPapers (working paper)

3. Districts are "vibe-coding" their own AI tools — and ed-tech vendors are starting to sweat

EdWeek Market Brief's September analysis argues that school districts building their own software with AI coding assistants is quietly becoming a competitive threat to education-technology vendors. The piece centers on Washington's Peninsula School District, where administrators with no computer-science background — paying roughly $200 a month for Claude Code — have replaced commercial subscriptions with homegrown tools: a lesson-feedback app called LessonLens, a career-and-technical-education budget tool, and a financial-reporting function that used to cost $75,000 a year, rebuilt in about 45 minutes once the underlying infrastructure existed. The district's running estimate of savings has climbed from an initial $220,000 a year to roughly $315,000–$325,000. Market Brief's warning to vendors is direct: districts that can prototype their own solution in an afternoon will expect vendors to justify their price against more than "we built it first," and the risk migrates to whoever leaves quality control, data security and staff turnover unaddressed once administrators become part-time developers.

Sources: EdWeek Market Brief, K-12 Dive, Peninsula School District

4. A ten-foundation coalition opens a $10M call for grants on "who controls AI" — education groups are an explicit target

Humanity AI, a five-year, $500 million pooled philanthropic initiative backed by the Ford, MacArthur, Mellon, Mozilla, Omidyar Network, Packard, Doris Duke, Lumina, Kapor and Siegel Family foundations, opened a second open call for grants on September 10, this time up to $10 million for US-based nonprofits, with individual awards of $75,000 to $1 million over up to three years. The call explicitly invites proposals from education, labor-and-economy, and humanities-and-arts organizations working to give communities — not just tech companies or school-system administrators — a say in how AI is built, governed and used; a companion informational webinar for applicants ran today, September 14, with applications due October 21. It follows a first round announced in May that put $18 million into a dozen groups spanning AI governance, education, journalism and worker protections. The move is a signal that major funders now see "who shapes AI in the classroom and beyond" as urgent enough to bankroll grassroots and community organizations directly, rather than leaving the agenda to vendor partnerships between school systems and AI companies.

Sources: Humanity AI, NC State College of Education, Siegel Family Endowment
