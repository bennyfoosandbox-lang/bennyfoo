1. A Houston student beats an "F" for alleged AI cheating on appeal

University of Houston-Downtown senior Mark Pieterson was given an F in a music appreciation course after his professor said his journal entries and discussion posts were copy-pasted AI output, even though he held A-level marks elsewhere in the class. He lost the first two appeal stages, the professor and then the department chair, but a student discipline committee of four deans ruled in his favor, citing concerns about the reliability and consistency of the evidence behind the accusation. The case matters because the appeal path began with the accuser, and because it shows how thin AI-detection evidence can be when a grade and a degree are on the line. Expect more students to contest such findings as detector-based accusations multiply.

Sources: FOX 26 Houston

2. Washington high schoolers walk out: "Pencils, not prompts"

More than 100 students at Evergreen High School in Vancouver, Washington left sixth period on Thursday to demand that the district set clear rules for AI use. Organizers argued for a single standard for staff and students, objecting that teachers can use AI for lesson planning and grading while students may not use it for anything. The district says it is drafting a policy but its published timeline runs through March 2027 for research, a public draft in the second half of 2027 and a final policy by August 2028, leaving the 2024 state guidance as the only rulebook meanwhile. The episode is a rare case of students, not adults, pushing for AI guardrails, and it exposes how slowly district policy moves against classroom adoption.

Sources: KGW (Portland)

3. AI literacy without screens: a Kenyan pilot teaches it through printed storybooks

An Arizona State University master's student and Mastercard Foundation Scholar, Martin Ndegwa, planned a digital AI-literacy curriculum for Kenyan schools but found rural primary schools in Kilifi County with unreliable electricity, few computers and limited internet, and in some places no nearby library within 30 kilometers. He pivoted to six Shomani Reading Clubs and two illustrated storybooks, drafted with help from an ASU research group's Instructional Agents system, that teach children to question a phone assistant's confident answers, check evidence and consult elders and teachers. The pilot is a small but pointed counterpoint to screen-first AI education: it treats critical thinking as the prerequisite and shows AI being used upstream to make low-cost print materials for learners who cannot reach chatbots.

Sources: ASU Engineering News

4. Three UT San Antonio undergrads build the campus AI agent themselves

UT San Antonio's Academic Innovation unit let three undergraduates design, build and launch MEEP (My Educational Engagement Partner), a Copilot Studio agent that fields campus support questions from a custom knowledge base. The project ran from late February to an early-April working agent on roughly $4,000, covering three licenses, three paid internships and laptops, and the students set the guardrails themselves: no personal data, no health advice and no help with coursework. All three have since landed internships in California, Illinois and New York. The model is notable as a low-cost way to turn students from AI consumers into builders at a time when entry-level openings requiring no experience have reportedly fallen 73% in four years.

Sources: UT San Antonio Academic Innovation

5. Update: students tell of grades sliding after Google's Gemini arrived in their classrooms

Building on the Wall Street Journal reporting already covered on Google's reach into schools, a Futurism roundup adds the student accounts. A Pennsylvania eighth-grader said Google's assistant "slowed my thinking down" and that he struggled on tests without it, ending the year failing a final despite earlier B-range math grades; a Miami art-history teacher said grades fell after the district enabled Gemini for all students; and a 15-year-old said her grades recovered after she quit the tool cold turkey. The piece frames the push as a customer-acquisition play, citing internal 2018 Google documents about a "pipeline of future users." These are anecdotes, not controlled evidence, but they sharpen the debate over default-on chatbots in school accounts.

Sources: Futurism
