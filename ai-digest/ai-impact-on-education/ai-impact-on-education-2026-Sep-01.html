1. University of Tampa makes AI literacy a required class for every sophomore

The University of Tampa began its fall semester this week with a new mandatory one-credit AI literacy course for all sophomores, replacing a digital-literacy class that used to teach Python. Built with higher-ed platform Codio, the self-paced course focuses less on tool mechanics and more on judgment: verifying AI outputs, recognizing hallucinations and knowing when not to rely on a model at all, a response to employers who increasingly expect graduates to already work fluently with AI. It's a sharper stance than most universities have taken so far — rather than leaving AI literacy to individual professors' syllabi, Tampa is making it a graduation-track requirement campus-wide from day one. The move lands alongside this digest's ongoing coverage of universities racing to formalize AI policy before students arrive, but stands out for building literacy into the core curriculum rather than just restricting or permitting AI use in existing courses.

Sources: Tampa Bay Times, GovTech, University of Tampa

2. Khan Academy and Google.org give Khanmigo the ability to draw its own diagrams

Khan Academy shipped a back-to-school update to its Khanmigo tutor built around a six-month Google.org Fellowship, in which a team of six Google engineers worked alongside Khan Academy's own product staff to bring Gemini's multimodal reasoning into the tutor. Khanmigo can now generate real-time interactive diagrams — charts, geometric shapes, parallel coordinate plots — when it judges a visual would help a student work through a math or science problem, and the diagrams respond live: dragging a vertex or line segment prompts Khanmigo to recognize the change and adjust its explanation. A redesigned practice tool also gives teachers final control over which AI-generated questions actually reach students, addressing a common teacher complaint about ceding assignment quality to a black box. The launch comes just weeks after this digest covered NBER's two-year Khanmigo study finding access was nearly universal but engagement stayed thin — this update is a direct bet that richer, more interactive content is what was missing.

Sources: Khan Academy Blog, Google, GCN

3. Israel opens its school year with AI-taught English rolling out to all 180 middle schools

As roughly 2.6 million Israeli pupils started the 2026-27 school year on September 1, the Education Ministry expanded its "English for Everyone" program — personalized, AI-driven English instruction — from a 28-school pilot across 17 local authorities to all 180 middle schools nationwide, aimed at seventh and eighth graders. More than 12,000 English and STEM teachers are being trained to work alongside the AI system, while eighth and ninth graders will also take a new 20-hour course on informed AI use, critical thinking and spotting misinformation as part of the broader curriculum, which adds financial literacy for ninth graders and expanded STEM content. Officials frame the push explicitly as a response to a severe English-teacher shortage and as part of a strategic goal to rank among the top three OECD countries for AI-based education. It's one of the largest single-country rollouts of curriculum-embedded AI tutoring this digest has tracked, notable for pairing the technology with mandatory AI-literacy instruction for students rather than deploying it as an unaccompanied classroom tool.

Sources: The Jerusalem Post, JNS, The Times of Israel

4. China's next Five-Year Plan puts AI companions into special-education classrooms

Seven Chinese government departments led by the Ministry of Education jointly issued an action plan on August 31 for special-education development covering the 15th Five-Year Plan period (2026-2030), which for the first time formally directs AI into that specific corner of the system. The plan calls for "intelligent learning companions" and AI-assisted teachers, explores AI-based classroom assistance for students with disabilities, and specifically targets home-based AI instruction for students whose disabilities are too severe to attend school in person. It sets a 2030 target of a "high-quality, integrated and inclusive" special-education system built on stronger digital and intelligent infrastructure. This is distinct from — and more targeted than — the general K-12 AI mandate this digest covered in July aiming for "basically universal" AI instruction by 2030; here China is applying the same national planning machinery specifically to the highest-need, hardest-to-reach student population, a segment most other countries' AI-in-education pushes have largely left out of the conversation so far.

Sources: Xinhua, China.org.cn, People's Daily Online

5. Global poll: Indians overwhelmingly back AI in classrooms while wanting social media banned for kids

Ipsos's Education Monitor 2026 — a multi-country survey of public attitudes toward education technology — found Indian respondents drawing a sharp line between different technologies: 78% believe children under 14 should be banned from social media both in and out of school, and 57% support banning smartphones in schools outright, yet only 26% think AI tools like ChatGPT should be banned in the classroom, with 61% actively opposed to such a ban. The gap suggests Indian parents and educators see generative AI as fundamentally different from social media and smartphones — a learning tool worth preserving rather than a distraction to eliminate — even as they grow more anxious about children's screen time generally. It echoes a pattern this digest has seen in US polling (the TCF/Scripps survey in August found Americans wanting stronger AI-in-schools rules, not a ban), suggesting the global public consensus is coalescing around "regulate AI in education, don't prohibit it" rather than the blanket restrictions being applied to social media. The findings arrive as India continues to develop formal national guidance on AI in schools separate from its tighter social-media and smartphone rules.

Sources: Adgully, BestMediaInfo, StratNews Global
