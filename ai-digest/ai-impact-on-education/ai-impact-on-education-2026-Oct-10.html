1. Oak’s Aila AI lesson planner cuts primary teachers’ prep time by about 49 minutes a week in an EEF trial

Results published on 6 October from a randomised trial run by the Education Endowment Foundation and NFER found that primary teachers using Oak National Academy’s Aila lesson assistant spent about 76% of the planning time of a comparison group, roughly 2.5 hours a week against 3.3. Reports describe 464 Year 3–6 teachers in 108 schools, and independent assessors rated the resulting resources no worse than those made without the tool. The catch is adoption: 81% opened Aila at least once, but it supported only about 7% of lessons in a typical week, usage fell over the ten weeks, and under a fifth said it meaningfully changed their teaching. It is one of the few high-security-rated trials showing a real, if modest, teacher-time gain from generative AI.

Sources: EdTech Innovation Hub, Resultsense, Education Endowment Foundation

2. Russia will make an AI and information-security course compulsory for grades 5–11

Education Minister Sergey Kravtsov said on 9 October that all Russian schools will teach a compulsory “Artificial Intelligence and Information Security” subject to students in grades 5 to 11, starting next academic year (2027 in the report’s framing). It places Russia alongside India (AI and computational thinking from Class 3 in 2026–27) and the UAE (AI as a K-12 subject since 2025–26) in moving AI from elective to core curriculum. Details such as hours per week and teacher preparation were not in the coverage we could read, so treat the specifics as preliminary.

Sources: Izvestia (English), Vietnam.vn

3. Google extends its free AI Educator Series to about 650,000 teachers in the UK and Ireland

Announced on 5 October, the expansion brings Google’s Gemini-centred training to primary, secondary and higher-education staff through 15–45 minute modules with micro-credentials, plus a series of in-person Gemini Roadshow days across England. The series first launched for six million US educators and has since reached India and Korea, so this is an incremental development of a programme we covered earlier. Commentary notes Google does not say how many of the 650,000 it expects to complete the training, and that free training from one vendor is still training in that vendor’s tools.

Sources: EdTech Innovation Hub, Google Blog, Resultsense

4. byteXL raises $9M to build AI skilling into Indian engineering-college degrees

Hyderabad-based byteXL announced a $9 million round on 7 October led by Elevar Equity, with Kalaari Capital and the Michael & Susan Dell Foundation participating. It sells to colleges rather than individual learners, folding tech and AI training into regular coursework at engineering institutions in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities. The company plans to grow from 40 to 120 institutions and from 2 lakh to 5 lakh students across 15 states within two years. Outlets differ on the founding year and on whether to call it a Series B.

Sources: YourStory, Inc42, Business Standard, Deccan Chronicle

5. AI for Math Fund grows to $35.1M as 22 proposals win grants for AI theorem proving and math tools

Renaissance Philanthropy and XTX Markets added $17.1 million on 8 October, lifting total commitments to $35.1 million after applications far outran the planned $10.5 million Core Grants round. Twenty-two proposals from 30 organisations were funded at $100,000–$1 million each for 12 to 24 months, covering AI-assisted theorem proving, datasets, evaluation tools and ways for mathematicians to understand AI-generated proofs, including a new Simons Institute research pod. This is research funding rather than classroom news, but it shapes the tools and the mathematical-reasoning benchmarks that future AI tutors will lean on. Sources disagree on the applicant count (400+ versus 470+).

Sources: EdTech Innovation Hub, Renaissance Philanthropy
