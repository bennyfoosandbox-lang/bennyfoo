1. Update: Google's K-12 plan could leave schools choosing between AI search and no search at all

A day after Google said it would switch Gemini off for student accounts by default, the fuller picture is drawing fire. EdSource reports that from January Google plans to make its "Guided Learning" AI the default search experience for K-12 accounts, with AI Mode and AI Overviews staying in place, so a district that wants no AI could end up with students having no Search at all (separate products such as Gemini and Gemini Notebook can still be switched off). Los Angeles Unified says it is reviewing the implications, Schools Beyond Screens calls it a "hostile ultimatum", the EFF's Rory Mir notes that dropping Google is hard given long contracts and training costs, and Common Sense Media says losing Search also costs a media-literacy tool. Coverage elsewhere, including a Montana district reacting and critics such as Khan and Fairplay, shows the row widening beyond California.

Sources: EdSource, University Herald, Montana Right Now, Shattered

2. Seattle tightens student tech and closes a Gemini loophole while parents demand a full AI pause

At a board discussion this week Seattle Public Schools announced it will block YouTube for K-5 and Minecraft for all grades from Monday, and that officials fixed a loophole that let students chat with Gemini, which is now banned districtwide (previously K-2 only). The district still backs approved tools such as MagicSchool AI and Microsoft Copilot and sketches an AI-literacy path: no AI in K-2, introductions from third grade, teacher-guided bias checks by sixth, and draft feedback in high school. Parent group Seattle Families for Intentional Tech rallied on 4 November to demand a pause on all student-facing generative AI, and board director Evan Briggs said there is no data that MagicSchool raised reading scores. It is a clear case of a large district trying to expand AI literacy and restrict screens at once.

Sources: KUOW, KING 5, KIRO 7

3. South Korea's president orders a smart-glasses crackdown two weeks before the college entrance exam

President Lee Jae-myung told the government on 5 November to make a special effort to block cheating with high-tech devices such as AI smart glasses, to train proctors better, to tighten on-site response and to protect proctors from unreasonable complaints, with the 2027 College Scholastic Ability Test two weeks away. The same day Seoul's education office began checking teacher-exam candidates for cameras and displays, with inspections set to expand, and the University of Seoul filed police complaints against two test-takers caught wearing or carrying AI-equipped glasses. Times Higher Education reports Seoul is vowing prosecution, framing tougher penalties as a way to uphold fairness in admissions. This moves the story we tracked in October from teacher surveys and school checks to criminal enforcement at the national level.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily, Times Higher Education, Chosun Biz (Seoul teacher exam), Chosun Biz (University of Seoul), Chosun Daily

4. Bangladesh's universities use generative AI widely but still have no national guideline

The Daily Star reports that Bangladeshi universities have made little progress on rules for generative AI, even as professors at Rajshahi, Jagannath and BRAC describe students submitting polished work they cannot explain or defend. The University Grants Commission has issued no guidance; its chairman says one policy cannot suit every university or department but hopes a guideline will be ready by January. Dhaka University formed a drafting committee about two months ago and Daffodil International University approved revised guidelines this year. The paper cites a 2025 study of 343 universities in five countries in which 52% left AI use to individual instructors, and a Dhaka education researcher argues teachers need training because "AI is a reality".

Sources: The Daily Star

5. Op-ed: West African exams must be redesigned before AI cheating outruns policy

A Modern Ghana commentary argues that exam malpractice in West Africa, historically about smuggled notes, now includes devices that can write essays, solve maths and generate code in seconds. The author says phone bans and surveillance alone will not protect integrity, and that AI detectors should not anchor integrity policy because they are inconsistent. Instead he urges assessments that reward reasoning over answers, more continuous assessment, teacher training in assignment design, and clear regional rules that account for unequal access to devices and AI subscriptions. It is an opinion piece rather than new data, but it shows the assessment-redesign debate reaching WAEC-style high-stakes systems.

Sources: Modern Ghana
