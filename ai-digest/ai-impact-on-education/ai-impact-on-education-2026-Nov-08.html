1. UK: 300+ teachers, parents and doctors demand a pause on government AI-tutor plans

More than 300 teachers, parents, academics and doctors have written to Andy Burnham and Education Secretary Lucy Powell asking them to halt plans to bring AI tutoring tools into schools, the Daily Mirror reports. The government’s plan is a controlled trial starting next year that could put the tools in front of up to 450,000 disadvantaged children, intended to build evidence on effectiveness and safety. Signatories argue that children should not be the test group for unproven products, which mirrors the pushback seen in the US over classroom AI. It makes England’s tutor programme the clearest live test of whether state-backed AI tutoring can survive organised professional opposition.

Sources: Daily Mirror

2. Fields medallist Richard Borcherds stops grading Berkeley homework for correctness

UC Berkeley mathematician and 1998 Fields Medal winner Richard Borcherds has stopped marking homework for correctness, giving credit for completion instead, and scrapped online tests. His final grades now rest mainly on in-person exams with no electronic devices. His reasoning is that chatbot answers are indistinguishable from student work, that grading for correctness creates a heavy incentive to cheat, and that AI-detection software is unreliable. It is a high-profile example of a pattern now spreading in universities: assessment is being moved back to supervised, offline settings rather than policed with detectors.

Sources: The Standard (HK), citing the New York Post

3. Sydney school strips laptops from years 7–10 as Australia’s “digital education revolution” unwinds

The Sydney Morning Herald reports that a run of policy announcements has effectively expelled screens from Australian classrooms, with Marian Catholic College removing laptops and one-to-one devices from years 7 to 10 from next year. The school says devices hurt focused attention and is moving to explicit-instruction teaching over inquiry-based approaches. The story widens the national picture beyond AI rules for assessment: schools are now questioning whether ubiquitous classroom technology helps learning at all. It lands the same week as the federal ‘digital detox’ push to cut screen time and limit AI use at school.

Sources: The Sydney Morning Herald, ABC News (Australia)

4. Australia: top students fear AI is eroding their edge as an assessment-integrity crisis turns “acute”

The Australian reports that high-achieving students worry AI is undermining their ability to stand out, while more than half of surveyed teachers and school leaders back a temporary freeze on take-home assessments. Former NSW education standards chief Tom Alegounarias warns the integrity crisis in school assessment has become ‘acute’. The finding matters because it shows the harm is not only cheating but lost signal: when anyone can produce polished work, strong students lose a way to demonstrate ability. Only the headline and summary were readable behind the paywall, so detailed survey figures are not reported here.

Sources: The Australian

5. Microsoft Philippines pushes Copilot and teacher training into public schools

Microsoft Philippines is working with the Department of Education, the ICT department, Audentes Technologies and ChildFund Philippines to widen AI use in public schools, BusinessWorld reports. The programme targets 2,000 teachers for training and certification, following an initial session with about 100 senior-high teachers, and covers access to Microsoft Copilot for 1 million public-school teachers. Training is paired with material on responsible use, risk assessment and digital safety. It shows the vendor-led route to classroom AI in emerging markets, where national rollouts are arriving just as richer countries debate pulling back.

Sources: BusinessWorld
