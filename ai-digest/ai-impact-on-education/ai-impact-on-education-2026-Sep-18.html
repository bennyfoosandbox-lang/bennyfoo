1. The UK tells every university lecturer to become an "AI trailblazer"

Universities UK published its Future Jobs Roadmap this week, shaped by conversations with roughly 200 employers, business groups and universities, and one of its central commitments is that AI tools be made accessible to every undergraduate — with every course assigned a designated "AI trailblazer" lecturer to guide students toward using it well. The University of Surrey is positioning itself as the model for what that looks like in practice: starting this month, every one of its degree programmes, from foundation year through postgraduate, is being redesigned so AI is taught in discipline-specific ways rather than bolted on as a generic skill — what counts as good evidence, risk or originality differs for engineering, health sciences, English or the creative arts, and Surrey is teaching AI through each discipline's own standards. Surrey is also launching an AI in Education Framework this September to govern how AI factors into teaching and assessment, with assessment increasingly judging students on their process rather than just a submitted output. The University of Cumbria is among the institutions publicly backing the wider roadmap. It's a more systemic bet than earlier campus-wide tool rollouts like Leicester's Copilot deployment — treating AI fluency as a matter of curriculum redesign rather than a licensing decision.

Sources: University of Surrey, Times Higher Education, Wonkhe, University of Cumbria

2. Update: Microsoft turns its teachers'-union deal into a nationwide "Privacy & Safety Standard for Schools"

A week after Microsoft and the country's two largest teachers unions announced a legally enforceable AI-safety agreement, Microsoft followed up with a company blog post formalizing a "Privacy & Safety Standard for Schools" covering its education products — set to take effect November 1 and available to every school district nationwide, not only ones affiliated with the AFT or UFT. The standard commits Microsoft to not using student or educator data to train its AI models, not tracking students, not selling or using their data for advertising, and not letting its AI make school decisions without a human in the loop. The AFT says it's now in active talks with OpenAI and Anthropic to extend similar enforceable commitments beyond Microsoft alone. The announcement lands the same week NYC's and LAUSD's own student-facing AI moratoriums are already in effect, giving other districts a company-level baseline to point to even where they've paused classroom AI use themselves.

Sources: Microsoft (official blog), WHIO

3. Computer science majors just had their worst year in memory — and AI job fears are the reason students give

New National Student Clearinghouse data shows computer science enrollment "cooling" hard: four-year institutions lost about 53,000 CS majors — an 8.4% drop — in spring 2026, with two-year colleges down an even steeper 11.2%, even as overall undergraduate enrollment grew 1.3% over the same period. The decline cuts across nearly every CS specialization and extends a slide that began in fall 2025, when computer-science and programming enrollment fell more than 10% year over year. Surveyed students are explicit about why: a Superhuman survey found nearly 70% worried AI would make it harder to find a job, and 22% said they'd already changed majors over it, while separate Lumina Foundation/Gallup polling found 13-19% of degree-seekers switched fields for the same reason. Students aren't fleeing STEM broadly, though — majors tied to occupations seen as harder for AI to automate, like healthcare and engineering, are absorbing much of the demand computer science is losing.

Sources: National Student Clearinghouse, The Chronicle of Higher Education, Futurism, EdSource

4. Detection is out, blue books are back: how colleges are actually trying to stop AI cheating now

A Cincinnati Edition panel on WVXU this week brought together an academic-integrity officer from UC San Diego and professors from Case Western Reserve and St. Thomas University to walk through what's replacing the failed experiment with AI detectors: in-class handwritten exams, oral defenses of written work, and assignments redesigned around a visible process rather than a single submittable output. It's part of a retreat that's been building for months — sales of the old pen-and-paper "blue book" exam are up as much as 80% at some universities since 2023, with Texas A&M, the University of Florida and UC Berkeley all reporting surging demand, and a growing number of professors switching from take-home essays to oral exams. Inside Higher Ed has tracked institutions moving away from AI-detection tools altogether, rather than trying to refine them, given their unreliable false-positive rates. The throughline across all of it: instead of trying to out-detect AI writing after the fact, professors are redesigning what counts as proof of learning in the first place.

Sources: WVXU, Fox News, Times Higher Education, Inside Higher Ed

5. Parents are showing up to IEP meetings with an AI tool of their own — and schools are having to adjust

A wave of recent coverage, from EdSurge to the Educational Freedom Institute, describes a fast-emerging dynamic in special education: parents are increasingly using AI chatbots to analyze their child's Individualized Education Program, generate questions, and prep for meetings — arming themselves with the same kind of tool districts have been using internally to help draft IEPs. Advocates frame it as a genuine equalizer for parents who previously had to hire an educational advocate or attorney to decode IEP jargon and push back effectively on a district's proposed services. It isn't friction-free: privacy researchers have flagged disclosure concerns since some of that advocacy work involves pasting a child's FERPA- and IDEA-protected evaluation details into a personal chatbot account. Guidance aimed at teachers stresses not treating an AI-armed parent defensively, verifying whatever the AI generates rather than dismissing it outright, and using the moment as an opening for more transparent dialogue about what services a child actually needs. It's the mirror image of a tension already drawing scrutiny when schools do the AI-assisted IEP drafting themselves — now the same technology is shifting leverage to the family side of the table.

Sources: EdSurge, Educational Freedom Institute, Complete AI Training
