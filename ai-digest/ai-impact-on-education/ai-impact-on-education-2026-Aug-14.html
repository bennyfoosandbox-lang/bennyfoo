1. Central Florida's school districts show just how differently "AI policy" can look next door

A district-by-district roundup published August 13 makes clear that Florida's push for every district to adopt a written AI policy hasn't produced one policy — it's produced half a dozen different bets. Orange County lets teachers opt in or out and requires parental notification before graded AI use; Brevard's late-July update to Policy 7540.08 lets the superintendent authorize AI use case by case; Osceola is handing Microsoft Copilot to every student 13 and older while running an 18-week "AI Fellows" teacher cohort to help shape further policy; Lake County is walking away from MagicSchool in favor of Google's Gemini built into Docs and Drive; and Sumter and Flagler each publish narrow, explicit lists of sanctioned uses (research, translation, accessibility support). The piece is a useful counterweight to the "states are mandating AI policies" framing we've tracked all summer — the mandate produces wildly different implementations depending on vendor relationships, budget and how much districts trust their own teachers.

Sources: ClickOrlando

2. Districts are spending billions on AI tools with almost no help figuring out which ones work

A Stateline investigation published August 12 — and quickly syndicated by outlets in Connecticut, Kansas, Missouri and Pennsylvania — finds school districts largely on their own when deciding which AI products are safe and worth the money, with the U.S. Department of Education offering districts little formal guidance on vetting purchases. The broader ed-tech market hit roughly $48 billion in 2024 and is projected to exceed $90 billion by 2030, with the AI slice alone forecast to grow from about $2.5 billion to more than $15 billion by 2033 — spending outpacing the evidence base on what actually helps students learn. Only a handful of states and groups have stepped into the gap, including the Southern Regional Education Board, which published a K-12 AI procurement and evaluation checklist last year. The piece also surfaces a concrete failure mode: teachers piloting AI tools noticed some systems misread or unfairly penalized English-language learners' work — a bias students themselves spotted before it became a teaching moment.

Sources: Stateline, Route Fifty, CT Mirror

3. Two days before the first bell, a Kansas district reversed its plan to ban AI outright

Shawnee Mission School District in Johnson County, Kansas had spent the summer leaning toward a blanket ban on generative AI across district devices, but the school board reversed course just two days before students returned August 12 — opting instead to let middle and high schoolers use built-in AI tools under direct teacher supervision, while younger students stay far more restricted. The district will roll out PICRAT, a research-based technology-integration framework, this fall to help teachers judge when AI genuinely supports learning versus when it's just doing the thinking for students, and updated handbooks now explicitly ban using AI to cheat, with penalties ranging from parent notification to loss of course credit. A broader, permanent policy is still being drafted by a community workgroup that will include parents, so this is an interim compromise rather than a settled answer. It's a vivid example of the last-minute whiplash several districts experienced writing AI rules this back-to-school season.

Sources: KCTV5 (Aug 10), KCTV5 (Aug 11), KSHB

4. Singapore's universities go all-in on campus-wide AI access, betting equity over restriction

On August 11, Singapore's two top universities took parallel but distinct bets on AI access. The National University of Singapore struck a strategic collaboration with OpenAI giving every student, faculty and staff member ChatGPT Edu access starting August 31. The same day, Singapore Management University unveiled an AI-in-Education Strategy — endorsed by its board back in February — giving every student free access to Google's Gemini Education Plus alongside new AI-focused courses in law, business, accountancy and computing. SMU frames the approach around three commitments: protecting core competencies like independent reasoning, deliberately building AI fluency, and strengthening distinctly human skills such as ethical judgment and collaboration, rather than treating blanket access as the finish line. Both universities are explicitly trying to close an affordability gap — making sure a student's AI fluency doesn't depend on what they can personally afford to subscribe to — a contrast to the piecemeal, restriction-first approach many U.S. districts adopted the same week.

Sources: OpenAI (NUS), SMU Newsroom

5. Three in four U.S. parents now say they're fine with AI in their kid's classroom

A National School Choice Awareness Foundation survey of 2,053 U.S. parents of school-aged children, fielded July 24–31 and released August 10, found 76% support at least some use of AI in schools, and 78% say a school's AI approach now factors into how they evaluate education options for their child — margin of error ±2.2 points at 95% confidence. The single biggest draw was personalization: 42% of parents cited AI's ability to tailor learning to a student's pace and needs, more than double the 21% who cited preparing kids for AI-driven careers, with smaller shares citing instant feedback and tutoring (12%), engagement (7%) or freeing up teacher time (6%). The survey adds a K-12-specific data point to a summer full of AI-and-parents research, sitting alongside the EAB college-search survey we covered on August 11 — together suggesting parental comfort with AI is rising faster on the "informed consumer" side than on the classroom-instruction side.

Sources: Hipther, PR Newswire
