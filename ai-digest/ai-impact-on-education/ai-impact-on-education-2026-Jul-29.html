1. UNAM suspends enrollment for 158,000 applicants amid AI-cheating investigation

Mexico's National Autonomous University (UNAM), Latin America's largest public university, suspended enrollment for incoming first-year undergraduates in the 2026–2027 cycle after an unusual spike in scores on its first fully online admission exam, taken by roughly 158,000 applicants. A technical commission appointed by Rector Leonardo Lomelí is investigating whether widespread AI-assisted cheating — reportedly including hidden earphones, second phones, and Discord screen-sharing to feed answers in real time — inflated results in high-demand programs like Medicine and Law beyond what remote proctoring could catch. Students admitted through UNAM's separate regulated high-school pathway are unaffected, and the university says it will report findings within days. It's one of the largest academic-integrity crises in the university's history, and a stark example of AI outrunning institutions' ability to secure high-stakes remote assessment.

Sources: Mexico Business News, El Financiero, El País, CNN Español

2. Upstate New York district pauses humanlike AI robot teacher after backlash

The Salamanca City Central School District in rural upstate New York put its plan to deploy "Sally" — a nearly $60,000 humanoid, AI-powered robot from Realbotix intended to enhance robotics and technology classes this fall — on indefinite hold after backlash from the state teachers' union, education officials, and local residents. Objections centered on unresolved student-data-privacy protections, fears the robot was a first step toward replacing human educators, and unease over Realbotix's corporate ties to a sister company that makes hyper-realistic sex robots. The district says the pilot stays paused while it negotiates "enhanced student data privacy agreements" with state officials and continues community outreach. The reversal is a case study in how quickly hardware-forward AI-in-the-classroom bets can collide with privacy law, labor concerns, and reputational risk.

Sources: AP News, Gothamist, Washington Post, ABC News

3. First classroom randomized trial finds AI teaching assistants boost teacher time but hurt students

One of the first randomized controlled trials of AI inside real classrooms found that giving teachers an AI teaching assistant backfired for students, according to research led by Wharton's Alp Sungu and reported by The Hechinger Report. Students whose teachers had AI access rated their classes as less enjoyable, less interesting, and less important than a control group, and scored lower on standardized final exams — with the damage concentrated among students whose teachers were already weaker instructors before the trial began. The tool did save teachers time on lesson planning and materials, but Sungu suggests some teachers leaned on it "as a crutch" rather than a complement to instruction, erasing the extra effort that previously helped weaker teachers compensate. It's a direct causal counterpoint to vendor claims that teacher-facing AI reliably improves outcomes, and the story was syndicated across dozens of local newsrooms this week.

Sources: The Hechinger Report, Action News Jax, Grand Junction Sentinel

4. A $40,000-a-year AI school with no classroom teachers opens in Oklahoma

Alpha School, a billionaire-backed chain of private schools that replaces classroom teachers with an AI tutoring platform, is opening its first two Oklahoma campuses in Edmond and Tulsa this August at $40,000 a year in tuition — nearly triple the areas' next-priciest private schools. Students spend two hours each morning on Alpha's adaptive AI learning software; human "guides" don't teach curriculum directly but instead monitor progress and run afternoon workshops on skills like sailing, coding toy cars, or Navy SEAL-style challenges. The model bets that AI can deliver core academic instruction more efficiently than a classroom teacher, freeing the school day for project-based "human" skills — a proposition still unproven at scale and now being tested with paying Oklahoma families for the first time. The launch adds a concrete, high-price data point to the national debate over how far AI can go in substituting for, rather than just assisting, the teacher.

Sources: Oklahoma Watch, KGOU, District Administration, Oklahoma City Free Press

5. Survey: 44% of teens have seen AI-generated sexual content — schools taught them nothing about spotting it

A Common Sense Media/Burson survey of 1,314 US teens found 44% believe they've seen AI-generated sexual content, showing up in social feeds, private messages, and even school conversations — and nearly one in five said they've made such content themselves or know someone who has. Just as striking for schools: 44% of respondents said teachers or schools had taught them nothing about identifying AI-generated or fake material, and only 13% said they'd feel comfortable raising it with a teacher or counselor. Common Sense Media does offer a free K-8 digital-literacy curriculum with an eighth-grade lesson on deepfakes and consent, but the survey suggests classroom guidance is badly lagging how fast generative tools are being used to create explicit material about real classmates. The findings sharpen pressure on schools to treat AI literacy as a safety curriculum, not just an academic-integrity or productivity issue.

Sources: Common Sense Media, EdTech Innovation Hub, CNN
