1. AI chatbot outperforms group therapy on student anxiety in a 3-arm trial

A randomized trial in Israel enrolled roughly 1,000 university students aged 18–35 and compared a 12-week AI conversational platform, Kai, with face-to-face group therapy and a waiting list. Among students with clinical-level baseline anxiety, 57.9% in the AI arm dropped to non-clinical levels, against 14.4% for group therapy and 9.8% for the control. Depression improved versus the control but not versus group therapy, and PTSD symptoms did not differ at all, which marks the limits of the tool. Coverage resurfaced this week, and it feeds the debate on whether campuses should lean on chatbots as counselling capacity runs short. This study was published earlier in 2026, so the date here is when it was picked up, not when it first appeared.

Sources: MentalHealthDaily, PsyPost, ConsultantLive, Let's Data Science

2. Gen Z uses AI to learn most, but struggles most to apply it

A Preply survey of 5,050 working professionals across nine countries found 88% use AI as part of their learning. Gen Z was the most likely to use it and also the most likely to say they struggle to use what they learned in real situations. Preply calls the gap between felt progress and real-world confidence the Progress Gap: 49% kept a learning routine for months yet still could not apply the skill, and 63% called themselves busy learning without truly improving. It is a vendor survey rather than a controlled study, but it echoes the homework-up, exam-down pattern in recent academic work.

Sources: Fortune, Unite.AI, Hi-Tech.ua

3. AFP: US campuses move away from AI detectors as primary evidence

An AFP report dated 6 October describes US universities, with Yale and Cornell named, banning or discouraging AI-detection scores as the main evidence in misconduct cases. The stated reasons are false positives, bias against non-native English speakers, and 'humanising' tools that disguise AI text. Harvard College Dean David Deming is quoted saying the sector would benefit from getting out of the AI-detection business because it damages trust between faculty and students. Tracking by others counts at least a dozen elite institutions that have switched Turnitin's detector off, so this is a development on an earlier retreat, with Yale limiting faculty to informal screening and Johns Hopkins making detector hits advisory only. Sources: Progressive Robot (AFP report), AI Weekly, GradPilot tracker

4. Orange County, Florida schools face new classroom AI requirements

Orange County Public Schools is working through new requirements after the Florida State Board of Education adopted a statewide AI rule on 16 September. The most notable change is parental consent when students directly use certain AI instructional tools, with a comparable non-AI alternative if a parent declines. The rule also requires training on AI safety, bars AI from meeting students' emotional needs, and requires staff to be able to switch off AI systems without vendor help, with districts having until 1 July 2027 to implement. The district only passed its first AI policy in July, which let teachers decide on use as long as parents are notified, so the state rule tightens it.

Sources: WKMG ClickOrlando, Fox 35 Orlando, Spectrum News 13, Florida DOE

5. Universities 'cannot grade their way out of AI'

An AI Weekly analysis argues the useful question is no longer whether students use AI but whether universities can teach them to work with it while still certifying what they can do without it. It says AI tutoring works only when built into a learning design, rather than bolted on. A companion eSchool News piece the same weekend argues AI literacy should centre on learner agency, judgment and verification rather than tool training. Both fit the shift from policing tools toward redesigning assessment.

Sources: AI Weekly, eSchool News, ETC Journal
