1. South Korea's top universities start handing down F grades in a widening AI-cheating crackdown

Six of South Korea's most selective universities — Seoul National, Yonsei, Korea, Sogang, Sungkyunkwan and Hanyang — have now confirmed disciplinary cases tied to AI-assisted exam cheating, with Sungkyunkwan converting every confirmed case into an automatic F grade in the course. The highest-profile episode remains Yonsei's "Natural Language Processing and ChatGPT" midterm, where an anonymous student poll found 190 of 353 respondents admitting they had used ChatGPT during a supervised, webcam-monitored online exam by manipulating camera angles or screenshotting questions into the chatbot. Universities are offering an amnesty of sorts — students who self-report get a zero on the exam but avoid further discipline, while those who don't risk suspension — and at least one professor has built a detection tool that other schools have asked to borrow. The scandal, which first broke last fall, is now driving fresh calls from faculty and administrators for clearer, campus-wide AI rules rather than course-by-course honor codes.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily, The Korea Herald, Korea JoongAng Daily, Times Higher Education

2. OpenAI publishes a six-pillar blueprint for teen AI use, with classroom literacy as pillar one

OpenAI released its Australian Youth Safety Blueprint, a policy roadmap built around six pillars: responsible educational adoption and AI literacy, privacy-preserving age assurance, under-18 safety policies, protection from manipulative or deceptive outputs, research-informed crisis response, and accessible parental controls. Leading with education is notable — the document frames AI literacy taught in schools as the foundation the other five protections build on, arguing companies should design youth safeguards into products rather than leaving families and teachers to manage risk after the fact. It follows OpenAI's August rollout of ChatGPT for Teens in Australia, which defaults under-18 accounts into stricter safety settings and lets parents set "quiet hours," disable memory and chat history, and opt out of having their teen's conversations used for training. The blueprint arrives as Australian regulators and other governments weigh their own rules for AI in schools, making it a test case for how much influence an AI company's own safety framework has on public policy.

Sources: OpenAI, AI Weekly, Ken Ashe

3. Three AI homework apps just cracked the App Store's top education charts at once

Gauth, Gizmo: AI Tutor and Turbo AI all climbed the U.S. Education charts in the same week, a rare simultaneous surge that trade trackers read as a clean signal of back-to-school demand for AI study help. Gauth rose to #3 overall in Education — ahead of TikTok, Gmail and WhatsApp on the broader App Store charts — while Gizmo jumped four spots to #10; both apps turn a photo of a homework problem or a set of notes into step-by-step explanations and practice quizzes rather than a bare answer. The pattern echoes what's shown up in enterprise data all September: with ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot now built into school-issued accounts, students are increasingly turning to purpose-built tutoring apps for the homework-help use case specifically, rather than general-purpose chatbots. It's a small but concrete data point in the broader debate over whether AI tools in students' pockets help them learn or just let them skip the work, since neither app's makers have published independent evidence on learning outcomes.

Sources: AI Weekly — In the Wild, Technology.org

4. Samsung and the Mark Cuban Foundation ship a free, seven-session AI curriculum for grades 6-12

Samsung and the Mark Cuban Foundation released a ready-to-teach AI curriculum for middle and high school classrooms, built around seven sessions covering how large language models work, how to prompt them effectively, and how to verify AI-generated content rather than take it at face value. The program is free and timed to land ahead of the 17th annual Samsung Solve for Tomorrow STEM competition, which opens for entries September 30, giving teachers a structured on-ramp rather than another ad-hoc AI unit assembled from scattered resources. Its explicit goal is moving students past casual chatbot use toward using AI to actually solve problems and critically evaluate its output — the same "AI literacy, not just AI access" framing showing up this month in OpenAI's Australian blueprint and in multiple state curriculum mandates. It adds Samsung to a growing list of consumer-tech companies (Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, OpenAI) now distributing their own free classroom AI-literacy materials directly to teachers.

Sources: Samsung Newsroom, Dallas Morning News, Seoul Economic Daily

5. India's Assam state signs six AI-in-schools deals in a single day, from teacher training to literacy screening

Assam's state education department signed memoranda of understanding with six organizations at once — Google AI, IIT Madras' Bodhan AI, Wadhwani AI, Educate Girls, EkStep-AXL and Canva Education — each targeting a different piece of the AI-in-schools puzzle rather than a single vendor rollout. The Bodhan AI partnership alone plans to train roughly 100,000 grade 6-12 teachers in AI proficiency by May 2027, while Wadhwani AI will use AI-enabled assessment to screen students' reading fluency and Educate Girls will apply AI-assisted tools to identify and re-enroll out-of-school adolescent girls specifically. Google AI's piece focuses on teacher-facing tools and professional development, and Canva Education's on digital literacy and design skills, with pilots already sized at 50 and 70 schools respectively. The multi-partner structure is a notable contrast to the single big-vendor deals (Missouri-Google, Utah-Google) that have defined most state-level AI-in-education rollouts in the US this year, spreading the bet across teacher training, literacy screening and equity-focused outreach instead of one platform.

Sources: Free Press Journal, Elets digitalLEARNING, Northeast News, Careers360
