1. South Portland, Maine weighs a pre-K–8 student AI moratorium over cognitive-development worries

The South Portland Board of Education was due to vote Monday night on a resolution banning student use of generative AI in grades pre-K through 8 for the rest of the 2026–27 school year, in class and on take-home work, while the district drafts a broader policy. The district has no AI policy today and admits it has no full accounting of how widely the tools are already used. Officials cite studies linking the technology to weaker learning, memory and social development, against a Pew finding that 54% of 13–17-year-olds use chatbots for schoolwork. Staff would get two hours of AI-awareness and three hours of AI-literacy training and could use only district-sanctioned tools, while IEP-required assistive technology, students with disabilities and English learners would be exempt.

Sources: WMTW, WGME

2. Chuncheon turns to St. John’s College’s “Great Books” model to keep Korean pupils thinking for themselves

NPR reports that the South Korean city of Chuncheon partnered with St. John’s College last year to bring discussion-only Great Books seminars into its schools, with no lectures and no outside sources such as the internet. About 120 college students, roughly 770 younger pupils and 40 trainee teachers now take part. The push responds to a post-COVID fall in Korean reading scores and to heavy chatbot use, with OECD figures showing 51% of Korean students use AI chatbots to study weekly. Program co-founder Lee Yonghwa warns that students are even losing the capacity to memorize because they gather facts and ideas mainly from AI, leaving them unable to judge its output.

Sources: NPR, Korea JoongAng Daily (opinion)

3. Academic: AI use is near-universal in universities, but only one in five has a formal policy

Writing in The Conversation, University of Queensland researcher Jason Lodge argues universities have adapted to generative AI more slowly than schools, noting that fewer than one in five institutions in a 90-country UNESCO survey had a formal AI policy a year ago. A July study of 39 Australian ICT-teaching universities found AI rules often buried in misconduct policies, only one with specific AI guidance and just seven with a coordinating body. He says rules cannot force students to learn, so the real risk is offloading learning to AI rather than cheating alone. His remedy is structural assessment redesign, explicit teaching of adaptive skills such as judging what you know, and a national enabling body like Ireland’s Higher Education Authority framework.

Sources: The Conversation

4. UChicago will require an AI-free writing course for all first-years as EIU students push back on faculty AI use

Inside Higher Ed’s new monthly AI roundup reports that the University of Chicago’s new Chicago Center for Writing will require every first-year student to take an AI-free writing course starting next fall. At the other end of the debate, an Eastern Illinois University student petition against professors substituting AI for course materials had 529 signatures, and the Student Senate passed a resolution in October asking administrators to regulate faculty AI use. The roundup also flags Berkeley’s Rubrica tool, which grades handwritten exams (85% score match with humans in a small study), and institutional AI investments including Rice’s plan to hire 75 AI-focused faculty and Purdue’s planned Google TPU access.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed

5. Alpha School opens in Southwest Miami-Dade with two-hour AI-tutor mornings and no traditional teachers

Alpha Miami, at 8000 SW 56th St., has opened using the Alpha model in which students spend a concentrated two-hour block on academics with AI tutors, then join human “guides” for workshops on public speaking, financial literacy, teamwork and resilience. The school says parents asked for exactly this trade of fast academics for life skills, and it joins Alpha campuses in Miami Beach and Boca Raton, with expansion planned. The story is a local TV profile that carries student and staff testimonials rather than learning-outcome data, which matters because the model is drawing growing criticism elsewhere over its achievement claims.

Sources: WSVN 7News
