1. The first independent report card on classroom AI tools finds most aren't ready to teach

Instruction Partners, a national education nonprofit, published what appears to be the first systematic, product-by-product review of the AI tools flooding classrooms, built from profiles of 20 named products — including ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, multi-purpose teaching assistants like MagicSchool and Google Classroom's AI features, and single-purpose tools like Amira Learning's reading software — plus site visits and interviews across 16 school systems. The review, called the AI Learning Tour, sorted tools into three buckets and found that general-purpose chatbots carried the most risk of doing students' thinking for them, while narrowly scoped, single-purpose tools fared comparatively better; crucially, it concluded that none of the 20 products was ready to independently handle the pedagogical work of teaching. Instruction Partners says it will review at least 20 more tools this school year and partner with Stanford's AI in Education Lab to weigh vendors' evidence-of-impact claims — a direct, practical answer to the "schools are moving faster than the evidence" critique Stanford's own K-12 AI review leveled earlier this month.

Sources: Education Week, WinsSolutions, Complete AI Training, Instruction Partners

2. Boston's AI-run private schools open their doors — in Jamaica Plain, Beacon Hill and Lexington

Alpha School — the Texas-born chain that replaces licensed teachers with AI-driven software and $100,000-a-year "guides" — opened its first Massachusetts campuses this month, bringing its no-teacher model into some of the most politically progressive, heavily unionized school communities in the country. Tuition runs $60,000 a year at the Jamaica Plain site, the former Croft School campus, and $65,000 at Beacon Hill, which launches with 25 students and plans to grow to 50 next year; students are promised two hours of AI-driven "core academics" each morning followed by six hours of workshops on entrepreneurship, financial literacy and public speaking. The openings are part of a national wave of roughly 27 new Alpha campuses this fall and cap months of local debate after Boston's School Committee approved the private school in June over objections from parents and education advocates questioning its academic claims and the elimination of certified teachers. A Boston Globe column published as classes began framed the arrival as a live test of how far AI-first schooling can go in a market that has otherwise resisted it.

Sources: Boston Globe, NBC Boston, WBUR, Lexington Observer

3. Bloomberg: AI is rewriting what an MBA is for — and who gets picked to lead a business school

Bloomberg reported this week that generative AI is now reshaping how MBA students learn, forcing deans and faculty to rethink curricula that treated AI as a niche elective just a few years ago rather than a core competency employers expect on day one. A companion Bloomberg profile of Yael Grushka-Cockayne — who became the University of Virginia Darden School's 10th dean, and its first woman, on August 1 — frames her AI expertise, including co-directing Darden's LaCross Institute for Ethical Artificial Intelligence in Business, as newly central to what business schools now look for in a leader. Both pieces describe deans wrestling with the same open questions K-12 schools face: whether faculty should use AI themselves, how much AI fluency employers will actually demand of graduates, and how to avoid simply outsourcing the thinking an MBA is supposed to certify. Separate reporting from Virginia Business finds business schools across that state accelerating data-science and AI coursework in response to employer demand, suggesting the pressure Bloomberg describes extends well beyond flagship programs.

Sources: Bloomberg, Bloomberg (Darden dean profile), Virginia Business

4. A Christian university bets students need help protecting their own ideas from AI, not just policing AI misuse

Cedarville University, a Christian liberal-arts school in Ohio, launched an "AI + IP Stewardship Pilot" this month that flips the usual campus AI conversation: rather than another policy aimed at catching AI-assisted cheating, it teaches students, faculty and staff how to identify, document and protect their own original work — coursework, creative projects, research — in an environment where AI tools can reproduce or repurpose that work in seconds. The pilot, developed under Berry Chair of Entrepreneurship Kary Oberbrunner, builds on Cedarville's existing campus-wide AI initiative and gives participants practical intellectual-property habits, such as creating time-stamped records of original work, that the university says will matter increasingly for students entering what it calls an "AI-driven economy." It's a narrower, more values-explicit version of a problem most research universities have approached mainly through detection software and honor-code language: proving what a person, not a model, actually made.

Sources: GlobeNewswire, The Manila Times, Middletown Life, Cedarville University

5. Carnegie Mellon's middle-school AI curriculum jumps from three states to seven

Carnegie Mellon University's School of Computer Science announced on September 10 that its AI4MiddleSchools initiative — which trains teachers to bring hands-on AI lessons to sixth-through-eighth graders — is expanding from Georgia, Texas and Florida into new communities in Connecticut, Mississippi, New Jersey and Pennsylvania this fall. The program, which grew out of the Georgia-only AI4GA project launched in 2021, has so far trained more than 75 educators and reached upwards of 1,700 students, with a stated goal of reaching 15,000 students within three years. Curriculum is built around CMU's "Five Big Ideas in AI" framework, covering machine learning, natural-language understanding and computer vision alongside bias, ethical design and the societal stakes of automated decision-making. It's a notable data point in a year when most K-12 AI policy debate has centered on high schoolers and college students: a well-resourced university-led effort explicitly targeting AI literacy for younger adolescents, years before most of the moratoriums and bans making headlines this month would even apply to them.

Sources: Carnegie Mellon School of Computer Science, WinsSolutions, GovTech, Georgia Tech College of Computing
