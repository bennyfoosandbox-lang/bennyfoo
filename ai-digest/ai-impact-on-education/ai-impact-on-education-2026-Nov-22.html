1. Update: Korea’s education minister resigns over the exam AI-cheating crackdown, as the final tally reaches 111 cases

South Korea’s AI-cheating crisis has now cost a cabinet minister his job. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin offered his resignation on Sunday 22 November and President Lee Jae Myung accepted it the same day. Choi took responsibility for the confusion caused by anti-cheating rules added at the last minute before the 19 November College Scholastic Ability Test. He also faced criticism for his remarks to the National Assembly. The ministry’s final count is 111 suspected device-assisted cheating cases, up from a provisional 98 after four of the exam’s five periods. On 21 November Lee repeated that anyone who reports exam cheating will get at least ₩100 million (about $75,000) per case, multiplied by the number of offenders. Cheaters who turn themselves in and name others will get lighter penalties. Lee had already ordered an “A to Z” overhaul of the CSAT and other state exams for the AI era, including technical blocks on photographing papers and traceable marks on question booklets. Teacher unions argue that surveillance alone will not work and that assessment itself has to change. This is the first time AI-assisted cheating has brought down a national education minister.

Sources: Korea JoongAng Daily (resignation accepted), Korea JoongAng Daily (offer to resign), The Korea Times, SBS News, Yahoo News (111 cases), The Korea Times (reward), The Korea Herald (reward), Korea JoongAng Daily (exam overhaul), The Kyunghyang Shinmun (analysis), The Express Tribune

2. Google’s own employees petition the company to let schools keep Search without its AI

The pushback against Google’s rule that schools must take AI in Search or lose Search now includes Google staff. A petition organised by the Alphabet Workers Union (CWA Local 9009) asks education chief Chris Phillips, CEO Sundar Pichai and the head of Search to let K-12 schools switch off AI features in Search without losing Search altogether. NPR puts the number of signers at more than 550 employees. Bloomberg, cited by The Next Web, says more than 540 employees and contractors, and Engadget says over 500. The union warns that the policy could damage Google’s reputation with students and teachers and push districts to drop its products entirely. The petition lands as Fortune reports that three of the four largest US districts, New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago, are weighing other search engines. Miami-Dade, a Gemini partner, has not said how it will respond. Google still says Search and AI are “deeply integrated” and that its early-2027 controls let schools turn Gemini and Gemini Notebook on or off, but any school that enables Search also gets AI Overviews.

Sources: NPR, Engadget, The Next Web, Gizmodo, Fortune (largest districts)

3. Update: 97% of voting Cal State faculty back a strike, with AI protections among the core demands

The California Faculty Association announced on 20 November that 97% of members who voted are ready to strike if needed to win a contract that includes AI protections. The union represents more than 28,000 CSU faculty and staff. The vote ran from 26 October to 19 November, and the union’s release did not give turnout. AI is a central demand alongside pay, workload and academic freedom. The union wants binding contract language, enforceable by an arbitrator, that bars CSU from using AI to evaluate or monitor faculty. It says management has offered only a memorandum of understanding that would expire. It also objects that CSU signed multimillion-dollar AI contracts without bargaining. A strike authorisation is not yet a walkout. A final fact-finding hearing is set for 10 December, followed by a report and a 10-day quiet period, after which a strike becomes legal. In the same week, the CSU Academic Senate again said AI should be used for administrative work, not for teaching. If the contract clause is won, it would be among the first to set written limits on AI at a public university system this size.

Sources: California Faculty Association (result), Times of San Diego, LAist, KALW (background)

4. A North Carolina charter school finds AI nude images made from photos of 47 students and 14 staff

Woods Charter School in Chatham County, North Carolina, told families this week that a juvenile is accused of using AI to make more than 50 nude images. According to Principal Cotton Bryan’s letter, the images were built from photos of 47 current and former students and 14 current and former staff members, and were shared online. The Chatham County Sheriff’s Office has opened a criminal investigation and has said little more because a minor is involved. The school says the suspect is not a Woods Charter student and has been banned indefinitely from campus and school events. Which AI tool was used is still unknown. On the same day, WAFB reported that a teacher in St. Landry Parish, Louisiana, had been arrested on child sexual abuse material charges. Investigators say he used AI to make sexualised deepfakes of several people, and he has been placed on administrative leave. Together the cases show that AI image abuse in schools now hits staff as well as students. Schools are handling investigations, family communications and campus bans before the law has caught up.

Sources: WRAL, WSPA, The NC Beat, WAFB (St. Landry Parish)

5. More AI undergraduate degrees: Missouri S&T gets the state’s first, and Singapore’s LASALLE adds two for artists

On 19 November the University of Missouri Board of Curators voted unanimously to approve a bachelor’s degree in artificial intelligence at Missouri S&T. It is the state’s first standalone undergraduate AI degree. The 120-credit program sits in the computer science department and combines computer science, mathematics, data science and ethics with dedicated AI courses. It is due to launch in fall 2027 if the state Coordinating Board for Higher Education approves it. In Singapore, LASALLE College of the Arts opened applications for two AI degrees, one on making work with AI and one on analysing it, with the first students starting in August 2027. The Daily Northwestern reports that Northwestern’s new AI major is in its first quarter, and every student in it must take a course on AI’s impact on society. These programmes come as traditional computer science enrolment softens. Universities are increasingly treating AI as its own discipline, and some are building ethics and creative practice into the degree rather than adding them as electives.

Sources: St. Louis Public Radio, KCUR, Danamic (LASALLE), The Daily Northwestern
