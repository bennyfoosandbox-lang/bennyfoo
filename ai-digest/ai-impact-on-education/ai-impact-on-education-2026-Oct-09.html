1. A 2,379-student randomized trial finds a course-integrated AI tutor lowered grades

A randomized trial of a course-integrated generative AI tutor covering 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors, run in fall 2025 and reported as a University of Maryland study, found that sections offered the tutor ended with final grades about four percentage points lower, or 0.37 standard deviations. Participation in the learning management system fell even more, by 0.90 standard deviations, and only about 15% of students offered the tutor actually used it. The authors read this as the tutor drawing students away from existing learning activities rather than adding to them. It cuts against earlier positive results from purpose-built tutors, such as a Harvard physics study, and underlines that deployment design matters. These figures come from the working paper and press coverage; the paper has likely not yet been peer reviewed.

Sources: EdWorkingPapers (Annenberg), paper 26-1598, EdTech Innovation Hub

2. OpenAI previews a College Planner inside ChatGPT for Teens

OpenAI announced on 7 October that a College Planner is coming to ChatGPT for Teens, consolidating application requirements, deadlines, tasks and financial-aid steps for the schools on a student's list. The first version targets US students in grades 10 to 12 who plan to attend a four-year college, with two-year colleges, trade schools and other countries to follow later. It does not submit applications or recommend where to apply, and OpenAI says counselors, families and colleges still shape those decisions. The company says hundreds of thousands of US teens already use ChatGPT weekly for application planning, which puts the product in direct territory long held by school counselors. Reports disagree on timing, with one saying it ships in the next few weeks and another saying no date has been given.

Sources: Fox Business, OpenAI, Runtime Wire

3. Common Sense Media rates ChatGPT for Teens an “Unacceptable Risk”

Common Sense Media’s Youth AI Safety Institute tested more than 4,000 prompts on teen accounts and rated ChatGPT for Teens an “Unacceptable Risk”, urging OpenAI to keep under-18s off the product until it is safe. Some protections held, such as refusing explicit sexual roleplay, but the group found crisis-support referrals less reliable and raised concerns about parental alerts; test accounts registered as adults were also never moved into the teen experience. OpenAI disputes the findings, arguing that most testing finished before parental controls were fully active. Readers should note that Common Sense Media receives funding from OpenAI and other tech firms (it says it keeps editorial control), and that this is an advocacy rating rather than a regulatory finding. The report landed the same week as OpenAI’s education push for teens.

Sources: Axios, The Next Web, La Nación (AP)

4. Colorado names its first “AI-Engaged Campus” colleges

Colorado has created a state designation recognizing colleges that engage with AI deliberately, and Colorado Public Radio reported on 8 October that six schools, including the University of Denver and Colorado State University Global, earned it in the first year. The Department of Higher Education’s framework has six pillars, among them AI literacy in the curriculum, faculty preparedness, institutional policy, workforce connection and equity and ethics, and applicants must show meaningful work in four. Officials framed it as acknowledging that AI is already ubiquitous rather than encouraging its use. It offers a contrasting model to the bans and moratoria spreading through K-12, using recognition and a roadmap instead of restriction. I could not independently confirm the full cohort list beyond the radio report.

Sources: Colorado Public Radio, Colorado Department of Higher Education

5. About half of Chicago school board candidates back a three-year AI pause

Illinois Families for Public Schools launched a “Pledge to Protect Human-Centered Education” in August asking Chicago Board of Education candidates to support a three-year pause on generative AI in classrooms, limits on screen time, a ban on collecting biometric data and a chief privacy officer. Reports count roughly two dozen of 42 candidates on the November ballot as signers, though exact numbers vary by outlet. The pledge has no legal effect, and any change would need district action after the election. It matters because it shows the New York City moratorium and Los Angeles device restriction becoming an electoral issue in a third giant district. This is not a last-24-hours story; it is about five weeks old and is included because it is new to this digest and the vote is weeks away.

Sources: Let's Data Science, Fox 32 Chicago, Stateline
