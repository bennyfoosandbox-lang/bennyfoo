1. Detection is dead: 60+ universities abandon AI detectors for redesigned assessments

A wave of institutions — now more than 60 across five countries, including MIT, Yale, Georgetown, Vanderbilt, Northwestern, Johns Hopkins, Waterloo, UC Berkeley and a dozen more that have disabled Turnitin's AI-detection module outright — have formally banned or restricted AI-detection tools as unreliable arbiters of cheating, Inside Higher Ed reported August 5. The retreat follows mounting evidence of false positives against multilingual and neurodivergent writers, and some schools are now warning faculty that using an unapproved detector could expose them to personal liability if a misconduct finding is later overturned. In detection's place, professors are redesigning assessments around oral defenses, in-class writing, process-based grading that tracks document revision history, and assignments that require students to critique or build on AI output rather than avoid it. MIT Sloan's conclusion, echoed elsewhere: policing AI use doesn't work, so the assessment itself has to change.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, AI Weekly

2. A disabled law student's AI-cheating case turns into a fight over sealed court records

Nicole Lawtone-Bowles, a Purdue University Global Law School student who uses speech-to-text software because of a disability, sued the university in April after it dismissed her — weeks before her scheduled August 25 graduation — over three alleged academic-integrity violations tied to AI-detection findings. Her complaint argues the university treated writing characteristics that are a direct byproduct of her accommodation, like unusual phrasing and pacing, as evidence of AI use, even though Purdue Global's own policy states faculty cannot rely solely on AI detectors, which the school itself calls "inaccurate and unreliable." The case escalated on July 30 when Purdue Global formally opposed a bid by the outlet Inner City Press to unseal the summary-judgment record, calling the media organization a "non-party ... with no direct stake in the outcome," even while saying it claims no institutional privacy interest in the sealed exhibits themselves. The dispute adds a disability-rights dimension to the growing list of lawsuits, alongside Yale and Adelphi, testing whether AI-detection findings can survive legal scrutiny.

Sources: The Indiana Lawyer, Inner City Press

3. Computer science enrollment craters further as colleges rewire the rest of campus around AI

Updating our August 3 coverage: Fortune reported August 6 that undergraduate enrollment in computer and information sciences fell 8.4% in spring 2026 — compounding a 3.6% drop the previous fall — while graduate CS enrollment fell 14% over the same stretch, as entry-level coding work is increasingly automated. But colleges aren't retreating from AI instruction; they're spreading it everywhere else. The University of Florida now offers more than 200 AI-related courses across all 16 of its colleges, with 71% of its spring 2026 graduating class having taken at least one; Ohio State's new "AI fluency" initiative will require every student starting with the class of 2029 to be fluent in AI applications relevant to their major; and liberal-arts schools like Colby College are folding AI literacy into humanities and social-science majors rather than standalone CS tracks. The pattern reinforces our earlier coverage: AI literacy is being democratized across every major just as the traditional CS pipeline shrinks.

Sources: Fortune, Startup Fortune, U.S. News (AP)

4. New Mexico districts revolt against a state-mandated AI reading test for 5-to-8-year-olds

Los Alamos Public Schools became the latest New Mexico district to pause use of Amira Learning, the voice-recognition AI the state mandates for K-2 reading assessments, after Superintendent Jennifer Guy wrote to the state education secretary raising unresolved questions about where children's voice recordings are stored, how long they're retained, and who can access them, Education Week reported August 5. Albuquerque teachers describe a mixed picture: some literacy gains, but also inaccurate scores tied to speech and accent variation, plus visible student frustration at being scored by a machine, according to the Albuquerque Journal. The state education department says updated guidance, covering both assessment use and student-privacy protections, is "forthcoming" but hasn't set a date. The episode previews a fight likely to recur nationally as more states mandate AI-scored literacy tools for the youngest, least AI-literate students in the system.

Sources: Education Week, Santa Fe New Mexican, Albuquerque Journal

5. Watchdog report says the AFT's $23 million AI-training push with Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic is a DEI vehicle

A late-July investigation by the conservative watchdog group Defending Education — older than our usual 24-hour window, but a fresh storyline still generating coverage this week — alleges that the National Education Association and American Federation of Teachers are steering "equity" and "algorithmic bias" framing into the AI guidance and training they hand to teachers, ahead of AI becoming standard classroom infrastructure. The report's central example is the AFT's National Academy for AI Instruction, a $23 million initiative launched with the United Federation of Teachers, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic, which the report says trains teachers that AI tools carry inherent bias and equity problems; it also flags an NEA model school-board policy urging districts to ensure classroom AI tools reflect DEI principles. The NEA and AFT respond that the work is meant to help educators use AI responsibly and ensure equitable access, not to embed ideology, and both unions maintain their guidance is squarely about safe, ethical implementation. The dispute previews a broader fight over who writes the ground rules, and whose values, for AI tools now reaching millions of classrooms.

Sources: Fox News, Defending Education (report), AFT (response context)
