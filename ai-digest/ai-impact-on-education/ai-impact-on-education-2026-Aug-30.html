1. New York state senators propose the FOCUS Act: no AI chatbots for K-5, opt-in for teens

State Sens. Andrew Gounardes, Jamaal Bailey and Jessica Scarcella-Spanton introduced the Fostering Optimal Classroom Use of Screens (FOCUS) Act on August 21 — the most sweeping state-level attempt yet to regulate both screens and AI in New York classrooms. The bill would require screen-free "analog" instruction from pre-K through fifth grade, a rotation model for grades six through eight, and parental opt-in before high schoolers can use chatbots or generative AI at all; it would also ban social media, direct messaging, gaming and student-facing cameras during instructional time and require vendors to demonstrate a product's educational value before districts can adopt it. It follows New York's earlier ban on personal smartphones in classrooms and lands amid the same statewide anxiety that's driven the ongoing fight over NYC's stalled AI moratorium and purchasing freeze, which this digest has tracked since early August. Sponsors frame it as putting learning ahead of tech-company profits; ed-tech vendors are expected to push back hard on the proof-of-value and opt-in requirements once the bill reaches committee.

Sources: NY Senate press release, Brooklyn Paper, Gothamist

2. Two professors got caught writing op-eds with AI — one of them was arguing AI is ruining students

A Harvard Kennedy School economist, Ricardo Hausmann, and a UC Berkeley math professor, Zvezdelina Stankova, both admitted this month to using generative AI to help write opinion pieces for major outlets, and the overlap of the two cases turned into a wider fight over double standards. The Financial Times appended a correction to Hausmann's August 13 tariffs essay disclosing he'd used AI to "condense" it, violating the paper's policy; two days later Stankova published a San Francisco Standard op-ed arguing UC should reinstate standardized testing because "some of my students can't do middle school math" — only for the AI detector Pangram to flag roughly a third of her own piece as AI-generated or AI-assisted. The Chronicle of Higher Education's follow-up names the irony directly: critics note it's hard to discipline students for exactly the AI use professors are now publicly defending in their own writing. Both professors say the final text reflects weeks of their own genuine argument and research, with AI used only for editing — the same defense many accused students offer.

Sources: The Chronicle of Higher Education, The Daily Californian

3. OpenAI's national AI-in-education program adds Singapore, shows early usage numbers from Estonia and Jordan

OpenAI used the Education World Forum in London to detail "the next phase" of its Education for Countries initiative, welcoming Singapore — where it's working with the Ministry of Education and GovTech on personalized learning and mother-tongue-language tools — alongside its original eight-country cohort (Estonia, Greece, Italy, Slovakia, Trinidad & Tobago, Kazakhstan, the UAE and Jordan). The company shared some of its first real usage figures: Estonia's ChatGPT Edu rollout, researched jointly with the University of Tartu and Stanford, now reaches more than 20,000 students and 4,600 teachers, while Jordan's homegrown "Siraj" assistant has been used by more than 1 million students and 100,000 teachers as part of a national digital-services push. OpenAI also launched a companion newsletter, The Edu Prompt, and previewed "OpenAI Luminaries," an educator-engagement track meant to let teachers co-design classroom resources across participating countries. The scale contrasts with the more contested US rollout of ChatGPT for Teachers this digest has tracked, underscoring how differently AI-in-schools adoption plays out where national governments, rather than individual districts, are the customer.

Sources: OpenAI, EdTech Innovation Hub, Digital News Asia

4. George Washington University rewrites its AI classroom guidance for the first time since 2023

New Provost Ed Balleisen emailed students on August 26 with GW's first update to its generative-AI guidelines since founding guidance was issued in April 2023, after consulting the Faculty Senate's Educational Policy & Technology Committee and school deans. The update keeps former Provost Chris Bracey's three-tier model — professors can grant general permission, allow AI only on specific assignments, or prohibit it outright — but adds language warning that over-reliance on AI can erode close reading, distinct writing style and logical reasoning, and it explicitly extends the Code of Academic Integrity to "AI-embedded technologies" like smart glasses, not just standalone chatbots. It also pushes professors to discuss their AI policy with students in the first weeks of class rather than stating it only in the syllabus. The revision follows GW's appointment of a special AI advisor to the president and provost and comes as the university plans a national search this fall for a permanent AI-strategy director, while student government and faculty senate bodies have passed dueling resolutions over how strict the eventual policy should be.

Sources: The GW Hatchet, GW Office of the Provost

5. Microsoft expands its AI study coach as schools head back for 2026-27

Microsoft's August back-to-school update for Microsoft 365 Education rolled out upgrades to its Study and Learn Agent, the Copilot-based AI tutor built to preserve "productive struggle" rather than hand students finished answers: it now supports image-based study materials and more than 40 languages, with a feature letting teachers launch students straight into a Study and Learn session from within an assignment arriving later in August. The agent is on by default for 13-to-17-year-old K-12 students with a Microsoft Education license and off by default in higher ed, where administrators can enable it. The same update adds "Remember Settings," which carries a teacher's subject and grade level across Copilot's Teach, Teams, LTI and OneNote Class Notebook tools instead of requiring re-entry each time, plus new Copilot Notebooks for building AI-assisted study materials. It's Microsoft's most concrete back-to-school counter yet to the OpenAI and Google classroom pushes this digest has tracked, betting on tight integration with tools schools already run rather than a standalone chatbot.

Sources: Microsoft Community Hub, Microsoft Education Blog
