1. Teachers’ classroom AI use stalls at 62% after two years of fast growth

A nationally representative EdWeek Research Center survey of 590 teachers, taken between August and October 2026 and published this week, found that 62% use AI tools in their classrooms at least a little this school year. That is up just one point from 61% in 2025, after use nearly doubled from 34% in 2023. About two-thirds of teachers (64%) now say they’ve had at least one professional-development session on AI, so lack of exposure no longer fully explains the plateau. Some coverage links the flat line to this autumn’s backlash against classroom tech, with district pauses and Google’s AI-search fight, but experts quoted by EdWeek say that is only one possible factor. Don’t mix this with RAND’s separate math-teacher survey, which showed a much sharper jump (45% to 74%) using a different sample and question.

Sources: EdWeek, WiseVoter, SuperPower Daily, Bytes Europe

2. College students say they use AI responsibly — it’s their classmates who don’t

A new National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, reported by Inside Higher Ed on 25 November, surveyed 1,003 college students through College Pulse. It found a consistent gap between what students admit and what they say peers do. About a third said they had used AI to draft essays, but about half said their peers did. For summarizing assigned readings, 39% reported their own use against 60% for peers. The authors suggest students may be reporting the worst behaviour they have seen across many classmates rather than what a typical peer does, but the gap also points to under-reporting in self-report surveys. That matters because campus AI policies and assessment redesigns are increasingly calibrated to such surveys, and it echoes a Packback survey from March that found the same self-versus-peer gap.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, The Morning Science, Dartmouth panel survey (related paper)

3. UAE’s new government AI assistant will tell public-school parents why their child is falling behind

Education Minister Sarah Al Amiri said on 24 November that, from March, parents of UAE public-school students will be able to ask Mira, the government AI assistant unveiled a day earlier, why their child is struggling in particular subjects and what would close the gaps. Mira will draw on data schools already collect but parents rarely see, including classroom observations, project results and benchmark assessments. It will also let parents check grades and attendance and book teacher meetings. The rollout covers more than 530 public schools serving about 280,000 students; private schools don’t have to adopt it. It is one of the first national attempts to turn school assessment data into a parent-facing AI channel. So far it rests on government statements, with no independent testing of how accurate or private it is.

Sources: Semafor, The National (Mira launch), Daily AI Brief

4. Ghana’s National Education Week ends with a pledge of strict rules for AI in schools and universities

Ghana’s 2026 National Education Week in Accra (23–24 November) was devoted to AI. Deputy Education Minister Dr Clement Apaak closed it by committing the Ministry of Education to firm policy and ethical frameworks for AI across pre-tertiary and tertiary institutions. He argued that AI in schools is a question of governance and institutional readiness, not just buying hardware or software, and that AI must strengthen foundational learning, not replace teachers. Ministry technical staff and donor partners were due to meet on 25 November to finalize the week’s aide-mémoire. Expected outputs include a proposed National AI in Education Roadmap for 2027–2030 and an ethical-guidelines and data-privacy framework. Education Minister Haruna Iddrisu separately set out plans to add AI, coding and robotics to basic schooling, while warning that over-reliance could erode critical thinking. These are still commitments, not adopted policy.

Sources: Rainbow Radio (closing address), Rainbow Radio (foundational learning), Rainbow Radio (aide-mémoire), Rainbow Radio (opening), Adom Online (curriculum)

5. A liberal-arts philosophy department bans AI “in any capacity and for any purpose”

Trinity College’s philosophy department in Hartford, Connecticut, has unanimously adopted a blanket rule forbidding deliberate AI use in its courses. The ban covers reading summaries, brainstorming, research and editing as well as writing. Faculty argue the technology undermines the department’s mission of teaching students “to think, to read, to write, to speak, to reason, and to live ethically.” The college says it still supports faculty setting course-level rules, but it declined a separate request from 40 faculty to suspend AI use campus-wide and divest from AI companies. The Chronicle of Higher Education calls department-wide bans like this rare so far, and commenters doubt it can be enforced. The case is a clear marker of humanities resistance at a time when many universities are signing campus-wide AI deals. This is a fallback item from about nine days ago, picked up in this week’s coverage.

Sources: The Chronicle of Higher Education, GovTech, Trinity Tripod, Daily Nous
