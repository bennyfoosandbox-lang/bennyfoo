1. National History Day bars students from using generative AI for any part of its contest

National History Day, the contest that draws students in grades 6–12, has rewritten its rules to prohibit generative AI outright, including for brainstorming topics and finding sources, uses it previously allowed. The organization says the change serves students' development of historical thinking skills. Education Week's October 8 report describes the stance as an outlier in social studies, where many teachers separate copy-pasting AI answers (cheating) from AI-assisted brainstorming. The piece also notes the American Historical Association's guidance that generative AI can help teaching and learning but cannot replace human instruction or historical method. I could not locate NHD's own rulebook, so the exact wording and effective date should be checked there.

Sources: Education Week

2. Hanover Research survey: high school educators are adopting AI faster than policies and classroom realities can keep up

A national Hanover Research survey released October 8 on AI use and concerns in K-12 points to a gap between adoption and governance: high school educators are using AI, while policies lag behind what is happening in classrooms. K-12 Dive flagged the survey in its October 9 weekly news roundup. That fits the wider pattern in recent coverage of teachers with little formal guidance and districts splitting between bans and managed adoption. I could only see the headline finding, not the survey's figures, so the underlying numbers are not reported here.

Sources: K-12 Dive (weekly roundup, Oct 9)

3. Colleges race to build AI security programs as frontier-model incidents raise the stakes

U.S. colleges and universities are adding AI-security education at every level, from undergraduate and graduate courses to professional certificates, according to Cybersecurity Dive. The outlet ties the push to recent autonomous-hacking incidents involving frontier AI models, and quotes a Carnegie Mellon official arguing for stronger AI governance as models act outside their training. Carnegie Mellon's Information Networking Institute already runs a Secure AI concentration and an MS in AI Engineering – Information Security, and Anthropic has committed $2 million to CMU, $1 million of it for the picoCTF free cybersecurity program for middle and high school students. The article's publication date could not be confirmed from search results, so treat it as recent rather than strictly last-24-hours.

Sources: Cybersecurity Dive, Carnegie Mellon INI — Secure AI
