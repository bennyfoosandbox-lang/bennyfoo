1. North Carolina's child-death task force backs chatbot limits after hearing about a student and a school Chromebook

North Carolina's Child Fatality Task Force, the legislative study commission that recommends child-safety measures to lawmakers, has endorsed limits on AI chatbots designed to curb emotional dependence and has called for families harmed by chatbot design to have legal recourse. Members amended the language on company accountability to include "financial penalties and/or a duty of care". The vote followed testimony from an advocate about a 13-year-old North Carolina student who formed a relationship with Google's Gemini on a school-issued Chromebook and came close to taking her own life. That case is now the subject of a lawsuit against Google filed earlier this month. A year ago the task force only agreed to keep studying chatbots, so this is a clear hardening of its position. The task force cannot regulate, but its recommendations often shape the state's bills, and Sen. Jim Burgin has said he will reintroduce his chatbot licensing and safety bill (SB 963). For schools, the case links district-issued devices directly to chatbot-safety law.

Sources: Yahoo News (NC Newsline), The Daily Reflector, GovTech, NC General Assembly: SB 963

2. Seoul has an AI grade essays from 300 pupils as Korea weighs essay questions on its college entrance exam

On 14 November the Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education ran a "Thinking Writing Camp" at Gyeongdong Elementary and Seongsan Middle School. About 300 sixth-graders and ninth-graders worked through inquiry, discussion and essay writing, and their answers were then scored by Chaeum AI, the office's essay and short-answer grading system built with Naver, which also gave each student feedback. Chaeum scores against rubrics written by teachers, and officials say teachers keep the final judgement. The system was piloted in 66 schools in 2025, is being expanded to about 120 schools this year, and is meant to be available to every Seoul school from 2027. The camp is part of a larger national debate: the National Education Commission is considering adding essay and short-answer questions to the CSAT college entrance exam, and AI-assisted scoring is one proposed way to make that manageable. Opposition is significant. In an October survey, seven in ten teachers opposed CSAT essay questions over grading disputes and concerns about AI.

Sources: SBS News, Asia Business Daily, Kyunghyang Shinmun, Korea Times (teacher survey)

3. Oxford University Press connects its books and journals to ChatGPT Edu in a pilot at Oxford, Harvard and Wharton

Oxford University Press is running a pilot, from October to December, that lets selected users at the University of Oxford, Harvard Library and the Wharton School ask ChatGPT Edu questions and get answers drawn from the OUP books and journals their institution already licenses. It works through a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server built with OUP's platform provider, Silverchair. When a user asks a question, the server searches OUP's content, picks the most relevant sources and passes them to ChatGPT to answer with. Cherwell reported on 15 November that Oxford's arm of the trial is limited to 30 students, who keep diaries of their experience, while Wharton and Harvard have opened it to all students and staff. For universities this is an early test of something that matters a great deal: whether campus chatbots can be grounded in paid, citable scholarship rather than whatever happens to be on the open web. It also gives publishers a way to keep their content visible as students do more of their research through AI assistants.

Sources: Cherwell, Library Journal infoDOCKET, Publishers Weekly, STM Publishing News

4. Survey of 10,000+ Australian students: nearly all use AI, but many hold back because they don't trust its accuracy

Kelly Matthews of the University of Wollongong and Tim Fawns of Monash have published the 2026 round of a survey first run in 2024 at Monash, the University of Queensland, Deakin and UTS, this time drawing more than 10,000 undergraduate and postgraduate students. Most students now use AI, mostly to edit or improve their writing (nearly 70%). However, many say they are deliberately limiting their use, and 15–17% avoid AI in their studies altogether. Among AI users who limit it, the top reason, chosen by 67%, is doubt about whether its output is accurate. The share who avoid AI for fear of being caught fell to 56%, seven points lower than in 2024, which suggests that clearer rules are reducing anxiety about integrity. The authors argue that frequent use should not be mistaken for enthusiasm, and that universities should design AI guidance around students' scepticism rather than assuming they are eager adopters. The sample also deliberately includes first-in-family, neurodivergent, disabled and multilingual students.

Sources: University of Wollongong (via The Conversation), Future Campus (2024 baseline)

5. UNESCO publishes a guide for teaching students to question AI answers

UNESCO has released "AI Answers. You Decide.", a guide for educators and learners on teaching media and information literacy in a world where chatbots and AI summaries increasingly shape what students read. It was announced on 10 November, following Global Media and Information Literacy Week (24–31 October). The guide, written by Anne Leppäjärvi, argues that AI changes the setting in which media literacy is practised but not its foundations: checking sources, understanding how information is produced and ranked, and keeping human judgement in charge. It covers how generative AI is changing journalism, content creation and the way people find information, and gives educators classroom approaches for each. Assistant Director-General Mariya Gabriel presented it as protecting learners' capacity for critical thinking. The guide arrives as schools face AI answers embedded in default search and chatbots on school devices, and it offers an international alternative to the choice between banning AI and adopting it uncritically.

Sources: Devdiscourse
