1. Florida finalizes its AI rulebook — parental consent, opt-outs and a 2027 deadline

The Florida Board of Education voted Wednesday to formally adopt statewide rules governing AI use in K-12 schools and the state's 28 public colleges, converting the framework first proposed in August from draft to binding regulation. The final rules require districts to notify parents before any AI product is used with their child and obtain consent, and to offer a comparable non-AI instructional alternative if a parent opts out; tools aimed at pre-K through fifth grade face extra review for age-appropriateness and alignment with state academic standards. Districts must also bar students from using AI on graded assignments or assessments unless a teacher explicitly permits it, and every district and college has until July 1, 2027 to finalize local policy ahead of the 2027-28 school year. The vote makes Florida one of the first states to convert a state-level AI framework into enforceable rule rather than guidance — a template other states drafting their own frameworks are likely to watch closely. This updates an August digest item that covered the rule while it was still a proposal.

Sources: Click Orlando, Central Florida Public Media, CBS News Miami

2. Canada bets $13 million that AI literacy belongs in every college, not just computer-science departments

The Canadian federal government, in partnership with the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute (Amii), this week launched a National AI Literacy Initiative backed by roughly $13 million, including a $5 million commitment from Google.org. Its centerpiece is a National Student AI Literacy Initiative consortium: starting September 21, any Canadian post-secondary institution can join to offer students a free, three-hour "AI Essentials for Canadian Students" course covering foundational AI knowledge, critical evaluation, and responsible, workplace-ready use. Organizers project the program could reach up to one million post-secondary students and more than 50,000 K-12 educators, making it one of the largest coordinated AI-literacy pushes by any national government to date. Where many US state actions this month have focused on restricting classroom AI, Canada's approach is explicitly additive — treating AI fluency as a baseline skill to teach alongside existing curricula rather than gate behind new bans.

Sources: Government of Canada, BetaKit, Amii, Mechanical Business

3. A voice-first AI tutor built by ex-Wyzant tutors raises $5M and goes national

Aristotle, a voice-first AI tutoring startup for students ages 13-18, announced a $5 million seed round led by True Ventures, with Wicklow Capital and angel investors from Anthropic, OpenAI, Sierra, Ramp and Cognition also participating. Founded by three Stanford alumni who spent nearly a decade tutoring on platforms like Wyzant and Varsity Tutors, the company is positioning its product — a live, spoken tutoring conversation rather than a text chat window — as a "high-dosage" alternative to generic chatbot tutoring. It says a closed beta saw more than 1,000 students complete over 1,500 hours of one-on-one tutoring across STEM, standardized testing and language learning before this week's nationwide launch. The raise adds to a growing pile of venture money chasing "mastery" and high-dosage tutoring models, a category investors have increasingly favored over generic chatbot wrappers after research questioned how much unstructured chatbot use actually helps learning.

Sources: PR Newswire, Middletown Life, Pressbee

4. Students are flocking to the degrees AI threatens to automate, not fleeing them

A study from King's College London's Dr. Bouke Klein Teeselink, with researchers from the Supervised Program for Alignment Research, examined applications to 1,090 UK degree programs from 2020 to 2025 and found no sign that students are avoiding fields seen as highly exposed to AI disruption. Degrees ranking one standard deviation higher in AI exposure — think computing, mathematical sciences and engineering — saw about 6% more relative growth in applications after ChatGPT's November 2022 launch, compared with less-exposed programs. The paradox: over the same period, entry-level and junior job postings in those same AI-exposed occupations fell by roughly 0.25 percentage points per standard deviation of exposure, pointing to a widening gap between what students are training for and what employers are actually hiring for at the entry level. The researchers warn that if the trend continues, universities may be steering a generation of students into skill sets the job market is simultaneously shrinking. The finding, published September 1, has been circulating in this week's higher-ed roundups and had not yet appeared in this digest.

Sources: King's College London, EdTech Innovation Hub

5. AI is flooding special-education dispute offices with longer complaints — and hallucinated case law

State special-education dispute resolution agencies are reporting a surge in complaint volume and length as more parents turn to AI tools to draft filings, according to reporting this month from K-12 Dive drawn from a presentation at Arizona's 2026 IDEA Conference. Filings now regularly run hundreds of pages citing the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, and some due-process complaints have cited case law that AI tools apparently invented outright, forcing administrative staff to spend far more time separating substance from fabrication. CADRE, the federally funded Center for Appropriate Dispute Resolution in Special Education, has since published a parents' guide warning that AI can misstate IDEA requirements, carries "affirmation bias," and risks exposing a child's private information if identifying details are pasted into a chatbot. The same presentation cited Center for Democracy and Technology data showing 57% of school staff used AI to help write IEPs or Section 504 plans in 2025, up 18 points from the year before — meaning AI is now generating paperwork on both sides of these disputes.

Sources: K-12 Dive, Educational Freedom Institute, NAPSA
