1. England's university regulator: many unsupervised assessments can be passed with AI in minutes, so move to AI-free testing

The Office for Students and Advance HE published “Making AI work for students” on 11 November, drawing on June–September discussions with more than 300 staff and students across roughly 120 English institutions. Its central warning is that a significant share of assessment is still unsupervised and that much of it can be completed to a high standard with AI, sometimes in minutes, in ways that cannot be reliably detected; detection tools are judged too unreliable to lean on. The OfS urges “far greater use of AI-free assessment” — closed-book exams, presentations, practicals and performances — to protect confidence in degrees, while stopping short of a mandate and noting that well-designed assessments that build AI in can work. It also flags the trade-off for disabled and neurodivergent students who benefit from AI, and says the regulator may intervene where providers are not meeting quality requirements; a sector webinar follows on 16 November.

Sources: OfS press release, OfS report, Wonkhe, HEPI, Tech Times, University Herald, Yahoo News

2. RAND: higher-income parents use AI more and push their teens to use it for schoolwork

A RAND report released 12 November, built on 2025–26 American Parents Panel and American Youth Panel surveys of about 1,000 parents and 1,800 teens aged 12–18, finds a widening income split in how families approach AI. Some 67% of higher-income parents encouraged AI use for schoolwork, against 45% of lower-income parents. Parental habits carry over: 63% of teens whose parents often use AI said they used it for homework in at least some classes, versus 40% of teens whose parents never do, and among 533 matched parent–teen pairs, 81% of teens who felt encouraged used AI for homework compared with 38% who felt discouraged. RAND cautions that it is not yet clear whether this gap will widen or narrow achievement gaps, but says schools' AI guidance should account for what students are being told at home.

Sources: RAND report, RAND press release

3. IFS/YouGov poll: 56% of parents want AI blocked in schools and three in four back a pause

The Institute for Family Studies released a report this week on a YouGov survey of 2,000 parents of K-12 students, fielded from late July to mid-August. Most want less AI in school or none: 56% would block it and a further 26% want less than schools now use, while roughly 75% support pausing AI's introduction — a view shared almost equally by Republican (75%) and Democratic (72%) parents. Only 11% favour a tablet-and-AI-centred model of schooling, with 38% preferring AI confined to computer labs while books and handwriting stay central, and fewer than two in five think AI will foster creativity. IFS is an advocacy group and other polls framed differently find majority support for some AI use, but the bipartisan pause numbers land as districts such as New York City and Los Angeles are already restricting classroom AI.

Sources: Institute for Family Studies, IFS blog, National Review

4. Kansas orders every district to adopt a policy covering AI and classroom tech by July 2027

At its 9–10 November meeting the Kansas State Board of Education approved a policy requiring every public district and accredited nonpublic school to adopt an instructional technology policy and implementation plan, which must address teacher and student use of AI along with privacy, security and digital citizenship. Plans must be posted publicly and submitted to the state by 1 July 2027. The most contested question was parents' role: the board settled on requiring a process for parents to acknowledge their school's policy by the 2028–29 school year, rather than the active opt-in/opt-out consent that five Johnson County districts had warned against. Commissioner Jake Steel framed the aim as ensuring technology strengthens teaching rather than replacing interaction between teachers and students.

Sources: KWCH, KSNT, Johnson County Post

5. Little-known journals publish AI-generated papers under prominent professors' names

Inside Higher Ed and Times Higher Education reported on 13 November that a cluster of obscure journals has been publishing shallow, apparently AI-generated papers credited to well-known academics who say they never wrote them. Gothenburg computer scientist Alan Said found one under his name through a Google Scholar alert; others listed include Stanford's Jure Leskovec, Fei-Fei Li and Christopher Manning and Berkeley's Michael I. Jordan. The journals share graphics and an editor-in-chief, and their domains were registered in 2026 even though their sites show archives going back years. For universities the concern is that borrowed names lend credibility that lures PhD students into submitting — and paying — while polluting the citation databases that hiring and promotion rely on.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, Times Higher Education
