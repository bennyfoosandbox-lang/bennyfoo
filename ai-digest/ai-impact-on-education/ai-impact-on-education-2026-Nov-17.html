1. Nigeria launches its first national EdTech strategy, with an AI chatbot for parents, pupils and teachers

On 17 November in Abuja, Nigeria’s Federal Ministry of Education unveiled its first National Educational Technology Strategy, built with the Mastercard Foundation and the World Bank. It is launched alongside AskFME, an AI assistant meant to give students, parents and teachers round-the-clock answers on official education policies and services in English and local languages, and a public portal for the Nigerian Education Data Infrastructure (NEDI). The strategy pulls 26 existing federal programmes into one framework with five pillars: data and interoperability, digital infrastructure, technology and procurement, teacher transformation, and related measures. It builds on connectivity schemes such as Project BRIDGE. At the launch, World Bank country director Matthew Verghis said edtech should be judged by learning outcomes, not by how many devices or platforms are available, and that AI should complement teachers rather than replace them. The launch matters because Nigeria has one of the world’s largest out-of-school populations and a shortage of qualified teachers, so an AI-first government service is a big test of whether chatbots can widen access in low-resource systems.

Sources: The Authority, Blueprint, World Stage News, Federal Ministry of Communications, EduTimes Africa

2. Update: New Jersey’s governor accuses Google of “strongarming” schools over AI-or-no-Search

Governor Mikie Sherrill’s office has publicly objected to Google’s K-12 policy. Under the policy, schools that keep Google’s AI tools switched off must also switch off Google Search for students. A spokesperson said the policy strongarms districts into exposing young learners to AI by threatening to cut off search, and that it puts corporate interests ahead of children. The state has not announced any action. Coverage uses Haddonfield as an example: the district bars generative AI for pre-K to grade 5 and now has to opt in to AI Overviews and AI Mode or lose Search for those pupils. More than 600 New Jersey districts face the same choice. Google says it is changing school Search so that its AI features walk students through problems step by step. This adds a sitting governor to the critics digests have tracked since early November, two weeks before Sen. Markey’s 30 November deadline for Google to answer.

Sources: Community News, NJ Advance Media via Yahoo News, The Philadelphia Inquirer (background on Haddonfield)

3. North Dakota’s first Student Legislative Summit writes its own classroom-AI bills before the 2027 session

Nearly 50 student senators met in the North Dakota Capitol on 16–17 November for the state’s first Student Legislative Summit. The Department of Public Instruction ran the summit, and this year it focused on artificial intelligence and educational technology. According to the North Dakota Monitor, students debated and passed mock bills. One, passed 40–8, would let students use AI tools from ninth grade but not to complete assignments, would require teachers to say when AI use is allowed, and would bar teachers from using AI to enter or assign final grades. The summit is meant to give lawmakers students’ views before the 2027 legislative session. State Superintendent Levi Bachmeier has argued that lawmakers should regulate AI companies and leave classroom AI use to school boards and teachers, and Fargo legislator Kathy Hogan said she hopes the students return during the regular session. Mock bills like these have no legal force, but they are a rare record of what students themselves think the rules should be.

Sources: North Dakota Monitor, Prairie Public, WDAY Radio

4. Algorithm-assisted advising at Georgia State lifted graduation, but advisers’ own judgment did most of the work

A Princeton study by Kara Schechtman and Lydia Liu, covered by Phys.org on 16 November, reanalyses Georgia State University’s part of the MAAPS advising trial. In that trial, advisers used algorithmic risk flags to decide which students to contact. Students in the programme averaged 0.16 grade points higher and had a 7% higher six-year graduation rate than the control group. Georgia State was the only one of eleven participating institutions with significant positive effects. The authors reviewed 8,385 advising actions across 5,269 meetings and found that in 67% of interventions advisers relied on information the algorithm did not have, such as health problems, encouragement or career goals. They conclude that the results do not show AI can replace human advisers. As colleges buy AI advising and ‘student success’ chatbots, this is a reminder that the gains seen so far come from human advisers working with algorithmic prompts. The paper is a preprint, and its power to detect graduation effects is limited.

Sources: Phys.org, arXiv preprint 2505.13325

5. Oregon State study: the most AI-confident STEM students fall deepest into “cognitive debt”

Oregon State University researchers led by doctoral student Rudrajit Choudhuri surveyed 299 STEM students at five North American universities and modelled how trust in generative AI relates to students’ thinking habits. Students who trusted and routinely used AI reported less critical thinking and reflection. The authors describe a loop in which weaker habits lead to more reliance on AI, which they call “cognitive debt”. Contrary to expectation, the most technically confident, AI-savvy students were the most likely to fall into deeper dependence. The authors recommend AI tools that push back with critique and counter-arguments instead of handing over answers, along with curriculum changes that keep students mentally engaged. The study is an unreviewed preprint (arXiv 2601.22430), funded by the NSF and USDA, and the findings are correlational. It came out in local and national coverage from 12 November, so it falls outside today’s 24-hour window.

Sources: Corvallis Advocate, KLCC, Straight Arrow News, arXiv preprint 2601.22430
