1. Families rally at Google offices in seven cities, demanding schools be able to keep Search without AI

On Wednesday, 11 November, parents, teachers and students protested outside Google offices in Chicago, Boston/Cambridge, Boulder, New York, Los Angeles, Durham and Portland, in rallies organized by a coalition of parent-advocacy groups. They object to Google's new K-12 policy: schools can switch off Gemini and the Gemini app, but cannot keep Google Search while turning off its AI Overviews and AI Mode. Turnout was modest (Chalkbeat counted about 30 people in Chicago's West Loop), but the protests put a public face on a fight that has drawn in Senator Markey, whose deadline for Google's answers is 30 November. Chicago Public Schools says it is exploring every option to suppress AI features in Search on its own, and will look at other search providers if Google won't separate them. Google says it shares the goal of stopping students from using AI to shortcut learning.

Sources: Chalkbeat Chicago, Mother Jones, Boston Globe, Block Club Chicago, WBEZ Chicago, Fairplay

2. England's DfE proposes a safety certificate for school edtech and pupil-facing AI tools

The Department for Education opened a consultation on 10 November on a certification scheme for edtech and AI products used in England's early-years settings, schools and colleges. It runs until 4 February 2027. Four product types are in scope: web filtering, edtech doing high-risk processing of children's data, pupil-facing generative AI, and pupil-facing personalised-learning tools. Coverage says higher-risk products would be assessed by independent bodies overseen by UKAS and would need to recertify every three years, or every year for generative AI. Teacher-facing admin tools would be exempt. The scheme would be technically voluntary: the DfE has no power to make schools buy certified products, so it plans to write the expectation into statutory guidance such as Keeping Children Safe in Education. With a response due in spring 2027 and a long build and transition period after that, analysts note that AI tutors will be in classrooms for years before any certificate exists.

Sources: GOV.UK consultation, DfE Citizen Space, Tes, Resultsense, Digital Watch

3. OpenAI: 79% of surveyed students use AI for homework, but only 47% have been shown how

On 10 November OpenAI published “Closing the AI guidance gap in education”, drawing on its government partnerships across Europe, the Middle East and Africa. It cites ThinkYoung survey data: 79% of students surveyed used AI for homework or assignments, but only 47% said anyone had shown them how. Students without clear guidance were twice as likely to have AI do the work for them (36% vs 18%). OpenAI argues the answer is faster educator training and clearer expectations from governments, not classroom restrictions. It points to work with Estonia, Slovakia, Greece and Italy and sets out five themes: clear goals, educator preparation, local partnerships, critical AI literacy and credible evidence. The report doesn't publish its survey methodology, the findings describe behaviour rather than learning outcomes, and it comes from a vendor whose products are at stake.

Sources: OpenAI, EdTech Innovation Hub, TUN

4. A matrix built from 5,000 studies sets out what AI may help or harm, by age

Compass Collaborative launched “AI Guidance by Age” this week, a free tool built from more than 5,000 studies in education, psychology and human development, covering 81 developmental areas. Users choose a role (K-12 teacher, higher-ed instructor, workforce educator, student, researcher or policymaker), a developmental domain and an age range. The tool returns a research summary, recommendations, the key milestones for that age, and how AI might support or undermine them. EdWeek, which credits the effort to Harvard, walks through an example on cognitive foundations for 9- to 11-year-olds. Inside Higher Ed points out the main limitation: most of the evidence predates generative AI by decades, so the guidance is reasoned from child development, not from trials of today's chatbots.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, Education Week

5. Google.org-backed fund names 28 Asia-Pacific partners, including teacher AI training in India and Korea

On 11 November AVPN named 28 local partners for phase three of the AI Opportunity Fund: Asia-Pacific, which is backed by Google.org and the Asian Development Bank and reaches classrooms and workplaces in 19 markets. In education, India's Pratham Education Foundation will train teachers to use AI critically in class, adapting Experience AI and Google for Education materials. JA Korea will pair Google's AI training with formal certification for public-school teachers, with dedicated outreach to rural schools. The Raspberry Pi Foundation joins as a new strategic partner, and Google.org's total commitment to the fund is now US$35 million. This is a press release, so there are no independent figures yet on how many teachers it will reach or when. It is still a sign that much teacher AI training in the region is being funded by the same companies whose tools are being taught.

Sources: PR Newswire (AVPN), ANI, The Manila Times
