1. Harvard report: AI is breeding "two-way suspicion" between students and teachers

A new report from Harvard Graduate School of Education's Center for Digital Thriving finds generative AI is eroding trust in classrooms: students and teachers increasingly suspect each other of using AI, whether or not anyone actually did. Drawing on a survey of 1,000+ US public school teachers and principals (April–May 2025) plus student interviews from April–June 2026, researchers say teachers "can't see their students anymore" because questions once asked aloud now go to chatbots. Some students said they deliberately dial back their best work to avoid false accusations, and many feel ashamed enough to hide their AI use. The authors recommend open, non-punitive conversations about acceptable use rather than tighter policing — a notable counterpoint to the detector-and-ban approach many districts are taking.

Sources: ABC News, Education Week, Harvard Center for Digital Thriving

2. Google.org backs three higher-ed AI initiatives at EDUCAUSE, aimed at community colleges and adult learners

At the EDUCAUSE annual conference, Google.org announced funding for three projects meant to keep educators at the center of campus AI adoption. The Coalition of Urban Serving Universities will fold Grow with Google resources and AI-integrated lessons into its adult-learner certification, with practitioners at 30 universities testing the methods; a second USU project will study 10 AI-enhanced community-based learning models over 15 months. Education Design Lab will also run a national competition in early 2027 for a state community-college system to undergo a two-year technology-enabled redesign. Google cites a statistic that 41% of higher-ed educators have had no formal AI training — the gap the grants target.

Sources: Google – The Keyword

3. Stanford-linked brief: 39% of California districts have essentially no organized response to AI

Policy Analysis for California Education (PACE) published a brief based on interviews with superintendents from a random sample of 94 districts (83 usable). It finds 39% of districts had essentially no organizational response to AI, 40% were at an early "awareness" stage, and only 20% described a broader response covering governance, professional learning, approved tools and monitoring. Meanwhile teachers and administrators are already using ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot on their own, so individual use is outrunning district leadership. The authors urge districts to adopt policy and invest in sustained, collaborative educator learning — including communities of practice — rather than one-day trainings.

Sources: PACE – Policy Analysis for California Education

4. Montclair, NJ proposes banning student AI in pre-K–8 and requiring parental consent in high school

Montclair's school board approved a first reading of a generative-AI policy that would prohibit AI use for pre-K through eighth grade except for narrow, superintendent-approved, research-supported exceptions. High schoolers could still use AI, but only under teacher-specified conditions and with parent or guardian consent; misrepresenting AI work as one's own, deepfakes and misuse of confidential data are listed as prohibited. A companion ed-tech policy would limit Chromebooks for younger grades. A second reading is expected at the October workshop. The story cites a new Fordham Institute national survey in which 22% of high schoolers said they use AI tools without teacher permission, and places Montclair alongside NYC and Los Angeles in the widening K-8 pullback.

Sources: Montclair Local

5. Fordham survey: 62% of high schoolers spend over half of class time on school devices, 22% use AI without permission

The Thomas B. Fordham Institute's "High Tech, Low Standards" report, based on a nationally representative survey of 2,000+ current and recent high school students, finds school-issued devices are a major channel for distraction and AI shortcuts. About 62% say they spend more than half their class time on school devices, 76% report at least one form of daily off-task tech use, and roughly one in five say they use AI tools without their teachers' permission. The data is now being cited by local districts weighing AI and device limits, such as Montclair. It lands as a counter-weight to vendor surveys showing near-universal student AI use, and stresses that device and AI policy are intertwined. (Published earlier in September; included because it is newly driving district policy debate.)

Sources: Fordham Institute – report, Education Next, Montclair Local
