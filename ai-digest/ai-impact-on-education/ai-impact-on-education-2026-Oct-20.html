1. Colleges report 6.6% fewer full-time staff in 2025, and analysts point to AI, though the causal link is contested

The Washington Times reports that the College and University Professional Association for Human Resources found U.S. colleges had 6.6% fewer full-time and 23.9% fewer part-time staff in 2025 than the year before, reversing a 2024 jump of 6.5% and 21.1%. Academics quoted say schools facing enrollment declines and federal funding cuts are eyeing smaller nonteaching workforces as AI automates research, event-planning and donor-relations tasks, while the association itself did not explain the drop. UC Santa Barbara economist Dick Startz cautions that he would be surprised if many layoffs are directly AI-related yet, noting the survey is voluntary and weak hiring in software jobs and campus financial stress are confounders. It matters because it shows AI's education impact reaching university operations and budgets, not only classrooms, and sets up a test of whether administrative headcount keeps falling.

Sources: The Washington Times, CUPA-HR workforce size data

2. Australia's AERO publishes AI-in-schools guidance and admits the evidence on learning outcomes is thin

The Australian Education Research Organisation released a resource collection on 14 October, with separate explainers for teachers and school leaders and for policymakers. It states plainly that, although generative AI is increasingly used by students and staff, there is limited evidence on its impact on learning outcomes. For classrooms it sets out five considerations: prioritise evidence-based teaching, monitor risks to learning, set clear expectations for AI use, rethink assessment, and apply a culturally responsive lens. AERO says it will keep monitoring the evidence and update the guidance, which makes this a notable example of a national research body steering schools toward caution and established pedagogy rather than tool adoption.

Sources: Education Matters Magazine, AERO: AI in schools collection

3. Fired Massachusetts kindergarten teacher sues, saying her principal used AI on her evaluation and the arbitrator consulted chatbots

Christine Apollos, a Concord kindergarten teacher of more than 20 years, is asking Suffolk County Superior Court to vacate an arbitrator's 27 August decision upholding her 2025 dismissal. She alleges the principal relied on AI to write the summative evaluation, citing GPTZero flags and rubric references that do not exist; the district says only a grammar checker was used and points to a run of weak reviews. The twist is that the arbitrator, who gave the AI claim no weight, allegedly asked GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 5 about GPTZero's reliability without telling either side, which the suit calls misconduct. The case, reported on 18 October and so a little outside the 24-hour window, shows AI now entering teacher evaluation and dispute resolution as well as student work.

Sources: Boston.com

4. Berkeley parents press for an AI moratorium after a district survey shows teachers split and the AI policy still unwritten

Berkeley Unified's survey of about 400 teachers found middle schoolers average roughly 37 minutes a day on district Chromebooks, and 31% of high school teachers allow free phone time, but parents doubt the self-reported figures and want an independent audit. The district disabled Gemini on school devices this year while ChatGPT, Claude and other chatbots remain reachable, which parents call baffling. At the 7 October board meeting, parents asked for a generative-AI moratorium like those in New York City and Los Angeles, and teachers split between wanting AI features switched off and wanting training on responsible use. The board gave no timeline for the district's AI policy, which would be its sixth technology policy in four years.

Sources: Berkeleyside

5. University of Montana runs a nine-credit 'Human-Centered AI' certificate built around skepticism, not tool mastery

The University of Montana says it launched the state's first career certificate in Human-Centered AI this year: three online, asynchronous courses open to any student. Students start by probing chatbots' limits, biases and ethical issues, take a course called Calling Bullshit in the Age of AI that grew out of a news-literacy class, and finish with leadership in an AI-centered world. Journalism director Lee Banville says the aim is to make students more skeptical consumers of AI-shaped content, not to sell them on AI, and organizers want to open the certificate beyond the Montana University System. It is a concrete example of campuses answering AI by teaching critical evaluation rather than banning or simply adopting the tools.

Sources: University of Montana News
