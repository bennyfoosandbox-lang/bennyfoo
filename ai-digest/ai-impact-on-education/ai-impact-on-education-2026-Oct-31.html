1. Hong Kong drafts a compulsory primary-school technology subject with AI at its core

Hong Kong's Education Bureau released a provisional curriculum framework on Friday for a new primary subject, information and innovation technology, to be phased in from September 2028 (Primary One and Four first, full rollout in 2030-31). Pupils get one 35-minute lesson a week, 168 lessons across six years, and AI and coding each take 41 of them: seven-year-olds hold simple guided "conversations" with an AI system, Primary Five pupils learn structured prompting, and Primary Six pupils are expected to recognise hallucinations and bias. The draft warns schools against pupils "outsourcing their thinking," the subject is excluded from Primary Five and Six internal assessments, and teachers can respond to a survey until December 2. A companion SCMP report shows the groundwork: roughly 60 per cent of Hong Kong schools already use AI in teaching or administration (an Education University of Hong Kong survey from June), each public school received a one-off HK$500,000 grant, and teachers must now complete 30 hours of digital training every three years. Educators there say the hard part is assessment, since grading only the final product lets pupils hand the work to AI, so they are shifting to evaluating process and how pupils work with it.

Sources: South China Morning Post: primary pupils to study AI under new subject, South China Morning Post: how AI is changing Hong Kong classrooms

2. Think-tank commentary: New York bans, Singapore tiers, and neither has proof it works

A commentary published October 30 by two researchers at Singapore's S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies sets New York City's one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI (2-K through Grade 8, with nearly forty programs disabled and companion chatbots barred at every grade) against Singapore's tiered model, which allows little AI in early primary years and adds teacher-supervised then independent use as students age, under the Ministry of Education's "Four Learns" framework. The authors argue neither approach guarantees learning: Singapore's supervised tools in its Student Learning Space remain largely untested by independent evaluation, while a blanket ban recalls New York's 2023 ChatGPT ban and reversal and could cut off supervised AI-literacy building. Their recommendation is a graduated "pilot, evaluate, then expand" path with baseline and follow-up measures, comparisons against non-AI alternatives, and checks on equity, since home-support gaps seen during COVID-19 school closures are likely to resurface. The piece is a useful yardstick for the many districts now choosing between bans and supervised adoption.

Sources: RSIS Commentary CO26225

3. Japan's universities face a second wave of AI policy: institutions, not just instructors, must own the risk

Writing in East Asia Forum, Shibaura Institute of Technology lecturer Tosh Tachino reviews 395 Japanese university AI policy documents from March 2023 to August 2024 and finds that most gave instructors discretion and put the burden of verifying accuracy and protecting data on individual students, often leaving terms like "appropriate use" undefined. Their sentiment toward generative AI scored 0.38 on a -1 to 1 scale, well below the roughly 0.95 reported in a 2025 study of the top 50 US universities, and the word "syllabus" appeared in only 3.3 per cent of Japanese documents. Tachino argues this first-wave approach cannot cope with multimodal, agent-based AI embedded in whole workflows, which raises procurement, access-permission, data-protection and cybersecurity questions that individuals cannot manage alone. He points to Toho University's October 2026 revision, which provides university-managed AI access with security rules and separate expectations for students, researchers, teaching staff, administrators and medical personnel, and to Kitasato University's move from restriction to university-wide adoption.

Sources: East Asia Forum

4. Survey of 505 Chinese college English teachers: AI confidence cuts anxiety, and school support amplifies the effect

A peer-reviewed study posted online October 31 in Frontiers in Psychology surveyed 505 college English teachers in China, from researchers at Yinchuan University of Energy, to see what separates teachers who use generative AI to build instructional content from those who do not. Using a moderated mediation model, it found that teachers' confidence in their own AI ability was linked to lower AI anxiety, and lower anxiety was linked to more AI-assisted content creation (text generation, rewriting, difficulty adjustment). Institutional backing mattered: where school support was higher, confidence reduced anxiety more strongly and anxiety suppressed content creation less. The design is a cross-sectional survey, so the results show associations rather than causation, and the abstract reports no effect sizes; even so, it adds evidence that training and support, not just tool access, shape whether teachers actually adopt AI.

Sources: Frontiers in Psychology

5. Malaysian law professor: completion-only grading is not the answer to AI, assessment reform is

This commentary is a follow-on to the already-logged story of UC Berkeley's Richard Borcherds grading homework for completion, and its new content is the argument. Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia law professor Shahrul Mizan Ismail says dropping correctness marks does not show anything was learned, and warns that institutions should not pass the entire verification burden to a final exam. He weighs the evidence on both sides: a 2025 PNAS trial of nearly a thousand Turkish secondary maths pupils in which unrestricted GPT access lifted practice performance about 48 per cent but cut later unassisted test scores about 17 per cent (a hint-giving tutor largely removed the harm), against a Harvard physics trial of 194 students where a carefully designed AI tutor more than doubled median learning gains on two lessons. He also cites a 2023 study in which seven detectors wrongly flagged 61.22 per cent of non-native English TOEFL essays as AI-written, and calls for oral follow-ups, process-based assessment, clear rules set in advance and equal access to paid tools, noting these cost staff time that institutions must fund.

Sources: Business Today Malaysia
