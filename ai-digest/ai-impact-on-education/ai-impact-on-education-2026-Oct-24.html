1. NCERT releases a 20-hour AI module for Classes 9 to 12, built around deepfakes, privacy and a 'helper' stance

India's National Council of Educational Research and Training released a 20-hour introductory module on computational thinking and AI for Classes 9 to 12, developed with IIT Madras and IIT Jodhpur and aligned to NEP 2020. Its five units cover AI in daily life, how machines learn from examples, AI errors and deepfakes, data privacy under India's 2023 data protection law, and using AI tools without depending on them. Students learn a five-step method for verifying AI output, are warned not to paste Aadhaar or PAN numbers or passwords into chatbots, and keep a "Curious Notebook" of their reasoning. An "unplugged" option lets schools without computers or connectivity run the core activities on paper. It follows a CBSE curriculum for Classes 3 to 8 launched in April, so AI education now spans most school years.

Sources: Free Press Journal, Times of India, India Today, BW Education

2. A University of Michigan English instructor tried AI, then banned it from her courses

Angela Berkley, an English instructor at the University of Michigan, experimented cautiously with large language models in a few assignments, then concluded there was no good way to use them in teaching writing. Her argument is that AI hides students' struggle from themselves and from her, and that reading and writing in college should be hard. She says the university keeps no count of how many instructors ban or embrace AI, but a Provost's Office working group paper backs her in part, saying learning means confronting "desirable difficulties". She also guesses some students skip her courses because of the policy. The piece is one instructor's view, not a campus-wide trend, but it shows the AI-free classroom becoming a stated choice at a flagship university.

Sources: MLive

3. Australian teachers' union tells a Senate committee AI has shifted workload rather than cut it

Australian Education Union federal secretary Nicole Calnan told the Joint Senate Committee on Artificial Intelligence this week that governments promoted AI as a way to reduce teacher workload, but members report it moves their time into checking and reworking outputs. She said teachers are consulted only after an AI system has been chosen, that regional, remote and Indigenous communities risk being left behind, and that leaving AI skills training to vendors reinforces existing disadvantage. She called sexualised deepfakes targeting students and teachers the most immediate harm, saying eSafety reports have doubled in 18 months, and asked for digital duty-of-care laws to cover teachers. A companion nine.com.au reader poll of 1,209 found 85 percent do not want chatbots in primary schools; it is a self-selected survey, not a representative sample.

Sources: Nine.com.au

4. Baltimore County school board hopefuls split over AI, from an outright ban to 'learn about AI, not from it'

With early voting near and four of seven Baltimore County school board seats open, candidates for the 108,000-student district disagree on whether AI belongs in classrooms at all. Lisa Mack says it does not, Maggie Litz Domanowski wants it kept away from student work ("We shouldn't be learning from AI. We should be learning about AI"), and Rod McMillion wants students exposed to it with consequences for misuse. Kelli Slattery McClelland calls AI the most urgent issue and wants use minimized until a formal district policy exists. The debate cites a PNAS study in which students with unrestricted GPT-4 access scored 48 percent better on practice problems but 17 percent worse on a later exam without AI. Maryland guidance leaves tool choices to districts, and neighbouring Frederick County has already banned student AI use.

Sources: Hoodline, The Baltimore Banner

5. Study: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot match official Lexile scores only 3% of the time

MetaMetrics, the maker of the Lexile reading framework, ran more than 20,000 analyses asking four mainstream chatbots to estimate the difficulty of 1,807 passages. Only 3 percent of the AI estimates matched the official Lexile measure, over 60 percent fell outside ranges suited to student learning, and the tools disagreed with each other and with themselves on repeat runs. In an op-ed, CEO Chris Minnich argues this matters because roughly 60 percent of teachers use AI to prepare materials and many now ask chatbots to level texts. MetaMetrics is piloting a ChatGPT app that calls validated Lexile analysis instead of guessing. Note the author sells the official measure, and this piece first ran on October 14 and was republished on October 24.

Sources: The Good Men Project (via The 74)
