1. Nearly two-thirds of Harvard faculty now say AI is hurting their courses

A Harvard Crimson survey of more than 460 Faculty of Arts and Sciences professors found that 64% said AI has had a "somewhat negative" or "very negative" effect on their courses this year — a sharp jump from 42% just a year earlier. Nearly 90% of respondents said they had received coursework they believed was AI-produced, though only 64% felt confident they could actually tell AI-written work from a student's own. AI permissiveness splits sharply by discipline: more than half of science and engineering classes surveyed in a random fall-2026 sample allow AI use, versus 35% of social-science classes and just 27% in the arts and humanities. The share of professors with no explicit AI policy at all has fallen to 3.5%, from 10% a year ago, as departments scramble to write rules faster than sentiment is souring. Inside Higher Ed picked up the findings on the same day the two-year trend line — a doubling of "negative impact" sentiment — became the story in its own right.

Sources: The Harvard Crimson, Inside Higher Ed

2. After banning phones, districts turn to banning AI smart glasses

Cambridge, Massachusetts became the latest and most-covered district to ban smart glasses and other wearable recording devices district-wide, with the School Committee voting unanimously at its first meeting of the 2026-27 year to prohibit the Meta/Ray-Ban-style eyewear (and other "surreptitious recording devices" like smartwatches) in all schools, citing spying, bullying, and Massachusetts wiretap law. Education Week reports the move is part of a wider wave: districts in Fairfax County and Prince William County, Virginia, Greenville County, South Carolina, Carson City, Nevada, and Parker, Arizona have all moved this back-to-school season to add AI-enabled smart glasses to existing cellphone bans. The concern is twofold — glasses that can silently record classmates and teachers, and glasses that give students an AI assistant to lean on during a test. Districts are carving out exceptions for students who need the devices for medical or disability accommodations, and most frame the bans as a starting point rather than a permanent policy while they watch how the technology develops.

Sources: The Harvard Crimson, Education Week, Hoodline

3. New national poll: most Americans think AI is doing more harm than good in schools

An NBC News Decision Desk poll powered by SurveyMonkey — 7,105 adults surveyed Aug. 20 to Sept. 1 — found 53% of Americans believe AI is doing more harm than good in K-12 education, versus just 27% who see more good than harm; the split is nearly identical in higher ed, at 54% harm versus 24% good. Perhaps more striking is the shift in what parents want schools to do about it: only 44% now say a school that integrates AI will better prepare students than one that bans it, down 9 percentage points from June 2025, suggesting public opinion is moving toward restriction even as adoption keeps climbing. The poll lands the same week NYC and LAUSD's AI moratoriums take effect and adds fresh, nationally representative numbers to a debate that, until now, has mostly been fought district by district.

Sources: NBC News

4. Google rebuilds Gemini Notebook into an adaptive study coach

Google shipped a bundle of new Gemini Notebook features aimed squarely at students: real-time voice conversations with your own uploaded notes, a lecture audio recorder that organizes transcripts alongside source material, interactive "learning overviews" (auto-generated quizzes and flashcards), and short video summaries of dense material. The headline addition is "Study notebooks" — students type a learning goal or upload class materials, take a diagnostic quiz, and Gemini builds a personalized sequence of bite-sized lessons that adapts as they complete more quizzes and upload new material. Eligible U.S. college students also get a year of Google AI Pro free. It's Google's most direct answer yet to purpose-built AI tutoring startups, folding adaptive-learning features that used to require a dedicated product into a tool already embedded in many students' workflow via NotebookLM.

Sources: Google (official blog), Digital Trends, Thurrott

5. Melania Trump tours a rural NC school as Nvidia and Be Best donate AI kits, amid a very different national mood on AI

Touring Mountain View Elementary in Ashe County, North Carolina — a district focused on AI, robotics, and engineering that's still rebuilding two years after Hurricane Helene — First Lady Melania Trump argued the U.S. "must lead" on AI and that students need hands-on exposure to it "to lead," while adding they need to stay "vigilant" about its risks. Her Be Best initiative and Nvidia are giving 1,500 Ashe County students subscriptions to StudyFetch, an AI-based learning platform, and donating 100 Jetson Nano developer kits — small AI-capable computers — to Mountain View and other district schools. The visit is a striking split-screen with this week's other big education story: the same week national polling shows most Americans souring on AI in the classroom, the administration's most visible messaging is a hardware-and-software donation aimed at getting more AI directly into elementary schools.

Sources: PBS NewsHour, The National Desk, WLOS
