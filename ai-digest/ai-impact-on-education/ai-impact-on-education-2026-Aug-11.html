1. The World Bank says AI could transform education in poor countries — but only after the lights and the internet arrive first

On August 4, the World Bank released its World Development Report 2026: The Promise of Artificial Intelligence, arguing AI could help developing countries leapfrog decades of gaps in education, health care, and public services within a decade. But the report is blunt about the precondition: in Sub-Saharan Africa, nearly a third of rural schools still lack reliable electricity and more than two-thirds lack dependable internet, and AI in the classroom can't reach students until those gaps close. The Bank frames its recommended path as "adopt-adapt-advance," organized around "four Cs" — connectivity, compute, context and data, and competency and skills — and argues fewer jobs in developing economies (4.5%) are at automation risk than in wealthy ones (14.2%), even though productivity gains look similar. It's a notably more cautious, infrastructure-first framing than earlier national rollout stories this digest has covered (like Kenya's junior-school digitization push) — this time it's a multilateral institution setting global expectations and warning that AI-in-education promises will stall without the basics first.

Sources: World Bank, WINS Solutions, China Global South Project

2. Florida moves to force every school district to write AI into its internet-safety policy — with a fight over opt-in vs. opt-out already brewing

Florida's Department of Education held an August 5 workshop on a proposed amendment to Rule 6A-1.0957 that would require every public and charter school board in the state to add artificial intelligence to its mandatory internet-safety policy. The draft would give districts until January 1, 2027 to adopt rules protecting students, parents, and staff from "unlawful, deceptive, unsafe, or unauthorized" AI use connected to school programs, while still supporting AI use for achievement and workforce preparation. The central fight is procedural but consequential: should students get AI access by default unless a parent opts out, or should access require a parent's affirmative opt-in first? Industry group SIIA has already asked the state to narrow the rule's scope, warning it could sweep in far more everyday classroom software than intended. Unlike the wave of individual district AI policies this digest has tracked (NYC, Fairfax, Katy ISD), this is a statewide mandate that would put roughly 4,000 Florida schools on the same compliance clock.

Sources: The Center Square, CBS News Miami, SIIA, WOKV

3. Law schools draw sharper lines on AI right as the fall semester starts — Columbia allows it with guardrails, Chicago bans devices outright

Columbia Law School's new 2026–2027 AI policy took effect August 1, permitting students to use generative AI to research and understand legal concepts but barring them from using it to draft legal analysis, form arguments, or sit exams unless a professor explicitly allows it and the student discloses the use; students remain fully responsible for anything they submit. The University of Chicago Law School took a blunter approach, banning laptops, tablets, and phones in first-year classrooms this fall specifically to keep generative AI out of the Socratic method, following UC Berkeley Law's spring ban on AI for assignments and exams. The split illustrates a distinct anxiety in legal education: unlike K-12's fights over cheating detection, law faculty increasingly worry that reflexive AI use before the bar exam could produce lawyers who never built the reasoning skills the job requires — a concern several commentators this summer pushed back on, arguing that banning AI in school just delays the reckoning new associates will face on day one at a firm.

Sources: New York Law Journal, ABA Journal, Columbia Law School, JURIST

4. The AFT/NEA-vs-DEI-in-AI fight escalates: unions circulate model policy language, conservative outlets pile on

A week after a Defending Education report accused the AFT's AI-training partnership with Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic of doubling as a DEI vehicle, the story escalated on August 10 as multiple outlets reported that the NEA is circulating a sample school-board policy that would require ed-tech vendors to build DEI safeguards into their AI tools before districts can adopt them. NEA president Becky Pringle and AFT president Randi Weingarten are named as the drivers; the AFT's National Academy for AI Instruction argues AI can "perpetuate bias" and "undermine educational equity" without guardrails. Conservative commentators and Defending Education counter that the campaign could embed DEI requirements into classroom technology before parents fully understand how the tools work, and note it could put adopting districts at odds with the Trump administration's anti-DEI funding conditions. It's the same underlying fight covered in this digest last week, now with the unions' actual policy language public and a much wider round of coverage.

5. A Central California district votes today on whether to formally welcome AI into its classrooms

The Salinas Union High School District board is scheduled to vote August 11 on a new AI policy, put forward by superintendent Zandra Jo Galván, that would take effect for the 2026–27 school year if approved. The draft commits to AI being "used ethically and transparently by all staff and students, with careful consideration of potential biases," and in compliance with intellectual-property and copyright law — deliberately general language meant to set principles rather than ban or mandate specific tools, following an AI committee the district formed back in 2023. It's one of several Monterey County districts finalizing AI rules for the new school year, alongside Pacific Grove Unified's newly approved framework covering digital safety, AI integration, and age-based screen-time recommendations. Coming right as the broader "back-to-school AI policy wave" this digest has tracked nationally plays out again, Salinas's vote is a small but concrete same-day data point on how a mid-sized district is choosing to write its own rules rather than simply restrict access.

Sources: Monterey County Now

6. Charleston County's AI policy goes live as students head back to class — "teach responsible use," not ban the technology

The Charleston County School District's board-approved AI policy, adopted in January, took practical effect this week as the district's roughly 50,000 students returned to class, per an August 11 Live5News report. Rather than restricting access, CCSD leaders framed the rollout around teaching responsible use: teachers get discretion to permit generative AI for classroom work, but the policy draws one explicit hard line — AI cannot be the sole basis for high-stakes decisions including discipline, grading, or special-education placement, and any approved AI platform must first clear district privacy and cybersecurity standards. Over the summer, CCSD teachers went through an AI training symposium with the AI for Education Foundation covering how generative AI works and how to use it for lesson planning and instruction, with the district aiming for every teacher and student to get some AI-literacy instruction this year. It's the same back-to-school policy-activation pattern this digest has tracked in Katy ISD and Woburn, but notable for spelling out, more specifically than most district policies so far, exactly which decisions AI is barred from making alone.

Sources: Live5News, ABC News 4

7. California's ~30 pending AI bills — including a classroom AI-literacy mandate — hit a do-or-die vote Thursday

California lawmakers wrapped final public comment in early August on roughly 30 outstanding AI bills, setting up a make-or-break "suspense file" hearing in the Senate and Assembly Appropriations Committees on August 13; any bill that doesn't clear that hearing is effectively dead for the 2026 session. Among the bills at stake is AB 2071, which would require California schools to fold "digital wellness" into health classes — teaching students how algorithms, social media, and AI actually work — with the state due to publish a curriculum on healthy screen habits and online safety by January 2028; it has already passed the Assembly unanimously and sits in Senate Appropriations. It runs alongside AB 2876, which would weave AI literacy directly into the state's math, science, and social-science curriculum frameworks, and follows California's already-enacted AB 2148, signed in June, which bars AI from serving as the instructor of record for K-12 students. With Sacramento setting one of the country's most detailed legislative AI-in-classroom agendas, Thursday's suspense-file vote is the clearest single checkpoint yet for how much of it survives intact.

Sources: EdSource, Transparency Coalition, Tech Times

8. AI has quietly become a college-search tool — for parents even more than for the teens applying

Two new EAB surveys this month put numbers on how thoroughly generative AI has entered the college-admissions process. Among more than 5,000 high schoolers surveyed, 46% now use tools like ChatGPT during their college search, up sharply from 26% in spring 2025, with 18% saying they dropped a school from consideration based on what an AI tool surfaced about it. A companion survey of over 2,500 parents, covered by Education Week, found roughly a third now use AI to gather and sift college information, with 34% saying it helped them discover schools they didn't already know about — though parents still lean most heavily on colleges' own websites (73%) as their top source. The same data shows AI amplifying anxiety about the payoff of college itself: 39% of students say AI is pushing them to consider alternatives to a four-year degree, and 38% believe AI will shrink the number of jobs that require one. It's a new front for a trend this digest has tracked mainly inside the classroom — AI now shaping the decision of whether, and where, to enroll in the first place.

Sources: EAB, Education Week, HigherEdJobs

9. Deepfake cyberbullying is "supercharging" — and schools are rewriting policy to catch up

An Education Week analysis published this month, drawing on a wider AP/RAND body of reporting, documents a fast-growing form of AI misuse in K-12 schools: students using generative tools to create nonconsensual, sexualized, or defamatory fake images and videos of classmates and, increasingly, teachers. RAND and NEA reporting cited in the piece finds 40–50% of students are already aware of deepfakes circulating at their own school, and districts are struggling to figure out discipline and victim-support responses since most existing cyberbullying policies were never written with AI image generation in mind. Some districts are catching up in real time — Illinois's Willowbrook High School, for instance, rewrote its cyberbullying policy this year to explicitly define deepfakes and spell out that creating or sharing nonconsensual sexual images is a criminal offense, following a 2025 Illinois law directing every school in the state to do the same. The story sits alongside this digest's earlier coverage of a Nashville teacher fired for AI-altering student images and Common Sense Media's finding that 44% of teens have seen AI-generated sexual content — together pointing to deepfakes as one of the fastest-moving safety problems in K-12 AI policy, well ahead of most districts' rulebooks.

Sources: Education Week, PBS NewsHour (AP), NEA Today, RAND
