1. UNAM's AI-cheating investigation results are in: 3,000 tests voided, 58,000 applicants face an in-person do-over

Updating our July 29 coverage: on July 31 Mexico's National Autonomous University (UNAM) received its special commission's findings on the admissions-exam scandal that has gripped the country's largest public university since mid-July — roughly 3,000 of the ~150,000 online entrance exams were formally invalidated after investigators confirmed cheating via hidden phones, companions, and in some cases identity theft. Rather than limit the fallout to those flagged, UNAM is now requiring an in-person "control exam" for every applicant who scored a passing mark this year or in any of the past five years — an estimated 58,000 people — before the new semester opens August 10. Rector Leonardo Lomelí has publicly appealed for "ethical behavior" from re-applicants scrambling to prepare with barely a week's notice, and CNN reports individual students still don't know if they'll be allowed to start classes on time. The episode is becoming a cautionary tale for any institution moving high-stakes assessment online: it was UNAM's own exam-monitoring system that first flagged the anomalous spike in perfect scores that triggered the probe.

Sources: CNN, KVIA (CNN wire), Mezha

2. AI is quietly killing the computer science major — and building an "AI literacy" one in its place

Same-day reporting from the AP wire, Fortune, and Built In (all August 3) captures a fast-moving shift in U.S. higher ed: undergraduate computer science enrollment fell 8.1% at four-year colleges in fall 2025 — the steepest drop of any major field and CS's first enrollment decline in nearly 20 years — as students grow wary of AI eating entry-level coding jobs. Rather than retreat from AI, colleges are racing to spread it wider instead: UT Austin's Peter Stone built an "AI essentials" course for non-CS majors ("We have to democratize it," he says), VCU and Purdue are rolling out AI minors and graduation requirements, and Harvard now teaches large-language-model literacy in freshman writing. A July 29 Inside Higher Ed opinion piece ties the trend to real labor-market pain — over 200 tech companies have cut roughly 124,000 jobs in 2026, including 10%+ workforce reductions at Meta, Coinbase, and Block — and argues CS should be reframed as a foundational discipline like math rather than a job-training pipeline. Net effect: fewer students are majoring in "coding," but far more of them, across every field, are now required to learn AI.

Sources: Fortune, AP News (via WRAL), Built In, Inside Higher Ed (opinion)

3. A 26,000-student study puts a number on the "AI learning penalty": +18% homework, -20% exams

A CEPR working paper, "The Generative AI Learning Penalty" (Stockholm University and University of Hong Kong), tracked 26,811 Chinese secondary students for 30 months and found generative-AI tool use raised homework scores 18% and cut completion time 30% — but within six months the same students' monthly exam scores had fallen 20%, and after two years their scores on China's high-stakes zhongkao and gaokao entrance exams dropped 24% and 18% respectively. Researchers attribute roughly 80% of the decline to "homework outsourcing" — students who finish assignments unusually fast with high scores, a signature of copy-paste AI use — while students who kept normal completion times saw only small losses; the effect was worst among junior students, high achievers, and boys, and concentrated in social-science subjects. Now three weeks past its initial release and still generating fresh coverage (Fortune published a new writeup this week), the study is being cited by researchers and district leaders as some of the most concrete evidence yet that AI-assisted homework can mask, rather than build, the skills exams are designed to measure. Note on timing: the underlying research and first news coverage date to mid-to-late July; we include it here because it remains actively cited in this week's discourse and had not yet appeared in this digest.

Sources: CEPR (working paper), South China Morning Post, Fortune

4. An Ivy League professor says AI cheating gutted his class — and that Brown sat on it for weeks

Brown University economics professor Roberto Serrano has gone public with what he says is evidence of mass AI cheating in his ECON 1170 course: after moving exams online, his class size jumped to a record 86 students, the take-home midterm averaged 96/100 with 40 perfect scores and ChatGPT-like phrasing, and the average collapsed to 48 once he switched the final to an in-person format. Serrano says Brown was slow to act until his account, first reported by El País and then Inside Higher Ed on July 8, went viral; the university disputes the "lackluster response" characterization, saying academic leaders reached out in May and its Standing Committee on the Academic Code began formally interviewing students after Serrano supplied evidence on July 8. The case — voided midterm grades, a reweighted final, and an ongoing student-by-student review — has become one of the most closely watched tests of how elite universities handle suspected AI cheating at scale, echoing similar disputes already underway at Yale and Adelphi. Note on timing: this story broke in mid-July and is included here as a fallback item because it had not yet appeared in this digest and Serrano's public criticism of Brown's response is still developing.

Sources: Fox News, Boston Globe, Inside Higher Ed

5. Kenya wires up 10,382 junior schools for an AI-ready classroom, training 62,000 teachers along the way

Kenya's government has begun rolling out its National Integration of ICT Learning in Junior Schools programme, launched July 12 by Deputy President Kithure Kindiki, which is equipping all 10,382 public junior secondary schools nationwide with teacher laptops and interactive smart boards — more than 20,700 devices across all 47 counties — to support the country's AI-era Competency-Based Education curriculum. The World Bank-backed Kenya Digital Economy Acceleration Project has already trained more than 62,000 junior-school teachers to integrate the new technology, and the launch event featured a live-streamed science lesson beamed from one rural school to classrooms in six counties, including Nairobi and Mombasa. It is one of the largest single national hardware-and-training pushes tied explicitly to AI-readiness goals so far in 2026, and a marker of how the "AI in education" story is playing out very differently in infrastructure-building economies than in the assessment-integrity battles dominating this week's US and Latin American headlines. Note on timing: the launch took place July 12; we include it as a fallback item because it had not yet appeared in this digest and rollout/training activity is ongoing.

Sources: TechAfrica News, Kenyans.co.ke, CIO Africa
