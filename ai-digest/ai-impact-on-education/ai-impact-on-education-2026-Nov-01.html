1. NSW pushes to bring back pen-and-paper NAPLAN writing tests, citing screens and AI

New South Wales will ask Australia's education ministers, meeting on 2 November, to return NAPLAN writing tests for Years 5, 7 and 9 to pen and paper from 2028; only Year 3 sits the test on paper today. The state says recent results reinforced a national decline in writing, and it links the move to screen-time limits, new guidance of at least an hour of paper-based learning a day, and recent limits on take-home HSC assessments aimed at curbing AI use. NSW's own research fund found only about a third of primary-school parents think technology's benefits outweigh its drawbacks. It is another sign that assessment design, not just AI policy, is becoming the lever schools pull against generative AI.

Sources: Mirage News (NSW Government release)

2. New study: pupils write more creatively with GenAI but are the least absorbed in the task

A study in the Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, reported by The Educator, found primary students writing with generative AI outscored peers writing alone or with classmates on creative writing, yet were the least absorbed in the work; students who believed in their own writing ability also did better. Monash University's Professor Mary Ryan points to a 'performance paradox', where AI lifts short-term output while potentially undermining durable learning, and cites estimates that at least 80% of Australian teenagers and over two-thirds of early secondary teachers use AI. Both she and writing-program head Kathleen Killick argue that idea generation, planning and drafting should be explicitly taught first, so students can judge whether AI output is any good.

Sources: The Educator K/12

3. European schools have no common rule on AI smart glasses as the EDPB and Austria weigh in

A collaborative European report finds the EU has no common ban or regulation on AI-enabled smart glasses in schools, even though recording students can fall under GDPR. The European Data Protection Board issued a statement this month warning of serious privacy concerns, with greater risk in sensitive places such as schools, while Austria's digitalisation secretary of state has proposed banning the glasses there. Italy, Spain and Greece rely on existing privacy or device rules, and Ireland's education department points to schools' acceptable-use policies. France's bac fraud reportedly rose 30% this year, with supervisors flagging smart glasses as a blind spot, and a Vienna court overturned one student's expulsion over his glasses, holding that mere discomfort is not grounds for exclusion.

Sources: The Journal (PULSE Europe)

4. London teacher and Micro:bit foundation back a 'GCSE in AI'

Metro profiles a Highgate primary teacher who builds AI into lessons, from fact-checking chatbot answers to teaching how machine-learning models work, and reports that the Micro:bit Educational Foundation wants the government to introduce an AI GCSE. Cited surveys say over nine in ten teens use AI while only 15% feel they have enough guidance. Campaigners warn the idea carries equity risks: the Digital Poverty Alliance found 30% of teachers have at least ten pupils without a suitable device or connection at home. Some pupils interviewed want AI kept out of homework altogether. The Department for Education had been approached for comment.

Sources: Metro

5. Update: Google's deadline to answer Hawley and Blumenthal on classroom AI arrives, with new crisis-prompt figures

The Hawley-Blumenthal letter (covered on 22 October) reaches its 2 November response deadline, and newly reported detail sharpens it: nine questions covering default-on AI features schools cannot switch off, crisis and self-harm detection, complaints received and promotional spending. Coverage also cites a July assessment, reported but not independently verified, in which Google's AI Overview gave appropriate resources in 58% of 214 crisis prompts and AI Mode in 77%, plus a RAND finding that 67% of teens think AI harms critical thinking. No detailed Google reply had been reported as of 1 November, when Google said it builds age-appropriate safeguards and works with educators.

Sources: Shattered, Times of India
