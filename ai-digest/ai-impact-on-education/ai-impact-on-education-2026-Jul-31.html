1. Houston ISD starts converting public elementary schools to AI-run "Future 2" campuses

Houston Independent School District — Texas's largest, still run by a state-appointed superintendent — has begun converting Gregg and Clemente Martinez elementary schools into "Future 2" campuses that use an AI-powered instructional platform (built with the Alpha School model) in place of traditional whole-class teaching, with students working through core subjects on an AI system while teachers act more as coaches. The pilot expands to nine campuses for the 2026–27 school year, with Superintendent Mike Miles's stated goal of scaling to 25 schools by 2027–28 and 100 by 2031. It's a notable escalation of the "AI replaces the classroom teacher" model from expensive private schools like Alpha into a large public system serving low-income students, and will be closely watched as a test of whether the approach holds up outside a $40,000-a-year private-school setting.

Sources: Houston Chronicle, GovTech, Houston Public Media, The Center Square

2. "Click the link to watch the full video answer": ChatGPT phrase exposes mass cheating at an Indian university

An evaluator at Dr. Harisingh Gour Central University in Sagar, Madhya Pradesh, spotted the sentence "Click the link to watch the full video answer" written verbatim on a student's answer sheet — a stray line copied straight out of a ChatGPT response. The discovery triggered an investigation that found dozens of students at an affiliated college, Satpura College of Information and Biotechnology in Chhindwara, had copied near-identical AI-generated answers across three separate subjects during their exams, apparently using phones during the test. The university has withheld exam results for the entire affiliated college while it investigates, even though results for the main university and its other colleges had already been released — the latest sign that AI-enabled cheating detection is now happening by accident (a hallucinated or boilerplate phrase slipping through) as often as by design.

Sources: IBTimes India, India TV News

3. A disputed Yale exam and an "unreliable" AI detector escalate into a 13-count federal lawsuit

A Yale School of Management student, Thierry Rignol, suspended after GPTZero flagged his final exam as AI-generated, has turned his wrongful-suspension complaint into a sprawling 13-count federal lawsuit against the university. A new Ars Technica investigation lays out how a disputed take-home exam and a detector whose reliability is itself contested spiraled into a case now testing how much due process students are owed when an algorithm — not a person — makes the accusation. Legal commentary on the case argues Yale's process gave the student little chance to rebut a black-box tool's verdict, a due-process question that is likely to recur as more universities lean on AI detectors for high-stakes discipline decisions.

Sources: Ars Technica, Yale Daily News, Crowell & Moring

4. New York's Senate education chair moves to ban humanoid AI robot teachers statewide

State Sen. Shelley Mayer, chair of the New York Senate's Education Committee, has introduced S.10671, legislation that would bar every school district, charter school, and BOCES in the state from using AI-enabled humanoid robots to perform teaching duties — delivering instruction, tutoring, grading, or evaluating students. The bill is a direct response to the Salamanca school district's paused "Sally" robot, covered in yesterday's digest, and follows sharp criticism from the state teachers union over the robot maker's ties to a sex-doll company. It's the first statewide legislative proposal of its kind aimed squarely at humanoid classroom robots, rather than AI software more broadly, and would make New York the first state to codify a ban on the category outright.

Sources: CBS6 Albany, CBS6 Albany (follow-up), Yonkers Times

5. Anthropic's free K-12 tool, Claude for Teachers, draws a student-privacy critique from education researchers

The 74 published a critical analysis of Claude for Teachers, the free K-12-focused version of Anthropic's chatbot that launched in mid-July, arguing the product's design — pulling in students' past assessment, assignment, and lesson data to help teachers build differentiated lesson plans — can nudge teachers into entering identifiable student data in ways that may sidestep district data-governance policy and create FERPA exposure, even with good intentions. Anthropic says training is off by default for verified educator accounts, conversations aren't used to train its models, and terms include FERPA-aligned commitments and a defined deletion SLA; EdWeek's coverage of the launch likewise noted mixed reactions from privacy-focused critics alongside praise from teacher groups like the American Federation of Teachers, which partnered on the release. The episode is a preview of a wider tension as every major AI lab races to put free tools directly in teachers' hands: contractual privacy promises are only as good as what actually happens once a teacher pastes a struggling student's data into a chat window.

Sources: The 74, Education Week, Anthropic (product announcement)
