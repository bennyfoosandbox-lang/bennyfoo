1. NYC's fight over an AI moratorium in public schools escalates as the chancellor freezes new tech purchases

New York City Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels has asked principals across the nation's largest school system to hold off on new AI and edtech software purchases until the Education Department finalizes guidance first floated as a draft back in March, Chalkbeat and Gothamist reported. The freeze follows a July 29 rally at City Hall where parents, teachers, students and more than half of the City Council signed onto a call for a full two-year moratorium on AI across the system's 1.1-million-student footprint, backed by an online petition that has drawn nearly 4,700 signatures. An August 7 Christian Science Monitor feature frames the standoff as part of a national "gold rush," quoting a Peninsula School District technology chief on the sector-wide land-grab dynamic and noting that Anthropic and OpenAI are both offering free premium classroom access — Claude for Teachers through June 2027, ChatGPT for Teachers through June 2028 — that critics see as a bid to lock in paying customers once the free windows close. The standoff makes NYC a bellwether: if the country's largest district holds the line on a purchase freeze or moratorium, other cities facing similar parent-led pressure will be watching closely.

Sources: Chalkbeat, Gothamist, Christian Science Monitor, Class Size Matters, K-12 Dive

2. Fairfax County bans generative AI for elementary students and pulls devices from pre-K and kindergarten

Fairfax County Public Schools — Virginia's largest district — approved sweeping new tech restrictions on July 23 that bar elementary-school students from using generative AI tools altogether, while secondary students can only use them with written authorization from a principal, superintendent, or designee for a specific project or purpose. The same policy strips FCPS-issued laptops and tablets from prekindergarten and kindergarten classrooms starting in the 2026-27 school year, carving out exceptions only for students with an IEP, a 504 plan, or multilingual-education needs. The board also directed the superintendent to deliver a full inventory of every generative-AI tool present on district-issued devices by January 14, 2027, and to pilot a "whitelist"-style firewall restricting which apps, extensions and websites students can reach. It's among the most restrictive generative-AI stances taken by a major US district so far this back-to-school season, running counter to the more permissive, teacher-discretion-based policies most districts have adopted.

Sources: FFXnow, Patch (McLean), Annandale Today

3. A federal K-12 AI literacy bill clears its first hurdle in the House

The K-12 AI Literacy and Readiness Act of 2026 (H.R. 8747), introduced by Rep. Randy Fine (R-FL) in May, passed the House Education and Workforce Committee this week — the first time federal legislation specifically aimed at classroom AI literacy has advanced past committee. The bill doesn't create new spending or mandate a national curriculum; instead, it amends existing law so states and districts can count AI-literacy curricula, tools, and staff training toward existing federal K-12 funding streams, covering teachers, librarians, support staff and administrators alongside students. The Computer & Communications Industry Association publicly praised the committee vote, with the group's Michael Marn calling AI literacy "quickly becoming a core workforce skill" that students need "so the United States can fully capture the benefits of AI." The bill still needs a full House floor vote and Senate companion legislation before it could become law, but committee passage puts K-12 AI funding flexibility on Congress's active agenda for the first time this session.

Sources: Congress.gov, Rep. Randy Fine's office, Bloomberg Government, Legis1

4. Three years of survey data: AI skills go from résumé boost to baseline job requirement

A three-year student-led research project out of American University's Kogod School of Business, authored by recent graduate Christina Eid, finds that the share of business students reporting employers asked about their AI skills during hiring interviews jumped from 11.6% in 2024 to 42.6% in 2026 — a 285% increase in two years — while regular AI use among the same student population rose from 6.2% to 29% over the same stretch. More than 80% of surveyed students say they've used AI for academic work in the past six months, and nearly a third now use it 11 or more times a week for school or work, according to the report, "AI at Kogod." But the same students are increasingly anxious about it: concern over AI's effect on future career prospects rose from 13.4% to 20.2%, and Axios and The Next Web both frame the findings as puncturing a "student AI revolt" narrative — students aren't resisting the technology so much as demanding clearer ethical guidance on how and when to use it. The results echo a theme surfacing across multiple higher-ed surveys this summer: student usage has outpaced institutional guidance almost everywhere it's been measured.

Sources: Kogod School of Business, Axios, Poets&Quants, The Next Web

5. A Virginia school board strips AI-specific language from its code of conduct, distrusting the detectors that would enforce it

The Manassas City School Board voted 6-1 on July 28 to approve its 2026-27 Student Code of Conduct without any explicit reference to artificial intelligence, after a two-week fight over a proposal from board member Sara Brescia that would have explicitly classified unauthorized generative-AI use as plagiarism. Opposition centered on enforcement: one board member cited a prior demonstration showing AI-detection software with roughly a 50% error rate, including a case where it flagged a fully human-written document as AI-generated, and argued that codifying detailed AI rules without a reliable way to enforce them would create more disputes than it resolved. Board chair Jill Spall said she personally wanted "AI" to appear in the document and stood by her vote reluctantly, and several members floated returning with simplified language at the board's August 11 meeting — just before students return — though handbook printing deadlines may not allow it. The episode is a small but telling data point in a now-familiar pattern: districts and universities alike are backing away from writing AI-specific rules into policy because the detection tools meant to enforce them remain too unreliable to trust.

Sources: Potomac Local (July 30), Potomac Local (July 15)
