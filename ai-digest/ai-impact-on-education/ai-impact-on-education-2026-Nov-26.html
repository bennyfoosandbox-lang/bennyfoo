1. Lilly Endowment approves $291.7M in AI grants for 35 Indiana colleges

Lilly Endowment has approved 35 implementation grants worth a combined $291.71 million under its Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education initiative, with awards running from $2.5 million to $25 million. The grants were approved on 23 November, and campuses announced them through 24–26 November. Indiana University, Purdue and Ivy Tech each received the $25 million maximum; IU says its award will fund AI in undergraduate teaching and student support across its nine campuses. Vincennes University got $12.5 million, the University of Southern Indiana $8.5 million over three years, and DePauw, Anderson and the University of Saint Francis $5 million each, mostly for AI literacy, faculty development and campus-wide AI strategy. This is the second phase of a programme launched in November 2025 with up to $500 million and planning grants of $125,000–$300,000. It turns a whole state's higher-education sector into a funded test of AI in teaching just as faculty senates elsewhere are pushing back on vendor-led rollouts. The $291.71 million total comes from one aggregator, CASRAI; the individual awards are confirmed by the campuses' own announcements.

Sources: CASRAI (round total), The Bloomingtonian (IU $25M), GlobeNewswire (DePauw), University of Southern Indiana, Yahoo News (Vincennes), PR Newswire (Anderson), 21Alive (Saint Francis), Lilly Endowment AIHE programme

2. England’s DfE warns schools that AI tools are giving them wrong answers about results checking

England’s Department for Education has told schools to be careful using AI tools for the autumn “checking exercise”, the yearly process in which schools confirm that DfE’s SATs, GCSE and 16–18 results data for them is correct. Schools Week reports that officials looked into an AI link after more than 100 enquiries about “problems” with the exercise. The department now cites reports that AI tools can generate incorrect information about how the process works. It is one of the first official cases of chatbot errors spilling into a statutory school data process, as opposed to classroom use. It also comes as the DfE runs its consultation on safety certification for edtech and AI products, and as critics say the department’s latest AI guidance pushes the risk onto schools. The details come from Schools Week’s report, published 26 November, which could not be opened from the cloud runner, so this summary relies on its search-indexed excerpt.

Sources: Schools Week, Schools Week (DfE AI materials critique), Lexology (DfE certification consultation)

3. Google DeepMind and Eedi’s bigger AI-tutor trial: wins in lessons, no gain on standardised maths tests

The second, larger randomised trial of Google’s LearnLM tutor in UK secondary maths randomly assigned 1,179 students in ten schools to one of four groups: Eedi’s standard hints, expert human tutors, or one of two AI tutor arms. Over ten weeks the AI arms produced clear gains on daily classroom tasks and quizzes and matched human tutors at fixing mistakes in the moment. Human supervisors approved 94.7% of the AI’s drafted messages without changes. On the Star Maths standardised test, though, students in both AI arms gained no more than students who got standard remediation, which Eedi’s Bibi Groot calls a null effect. One explanation the teams give is a breadth-versus-depth trade-off: students in the AI arms worked through about 99 questions, against about 132 with static practice and about 82 with human tutors. Eedi is now running a full-year trial with 15,000 students in 26 state schools. The result is a needed reality check on headline claims about AI tutors. This is a fallback item: coverage has built up over the past few weeks, and the study has not yet been peer-reviewed.

Sources: The 74, Google / Eedi LearnLM report (PDF), Eedi Substack (Bibi Groot), Google blog, Tina Austin (analysis)

4. NYU Tandon’s traffic-light playbook says the real AI risk is learning loss, not cheating

NYU’s Tandon School of Engineering has published “Protecting Learning”, a practical guide to AI-use policy for instructors, departments and schools. A faculty working group in Tandon’s AI Across the Curriculum initiative wrote it. The guide sorts assessments into three tiers. Red means AI is banned and students must show independent competence; yellow allows AI in limited supporting roles; green treats using AI well as part of the skill being assessed. Where independent competence matters, it recommends checks done without AI, such as oral questions, supervised quizzes and in-class exams. The authors say their biggest worry is not cheating but students handing in correct work without having mastered the material. The guide is explicitly not official Tandon policy. Feedback gathered in November will shape a first revision in January 2027. NYU’s student paper covered it on 24 November, a sign that faculty-written frameworks are filling the gap left by slow institution-wide rules.

Sources: NYU Tandon announcement, Washington Square News, NYU Tandon AI-Use Policy Guide, AI Weekly

5. Newport-Mesa asked 1,400 students about AI, and most wanted clearer rules, not a ban

At the CITE26 conference, California’s Newport-Mesa Unified School District explained how it used student input to shape its AI strategy, GovTech reported on 25 November. A district survey of about 1,400 students found that 82% had used AI in the previous 30 days. The most common request was clearer expectations about when AI use is allowed. That puts the district on the side of governing AI students already use, while several large California and national districts have gone the other way with pauses and moratoriums, and the California Federation of Teachers has called for a three-year K-8 pause. A separate CITE26 session described the state’s AI guidance as “a map rather than a mandate”. It recommends that teachers spell out acceptable use and not rely only on AI detectors when deciding discipline. The details come from conference coverage, not a published district report.

Sources: GovTech (Newport-Mesa), GovTech (California guidance), GovTech (CFT K-8 pause call)
