1. Federal research push: a coalition says schools are adopting AI with almost no causal evidence

The Alliance for Learning Innovation released federal policy recommendations urging Congress and the administration to fund R&D on safe and effective AI in classrooms. The report leans on a Stanford SCALE review of more than 800 K-12 AI papers, only about 20 of which produced strong causal evidence, even as most teens already use AI for schoolwork. The timing is awkward: IES lost roughly 90% of its staff, went over 15 months without a grant competition, and OMB has yet to release more than half of its 2026 budget, though lawmakers from both parties rejected proposed two-thirds cuts and a bipartisan Senate bill on education R&D was reintroduced last month. Principals say what they most need is funded professional development, model policies and training time.

Sources: Education Week, Alliance for Learning Innovation report

2. OpenAI’s head of education: AI could “enfeeble the next generation” and mostly lifts the already curious

In a Forbes interview following OpenAI’s expansion of ChatGPT for Teachers to 55 more school systems (100,000+ additional educators), VP of education Leah Belsky warned that without a plan for effective use, AI risks enfeebling students, and said students with stronger prior knowledge gain the most. OpenAI’s own usage data shows power users are about seven times likelier to pick the most capable models, a pattern that could replay school stratification at scale. The piece argues for assessing process rather than finished products, citing a Duke Fuqua professor who recorded team meetings and used AI analysis to expose shallow understanding of course frameworks. It also points to OpenAI’s Learning Outcomes Measurement Suite, with a randomized trial under way in Estonia. This is a new angle on a rollout already covered in August.

Sources: Forbes, OpenAI

3. Will the AI backlash leave low-income students behind?

A Minnesota Star Tribune column argues that wealthier schools and micro-schools are moving toward human-centered, screen-light education while poorer schools are pushed toward apps and robots. Columnist David Perry cites the University of Chicago’s new AI ban in its social science core and UC Berkeley Law’s AI policy, and notes that Google executives reportedly send their own children to screen-free schools while Google promotes AI in classrooms. It is opinion rather than data, but it frames a live equity question: the digital divide may be flipping from access to technology into access to human instruction.

Sources: Star Tribune

4. Falls Church schools publish their approved AI tools — and the questions they haven’t answered

Falls Church City Public Schools told the local News-Press which AI tools teachers and students may use. Teachers get four (Google Gemini, SchoolAI, Diffit and Canva); students get two, with SchoolAI for grades 6–12 and Gemini for grades 9–12, while ChatGPT and Copilot are off both lists and K–5 students do not use generative AI independently. The district says AI is not used for grading, discipline, special education or placement decisions and that vendors may not train on student data, but it has not said what data vendors actually see or what AI costs. It is a useful small-district case of a written policy that still leaves transparency gaps.

Sources: Falls Church News-Press

5. Sharjah issues an AI ethics framework for private schools

The Sharjah Private Education Authority launched an ethical framework for AI in schools covering fairness, transparency, accountability, human oversight, cybersecurity and verification of AI output. Schools must set clear rules on AI in assignments and assessments, require disclosure of AI use, and ensure AI-generated material does not replace students’ original work. It assigns responsibilities to school leaders, teachers, students, IT teams and parents, and extends to AI use in school media and communications. It aligns with UAE national directives and is another sign of Gulf regulators moving from encouragement to explicit guardrails.

Sources: Gulf Business
