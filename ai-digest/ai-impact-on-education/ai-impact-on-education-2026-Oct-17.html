1. Bihar drafts a state AI policy for universities, with disclosure rules for teachers and students

The Bihar government is preparing an AI policy for all state universities and higher-education institutions, with Deputy Chief Minister Samrat Chaudhary directing the Higher Education and Science & Technology departments to draft it after consulting experts. The framework would cover answer-script evaluation, question-paper preparation, curriculum design, virtual labs and student-support chatbots, and would be run by an advisory committee and an implementation committee. The most notable provision is disclosure: teachers would have to declare AI use in curricula, study materials and exam papers, and students would have to state whether they used AI on assignments. The aim is to issue the policy before the next academic session, though the final text and timeline still depend on the drafting and consultations.

Sources: Patna Press

2. Survey of 150 Cambodian lecturers: 93% have used AI to teach, but training is the top ask

A new survey in Discover Artificial Intelligence, covering 150 lecturers across Cambodian universities, finds that only 7.3% have never used an AI tool for teaching. The most-used tools were Google Translate (70.7%), Grammarly (66%) and ChatGPT (62%), mainly to create teaching materials, brainstorm activities and simplify technical concepts. Attitudes were positive overall (mean 3.69 of 5) but dipped on trusting AI to grade fairly (3.25) and on memory-loss worries (3.17), while 86% said training and technical support should come first. The authors caution that the sample is a small, convenience one that skews male and relies on self-reports, but it is one of the few systematic looks at AI in a lower-resourced higher-education system.

Sources: Bioengineer.org (Scienmag report), Discover Artificial Intelligence (DOI)

3. Structured AI use lifted future teachers' creativity and attitudes in a five-week Turkish study

Researchers at Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University tested generative AI (ChatGPT, Claude, NotebookLM, Gamma, Suno, Perplexity) in a five-week Philosophy of Education course with 50 pre-service teachers. The AI group's positive attitudes toward AI rose from 3.18 to 3.69, four of six metacreativity dimensions improved significantly, and their adjusted post-test achievement beat the control group (Cohen's d of 0.66), while the control group stayed flat on creativity measures. Students were told to interrogate and revise AI output rather than accept it, and the authors credit that design rather than the tools themselves. Limits are real: a small single-university sample, different programmes in each group, self-reported creativity, and no delayed follow-up.

Sources: Bioengineer.org (Scienmag report), Discover Artificial Intelligence (DOI)

4. Language educator warns AI could make teachers more productive but less competent

In a widely shared post, language-teaching specialist Gianfranco Conti argues that routine delegation of lesson planning, diagnosis and feedback to AI risks professional deskilling: weaker pedagogical content knowledge, less reflective practice, reduced autonomy and fewer chances for novice teachers to build expertise. He lists ten specific risks and notes a growing 'illusion of competence', where polished AI-made materials mask thin understanding. Importantly, he concedes that longitudinal evidence of measurable deterioration is still limited and that teachers who critically challenge AI output may strengthen their expertise. It is an opinion piece rather than research, but it shifts the debate from student cheating to what AI does to the teaching profession.

Sources: The Language Gym

5. Nigerian startup UniBeta rebuilds around AI exam revision tuned to local syllabuses

TechCabal profiles UniBeta, founded at the University of Lagos, which abandoned a failed campus 'super-app' to build UniBeta AI, launched in October 2025. Students upload photocopied course packs and past papers, and the model finds recurring topic clusters to generate flashcards, quizzes and summaries, on the premise that Nigerian federal-university lecturers repeat topics rather than exact questions. It competes with general chatbots like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude by targeting local syllabuses and charges roughly ₦2,000-₦6,000 for paid plans. The founders say exam-season demand is highly seasonal, and are in talks with Nigeria's National Universities Commission on a shared data structure to scale nationwide.

Sources: TechCabal
