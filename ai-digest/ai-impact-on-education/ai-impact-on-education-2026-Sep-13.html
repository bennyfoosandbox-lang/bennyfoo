1. Maryland districts race a state deadline: AI policies due October 22, and the votes are starting

Talbot County's school board unanimously passed a districtwide AI policy this week, and Washington County's board gave first-reading approval to its own "Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence in Teaching, Learning and School Operations" policy — both moving to comply with Maryland's Artificial Intelligence Ready Schools Act, which requires every local school system in the state to adopt a board-approved AI policy by October 22 and name a non-instructional central-office "AI coordinator." The state law, passed by the General Assembly in May, leaves districts free to choose their own tools but makes student privacy, data security and human oversight non-negotiable baseline requirements. Talbot's policy explicitly states AI must "support, but not replace" professional judgment, authentic student learning and independent decision-making; Washington County's board discussion also surfaced a less common concern — a student board member raised the environmental cost of AI data centers during deliberation. With the deadline five weeks out, expect a wave of similar votes across Maryland's 24 school systems this month.

Sources: Star Democrat, LocalNews1, WBOC, Maryland Matters

2. McGraw Hill tells Wall Street its AI bet is curriculum, not chatbots

At the Goldman Sachs Communacopia + Technology Conference on September 8, McGraw Hill CEO Philip Moyer pitched the 130-year-old textbook publisher as a long-term winner from AI in education — but drew a sharp line against the generic-chatbot approach of newer entrants. The company says it has deployed AI products to more than 7.5 million users across its 100-million-plus paid-subscriber base, building tools on top of its own licensed curriculum content rather than wrapping a general-purpose model around it; one product, Sharpen, was described internally as "Spotify for professors," bundling content into playlists with built-in engagement tracking. Moyer was candid about the headwinds — education is a heavily regulated, fragmented market where trust and local rules slow adoption, and teacher skepticism about AI remains real — but framed McGraw Hill's curriculum ownership and existing distribution as a moat that pure-play AI edtech startups lack. It's a notable data point in the AI-in-education story: the incumbent publishers are betting their advantage is proprietary content, not model access.

Sources: Investing.com, Seeking Alpha, CNBC, StockTitan

3. Study: AI graders reward "style over substance," and inflate the weakest essays most

A study making the rounds again this month — first published in late August and still being picked up by outlets into September — found that AI models consistently mark student essays higher than human graders do, and can't be relied on for an accurate read of performance. Researchers uploaded essays to multiple AI systems under several prompting conditions and found "significant discrepancies" against human marks in all but one test case; in the most extreme example, an AI grade differed from a human grade by 40 points on a 100-point scale. The pattern cut against what you might expect: lower-scoring essays were the ones most likely to get inflated by AI, while stronger essays were sometimes marked down relative to human graders, with the models rewarding essay length, vocabulary range and sentence complexity over the actual quality of the argument — "style over substance," as the researchers put it. It adds to a growing body of evidence, alongside a separate Cambridge-led study of frontier models on UK university essays, that AI grading remains far from a drop-in replacement for human assessment even as districts and colleges rush to deploy it.

Sources: Inside Higher Ed, Times Higher Education, TaxProf Blog
