01 · The idea

A quiz for fans of every fandom

A “multistan” is a fan of more than one group. The game asks one question, “Are you a multistan?”, and tests it with trivia across 101 groups rather than a single fandom.[1]

From the start the brief was a game you could share with friends and host for free, with no paid backend.[3]

02 · How it was built

Data first, then the game, then the growth agents

  • Knowledge base. Members, roles, debut dates, companies and fandom names for each group were researched into structured files, with member photos credited to Kpop Wiki and Wikimedia Commons.[2][1]
  • Game engine. A single-page React app generates 15 question types from that data: oldest member, leader, debut year, company, photo rounds and more. 15 seconds per question, with a speed bonus and streaks.[3]
  • Multiplayer without a server. Quizzes come from a seeded random generator, so a challenge link replays the same questions for every friend and carries the scores inside the link.[3]
  • Live play. The game later moved to Firebase, adding a global leaderboard and real-time rooms where everyone answers the same question at once.[1][5]
  • Growth agents. Scheduled Claude routines took roles as Analyst, Scout, Creator, Publisher and Reporter, reading analytics, vetting communities and drafting posts for human approval.[4]
03 · What went well

Tested at scale, used by real fans

  • 12,000 questions checked. Before launch, 600 seeded quizzes were generated in a headless browser with zero invalid answer options.[3]
  • Real players. The global leaderboard is live, and in one 24-hour window in October fans sent 36 score submissions and 40 new votes for the fan Top 10.[1][6]
  • Agents that caught problems. The Analyst flagged missing analytics events and a heavy page, both fixed. The Scout found that r/kpop bans self-promotion and pointed posts to r/kpoppers instead.[4]
  • Sourced questions. Weekly challenge questions go through a validator that rejects anything with fewer than two sources.[5]
04 · Lessons learned

What I would carry into the next build

  • Measure what is live. Two early performance reports measured code that had not shipped yet. A speed claim only counts when it is tested against production.[4]
  • Real-time needs a server. Link-based challenges went far with zero infrastructure, but “everyone answers at once” needed a backend, which is why the game moved to Firebase.[3][1]
  • Seeded content is fragile. Any data update changes the questions a seeded link produces, so old challenge links can drift.[3]
  • Label confidence honestly. When the agents could not open news sites, their questions stayed marked “Likely” rather than “Confirmed”. A human still ticks the final picks.[5]

01 · The idea

How much can one person and a team of agents do?

Void Deck Whisper started as an experiment in that question. Instead of asking one AI for everything, the work is split into desks the way a real newsroom is: each agent has one job, one set of rules and the right to say no.[2]

The name comes from the void deck, the open ground floor of a Singapore HDB block where neighbours meet.[1]

02 · How it was built

A newsroom of personas, with a pipeline and gates

  • Personas, not people. Each of the 12 “staff” is a set of instructions for Claude, with illustrated faces, and the site says so plainly.[2]
  • Six steps per story. Pitch, research from official sources, draft, fact-check, compliance review, then human approval.[2]
  • Gatekeepers with a veto. The fact-checker labels each claim confirmed, likely or disputed and can block any story. The compliance agent flags anything that needs a real lawyer.[2]
  • Build checks. Every page must pass: no broken links, one headline per page, and every confidence label linked to its source.[3]
  • Free to run. Free hosting, with the agents’ work coming out of an existing Claude plan.[2]
03 · What went well

A working site in one week

  • Speed. From one sample article to a live site in a week: nine changes merged between 9 and 14 November, and 19 articles across five sections.[3]
  • The fact-check gate paid off. It caught an opening date that had moved to February 2027, a quote credited to the wrong outlet, and a temperature record two outlets disagreed on, which was left out.[3]
  • Transparency as a feature. Articles carry their sources, a fact-check date and the approving editor, and the newsroom page explains exactly how the site works.[1][2]
04 · Lessons learned

Agents scale the writing. Trust is still human.

  • Many personas, one model. Twelve agents played by the same AI are useful points of view, not twelve independent opinions. When they “vote”, a human makes the call.[2]
  • The human is the bottleneck, by design. Every item blocked in week one was waiting on the editor: approvals, a corrections form and a privacy note.[3]
  • Trust features before growth. The corrections form waits for anti-spam protection, a privacy note comes before traffic grows, and the newsletter waits until emails can be looked after properly.[3][1]
  • Don’t print what changes daily. Hawker prices change too often to publish, so the food guide leaves them out.[3]
